Abstract 

Large language model (LLM) agents can self-evolve at test time by accumulating experience: retrieving relevant memo-ries before acting and writing new lessons back afterwards. Yet most are single-level : trajectories, distilled workflows and reasoning items all land in one undifferentiated pool indexed by embedding similarity, with no typed abstraction hierarchy. They never forget or deduplicate, and are prone to negative transfer , where a superficially similar but directionally oppo-site experience is recalled and its concrete steps are copied verbatim, actively harming the agent. We introduce STRATA, a structured, outcome-grounded, self-maintaining memory that organizes experience into a three-layer graph of atomic skills and code snippets, task workflows and generalized meta-frameworks, connected by typed edges. STRATA maintains it-self through five mechanisms sharing one similarity geometry: a multi-dimensional similarity that scores retrieval by opera-tion and target; a typed reflector crystallizing procedures from successes and pitfalls from failures; community detection that induces meta-frameworks; pressure-triggered garbage collec-tion with articulation-point protection; and contrast edges that flag confusable, direction-opposite experiences. Across four interactive benchmarks (AppWorld, WebArena, ALFWorld, ScienceWorld), two frozen backbones and seven baselines, STRATA attains the best average score while simultaneously using the lowest latency and fewest environment steps of any memory-augmented method, and 44% fewer lines of injected memory than the most context-hungry baseline. We isolate two failure modes of prior memories: single-level memory degrades sharply as the task set grows, and single-level con-crete memories suffer catastrophic negative transfer, which contrast edges repair, lifting ScienceWorld from 78 .7 to 96 .7

within the same system.

Despite rapid progress, prevailing agent memories share three limitations. 

(1) They lack a typed abstraction hierarchy. Several do organize what they store (AWM distils workflows from tra-jectories (Wang et al. 2024b), A-Mem links notes into a net-work (Xu et al. 2026)), but within one undifferentiated level: nothing separates a one-off tool call from a reusable pro-cedure from a cross-task principle, and no operation folds procedures into a principle. Retrieval stays a single simi-larity search dominated by surface overlap, merging same-material, different-operation pairs like “create a playlist” and “add months to a note”.

(3) They are vulnerable to negative transfer. When two experiences are superficially similar but directionally oppo-site, as in “heat the substance” versus “cool the substance”, similarity recall surfaces the wrong one, and if the memory stores concrete executable steps the agent copies them and acts catastrophically. This is not hypothetical: on Science-World a production agent’s native flat memory scores −100 

on a task family that a memoryless agent solves outright. We address these limitations with STRATA, a memory that is structured, outcome-grounded and self-maintaining. STRATA organizes experience into a three-layer directed graph: atomic nodes (reusable skills, code snippets, or spe-cialized sub-roles), workflow nodes (generalized task proce-dures), and meta-framework nodes (task families abstracted from recurring workflows), linked by typed call, general-ization, merge and contrast edges. The graph is grown and pruned by five mechanisms that all operate in one similarity geometry: (i) a multi-dimensional similarity combining con-tent and goal embeddings with an explicit operation/target tag term; (ii) a typed reflector that crystallizes a reusable proce-dure only from solved episodes and attaches a pitfall bullet on failure, with a per-group adaptive threshold deciding whether to create, extend, or skip a workflow; (iii) community detec-tion that induces a meta-framework whenever a community is large, tight, and novel; (iv) pressure-triggered garbage col-lection that ranks workflows by a competition-aware quality score and forgets the lowest while protecting articulation points of the similarity graph; and (v) contrast edges that connect confusable, direction-opposite experiences so that retrieving one injects an explicit “do not confuse” warning about the other. Our contributions are as follows. 

• We propose STRATA, a three-layer structured experi-ence graph for self-evolving LLM agents, together with five maintenance mechanisms that grow, generalize, and forget it, formulated precisely and reproducibly. 

• We evaluate STRATA on four interactive benchmarks across two frozen backbones, seven baselines and three seeds, with a component-wise ablation of every mecha-nism. STRATA obtains the best average score at the low-est latency and fewest environment steps of any memory baseline. 

• We identify two failure modes of prior memories: a scale failure of single-level memory that neither abstracts nor forgets (on Flash the strongest baseline ranks first at 168 

tasks and seventh at 812 ), and negative transfer in single-level concrete memories, which contrast edges repair, ex-posing a structure-versus-abstraction trade-off. 

Related Work 

Memory for LLM agents. A growing line of work equips LLM agents with an external memory that persists across episodes. Early systems store and summarize raw interac-tion history (Packer et al. 2023; Chhikara et al. 2025; Zhong et al. 2024); Agent Workflow Memory extracts reusable ac-tion workflows from past trajectories (Wang et al. 2024b); Agentic Context Engineering (ACE) maintains an evolving, per-application playbook of bullet-point strategies (Zhang et al. 2025b); ReasoningBank distills generalizable rea-soning strategies from both successful and failed trajecto-ries (Ouyang et al. 2025). A-Mem organizes memories in a Zettelkasten-style linked note network (Xu et al. 2026), MemGen weaves a generative latent memory into the agent’s own reasoning (Zhang, Fu, and Yan 2025), MemEvolve meta-evolves the memory architecture itself (Zhang et al. 2025a), and MemInsight autonomously augments stored items (Salama et al. 2025). These methods differ in what they store but share a common structure: a single-level col-lection retrieved by similarity, with no typed hierarchy and, typically, no explicit forgetting or anti-confusion mechanism. A-Mem is the most structured of them and, with MemEvolve, our strongest baseline: it links notes into a network and lets new memories revise old ones, but the network is single-level, one note per memory linked to its neighbours, so it neither abstracts a family of notes into a reusable princi-ple nor removes any note once written. We show that this produces a sharp scale dependence. STRATA departs from this line by making the memory a typed, layered graph that is actively maintained, consolidated into meta-frameworks, garbage-collected under pressure and wired with contrast edges against negative transfer. 

Self-evolving and experience-based agents. Reflexion con-verts episodic failures into verbal self-feedback (Shinn et al. 2023) and Self-Refine iterates on its own output within an episode (Madaan et al. 2023); Voyager accumulates a skill library of executable code (Wang et al. 2023); ExpeL gathers cross-task insights for in-context reuse (Zhao et al. 2024); Synapse retrieves trajectory exemplars (Zheng et al. 2024); CoALA frames such designs as cognitive architec-tures (Sumers et al. 2023), alongside simulacra systems that persist state across episodes (Park et al. 2023). STRATA is complementary and mechanism-level: rather than a new learning signal, it contributes the data structure and main-tenance policy governing how experiences are typed, con-nected, generalized and forgotten, so accumulated experience stays organized as it scales. 

Structured retrieval, forgetting, and negative transfer. 

Outside the agent setting, graph-structured and hierarchi-cal memories (Edge et al. 2024; Gutiérrez et al. 2024, 2025) improve retrieval precision over flat retrieve-then-generate (Lewis et al. 2020; Karpukhin et al. 2020; Izac-ard et al. 2021) and its refinements (Gao et al. 2023; Asai et al. 2024), while unstructured stores suffer the classical catastrophic-forgetting and negative-transfer problems (Mc-Closkey and Cohen 1989; Kirkpatrick et al. 2017; Parisi et al. 2018; Zhang et al. 2022). Our maintenance machinery borrows standard graph tools: Louvain community detec-tion (Blondel et al. 2008; Newman and Girvan 2004), con-ductance as a cut quality measure (Shi 2000), and Tarjan’s articulation-point algorithm (Tarjan 1972); the three-layer split mirrors the episodic/semantic distinction and comple-mentary learning systems (Tulving 1972; McClelland, Mc-Naughton, and O’Reilly 1995). In the agent setting the risk is sharper: because retrieved experience is executed , an in-correctly recalled procedure does not merely mislead but actively harms. STRATA adds an explicit operation/target gate and surfaces the opposing experience as a retrieval-time warning, a mechanism we are not aware of in prior agent memory. 

Method 

The Three-Layer Experience Graph 

STRATA maintains a graph G = ( V, E ) whose nodes carry a type τ (n) ∈ { atomic , workflow , meta }: atomic nodes are the smallest reusable units (one tool/API, a parameterized code snippet, or a specialized sub-role); workflow nodes are generalized procedures for a whole task; meta nodes are paradigms abstracted from a family of similar workflows. Typed Reflection with Adaptive Thresholds 

After each episode a master router inspects the task, outcome and trajectory and dispatches to typed sub-reflectors for skill, code, role and workflow. Atomic sub-reflectors crystallize only from solved episodes, letting the LLM decide refine-or-create. The workflow sub-reflector is threshold-driven, and the thresholds must adapt: equivalent task pairs score ≈ 0.4–

0.5 in one application and ≈ 0.8 in another, so one global band either over-merges or over-fragments. We therefore group nodes by their leading tag and shrink a per-group quantile towards a global prior in empirical-Bayes fashion (Efron and Morris 1973):

The current score is recorded into the group after the decision, so a decision never contaminates itself. Crucially, failed episodes never crystal-lize a “do this” procedure; at most they attach a pitfall bullet to the nearest workflow, rendered to the agent as something to avoid.

Inducing Meta-Frameworks by Community Detection 

Meta nodes are triggered not by counting children but by 

community structure . We build W by joining a workflow pair when snn exceeds a per-group adaptive threshold (shrunk as above, κE =6 , intra-group default 0.40 ) or a fixed cross-group threshold 0.55 , then keeping each node’s 6 strongest edges (an edge survives if either endpoint ranks it that high). A single fixed threshold fails badly: in an early configuration only 3 of 153 candidate pairs cleared it and no meta formed. Louvain (Blondel et al. 2008) at resolution 1.5 yields commu-nities (the Leiden refinement (Traag, Waltman, and van Eck 2019) would also serve), and a community is promoted only if it is large (|C| ≥ Nmin ), tight (conductance ϕ(C) < 0.6:dense inside, sparse outside (Shi 2000)), and novel . Letting 

c(C) be its centroid and d∗ its distance to the nearest existing meta, we create if d∗ ≥ Rmeta =0 .6 and otherwise update the matched meta by absorbing its unlinked members. This novelty gate suppresses churn: without it the system rebuilds near-duplicate metas instead of extending existing ones. 

Contrast Edges against Negative Transfer 

The content dimension rates same object, opposite direc-tion experiences as highly similar, such as “heat tin until it melts” versus “cool tin until it solidifies”. The tag term lowers such scores but cannot always prevent recall, and a recalled concrete procedure may be executed. A contrast edge is a bidirectional evolution edge linking two such nodes, 

contrast( a, b ) ⇐⇒ op( a) opposes op( b), (5) instantiated by rule over a fixed lexicon of opposite operator pairs, {heat , cool } and {create , delete }. We do not require 

obj( a) = obj( b), because negative transfer is often cross-object, as when a melt-lead procedure misleads a freeze-tin task. A retrieved seed pulls its contrast neighbours into the subgraph, rendered with a prominent do not confuse warning rather than as a flat procedure. Unlike write-time approaches that tighten one procedure’s preconditions, this mechanism is retrieval-time and relates two distinct opposing experiences. 

The Full Loop 

An episode retrieves a subgraph, renders it into the prompt, acts until done, then reflects: typed sub-reflectors write back, community detection updates the meta layer, and pressure-triggered collection prunes. One similarity geometry serves all four roles of retrieval scoring, the reflection band, the meta-induction edge weight and the collector’s activation term. 

Experimental Setup 

Benchmarks. Interactive agent evaluation now spans many suites (Liu et al. 2024; Deng et al. 2023; Yao et al. 2022); we select four that are long-horizon and cover distinct action spaces, namely API orchestration, web navigation, embodied household tasks and scientific reasoning: App-World (Trivedi et al. 2024), reporting Task Goal Com-pletion (TGC) and Scenario Goal Completion (SGC) on 

Test-Normal (168 tasks) and Test-Challenge (417 tasks); 

WebArena (Zhou et al. 2024) ( 812 tasks, success rate); ALFWorld (Shridhar et al. 2020) ( 134 tasks); and Science-World (Wang et al. 2022) ( 23 tasks, average score). 

Backbones and baselines. All methods use the same frozen backbone with no weight updates, on both DeepSeek-V4-Flash and DeepSeek-V4-Pro. We compare against seven baselines: No-Memory (a ReAct agent), Flat-Memory (an unstructured list of episode summaries retrieved by embed-ding similarity), ReasoningBank (Ouyang et al. 2025), Her-mes 1 (an open-source agent framework with native persis-tent memory), A-Mem (Xu et al. 2026), MemEvolve (Zhang et al. 2025a) and MemGen (Zhang, Fu, and Yan 2025). 

Protocol. Memory accumulates online across a single se-quential pass over each benchmark, so later tasks benefit from earlier ones, the setting in which maintenance matters. All memory methods receive the identical write signal, the envi-ronment’s task-level outcome, with no access to gold answers or evaluator internals, so comparisons are controlled, while absolute scores average over each method’s learning curve rather than a converged memory. Embeddings come from a local sentence-embedding model of the Sentence-BERT family (Reimers and Gurevych 2019), and the workflow cap is set per dataset. Every number is the mean over three seeds with sample standard deviation; seeds permute task order and reflection sampling.

Where Single-Level Memory Loses Ground 

A-Mem is among the strongest baselines (within 0.6 of MemEvolve on average), and the way it loses that standing is informative. It differs from the others by being explicitly or-ganized , with notes linked into a network and existing notes revised as new ones arrive (Xu et al. 2026), but the organi-zation is single-level: there is one note per memory, and no operation ever replaces a family of notes with a single more general one, nor removes a note. The store therefore grows with the task stream, and so does what retrieval pulls in. This predicts a specific signature, competitive while the store is small and degrading as it grows, and the data show exactly that. Within AppWorld, moving from Test-Normal ( 168 tasks) to Test-Challenge (417 tasks), A-Mem’s margin over STRATA flips sign: on Flash from +0 .4/ + 3 .4 (TGC/SGC) to −4.3/ − 6.3, on Pro from −1.2/ + 1 .8 to −2.2/ − 4.3.We state the confound plainly: Test-Challenge is not merely larger but intrinsically harder and draws on a different appli-cation mix ( amazon supplies 212 of its tasks and is absent from Test-Normal), so the comparison entangles scale with difficulty and distribution shift. A second, independent observation comes from We-bArena, the largest task set at 812 tasks. There A-Mem scores 

28 .4 on Flash, seventh of eight methods, below Flat-Memory (30 .7), Hermes ( 31 .6) and MemGen ( 29 .5), and above only the memoryless agent: on Flash a method that ranks first at 

168 tasks ranks seventh at 812 . On Pro the drop is milder but in the same direction, fourth at 38 .7, behind MemEvolve (41 .0) and ReasoningBank ( 40 .3). The cost is directly mea-surable in Table 3: A-Mem injects 41 .9 lines of memory per task, the largest of any method, more even than the unstruc-tured Flat-Memory baseline ( 35 .6), while STRATA scores higher using 23 .3. Injecting more is not injecting better. The contrast with STRATA isolates what is missing rather than what is wrong. A-Mem links memories; STRATA ad-ditionally abstracts them ( 812 WebArena tasks condense to 

112 workflows under 16 meta nodes) and forgets the ones that stop earning their place. Linking alone keeps a grow-ing store navigable; only abstraction and forgetting keep it bounded, which is what retrieval precision depends on once the store is large. Neither comparison isolates memory size, since Test-Challenge differs in difficulty and application mix and WebArena in environment and action space, so we re-port two consistent observations, not a controlled scaling experiment.

Negative Transfer: A Case Study 

The clearest instance is ScienceWorld’s find-living 

family, where Hermes scores −100 (Flash) and −80 (Pro) while a memoryless agent solves the task. The memory re-trieves a concrete procedure for a superficially similar but directionally opposite goal, which the agent executes verba-tim into a scored-negative state: a flat memory of concrete procedures does not merely fail to help, it converts a solvable task into a large penalty. Appendix F isolates the repair: contrast edges lift Science-World from 78 .7 to 89 .8 with hand-placed edges and to 96 .7

by rule over the operator lexicon, and ALFWorld from 89 .5

to 98 .6. Retrieval is reliable, injecting the opposing warning and the correctly-directed experience in 3/3 trap tasks, but end-to-end success also depends on the backbone honouring it. This exposes a structure vs. abstraction trade-off. Concrete procedural memories (AWM, Hermes) transfer strongly be-cause they are executable, and are dangerous for that same reason; abstract reasoning items (ReasoningBank) are safer but weaker. Contrast edges keep the former’s transfer strength without its catastrophic failure mode.

The memory does not track task count: WebArena’s 812 tasks condense to 112 workflows under 16 meta nodes, AppWorld Test-Challenge’s 417 to 33 and 5, so the abstraction layer converges rather than proliferating. Efficiency 

A structured memory might be expected to cost more; Ta-ble 3 shows the opposite. Among the seven memory sys-tems STRATA has the lowest latency ( 58 .7s vs. 60 .2–72 .4), the fewest output tokens ( 5,217 ) and the fewest environment steps ( 11 .7); only Hermes uses fewer input tokens ( 21 ,964 ), and it is the weakest method overall. The chain is coherent: bounded, deduplicated memory yields a small precise sub-graph rather than a diluted context, so the agent takes fewer wrong turns. Conclusions and Future Work 

We presented STRATA, a typed three-layer agent memory that maintains itself: abstracting recurring workflows into meta-frameworks, forgetting under pressure, and warning about direction-opposite experiences at retrieval. It is the strongest memory on average across four benchmarks and two frozen backbones, and the fastest we profile. Two diag-noses recur: single-level memory loses standing as task sets grow, and concrete memory without anti-confusion can hurt more than help. Several directions remain open. Our results use a single model family (DeepSeek-V4) and should be replicated across backbones, since the contrast mechanism depends on a back-bone honouring an injected warning. Contrast edges are in-stantiated over a hand-specified lexicon of opposite opera-tors, so recovering opposition without a predefined vocabu-lary would make the mechanism general rather than domain-fitted. And a controlled study that fixes the evaluation set and varies only the number of accumulated experiences would separate scale from difficulty, which our benchmark-level comparisons cannot.

A. Final Memory Graph Statistics 

Table 4 reports the composition of the memory graph after a full sequential pass over each benchmark. The central observation is that memory size is decoupled from task count : WebArena’s 812 tasks condense into 112 workflows and 16 meta nodes, and AppWorld Test-Challenge’s 417 tasks into 33 workflows and 5 metas. The abstraction layer therefore converges instead of proliferating, which is the property that makes retrieval cost stay flat as experience accumulates. Three secondary readings are worth noting. C. Adaptive Reflection Thresholds 

Table 6 evaluates the per-group empirical-Bayes thresholds. The final column, the number of workflow nodes surviving the run, is what makes this table informative: it must be read together with accuracy, because neither more nor fewer memories is intrinsically better, the goal being the right number. A single fixed global band fails in two directions at once. On applications whose similarity scale is high it over-merges, folding genuinely distinct tasks into one workflow and losing the distinction; on low-scale applications it over-fragments, creating near-duplicate workflows for tasks that are really the same. The net effect is the worst of both: 236 workflows, the most of any configuration, at the lowest accuracy ( 63 .9). Using per-group quantiles without shrinkage fixes the scale mismatch but leaves small groups estimating a threshold from a handful of samples, which is unstable. Full shrinkage produces 201 workflows at 

72 .9: fewer memories, higher accuracy. This sweep refines the single w/o adaptive threshold row of Table 2, which disables the mechanism as one block ( 67 .0 average). Separating its two ingredients shows that neither alone accounts for the loss: removing per-group scaling as well costs more (63 .9), keeping per-group quantiles but dropping shrinkage costs less ( 69 .1), and the Table 2 figure falls between them. Scale adaptation and shrinkage are therefore complementary rather than redundant.

F. Contrast-Edge Ablation 

Table 9 decomposes the contrast mechanism into the value of the edges and the value of instantiating them by rule . Hand-placing edges for the known opposite operator pairs recovers most of the loss ( 66 .4 → 70 .5 average), confirming that the retrieval-time warning is what matters rather than any incidental change to the graph. Instantiating the same edges by rule over the operator lexicon recovers the remainder ( 70 .5 → 72 .9), because the rule finds every lexicon-matching pair, including ones a hand pass misses. The rule does not discover opposition: the operator pairs are supplied. The gains are concentrated exactly where the precondition holds. ALFWorld gains 9.1 points and ScienceWorld 18 .0 between the first and last rows, while WebArena gains 1.8. Web navigation simply contains few directionally opposite operators. I. Example Memory Nodes 

Figures 8–10 reproduce two real nodes from the AppWorld Test-Challenge graph, verbatim in STRATA’s typed-markdown serialization. Together they illustrate one workflow → meta relation: the meta node in Figure 10 is one of the 5 metas counted for AppWorld Test-Challenge in Table 4, and the workflow node in Figure 8 is one of the 12 workflows it generalizes. The workflow node shows what outcome-grounded accumulation produces in practice. Figure 8 is the node as first crystallized: the structured app: /obj: /op: tags that drive the tag term, an applicability string that forms part of the index view, and one generalized procedure. Figure 9 is everything the reflector added afterwards, namely 23 bullets, of which 7 are pitfall bullets distilled from failed episodes and rendered to the agent as things to avoid, and 16 are variant bullets from solved near-neighbour episodes whose similarity fell inside the [τlow , τ high ] band. Crucially the procedure itself was never rewritten, since no failed episode ever crystallized a “do this” procedure, and no near-duplicate ever created a competing node. The meta node shows what community detection abstracts. Each member contributes one variant bullet keyed by its applicability condition, so retrieving the meta gives the agent a routing table over the app:gmail family rather than a single procedure. It reached support 12 through repeated update rather than repeated create, which is the novelty gate of Table 8 operating on a real graph. One implementation detail is visible here and worth stating plainly. The tag list is not the union over all 12 children: an update recomputes it from the members of the community detected in that round, so tags contributed by children attached in earlier rounds can drop out. That is why op:delete , carried by the workflow of Figure 8, does not appear on the meta. Bullets accumulate monotonically while tags do not.
