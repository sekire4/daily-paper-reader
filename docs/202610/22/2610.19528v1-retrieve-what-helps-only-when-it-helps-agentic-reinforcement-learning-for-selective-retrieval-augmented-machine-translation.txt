# Retrieve What Helps, Only When It Helps: Agentic Reinforcement Learning for Selective Retrieval-Augmented Machine Translation 

Shijun Li 1*, Yang Liu 2, Najmeh Sadoughi 2, Dongmei Wang 2,Xinjie Li 2, Zhu Liu 2, Rohith Mysore Vijaya Kumar 2, Yuzong Liu 21The University of Texas at Austin, 2Amazon

Abstract 

Retrieving external knowledge can improve large language model (LLM) translation by pro-viding supplementary information such as id-iom interpretations and terminology. However, existing retrieval-augmented machine trans-lation systems typically apply predefined re-trieval strategies uniformly across all inputs. We argue that translation operates in a partial-evidence regime: LLM’s parametric knowledge suffices for many inputs, while external evi-dence benefits a subset and usually supports rather than directly provides the desired trans-lation. This creates two challenges: deciding 

when to retrieve and what evidence to acquire. We introduce Selective Retrieval-Augmented Translation (SRAT), an agentic reinforcement learning framework that learns when to trans-late directly or retrieve, what to retrieve, and how to integrate these decisions by optimiz-ing final translation quality. To align evidence retrieval with downstream translation quality rather than simple semantic similarity, we further develop joint Plackett–Luce optimiza-tion and translation-quality contrastive train-ing for retriever-training. Experiments on id-iom, knowledge-intensive, and document-level translation with offline or online retrieval show that SRAT consistently outperforms never- and always-retrieve baselines, and quality-aligned retriever training provides further gains when similarity-based retrieval is insufficient. The results demonstrate the benefit of treating re-trieval as a learnable translation action rather than fixed preprocessing. 

1 Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) have become strong general-purpose machine translation (MT) systems, particularly after multilingual instruction tuning and translation-specific adaptation (Jiao et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2024). In particular, re-cent LLMs demonstrate strong ability to inter-

These capabilities have motivated retrieval-augmented MT systems that provide bilingual ex-amples, idiom definitions, terminology, or support-ing documents to further support and improve trans-lation quality (Li et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025b). However, recent studies (Sun et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2023) have shown that retrieval is not uni-formly beneficial for translation. Existing retrieval-augmented MT pipelines typically apply a prede-fined retrieval strategy: retrieve for every input with a heuristic, similarity-based matching rule. This overlooks a fundamental gap in LLM-based translation: for some inputs, the model’s paramet-ric knowledge is already sufficient, while for oth-ers, external evidence such as idiom interpretations, canonical entities, technical terms, or background knowledge can be crucial. Moreover, heuristic, similarity-based retrieval can provide no help and even introduce noisy or misleading context that distracts the model and degrades an otherwise cor-rect translation (Sun et al., 2026). For example, a semantically close document may merely repeat query terms without providing actionable knowl-edge, while a more distant one may contain ex-actly the idiom interpretation, entity convention, or background knowledge the translation requires. Translation therefore differs from answer-oriented 1arXiv:2610.19528v1 [cs.CL] 14 Oct 2026 retrieval settings, in which a relevant document often directly contains the evidence needed to pro-duce the final response. We refer to this setting as the partial-evidence regime, which captures two properties of retrieval in MT. First, external evidence is beneficial for only a subset of inputs where the model’s para-metric knowledge doesn’t suffices. Second, when evidence is needed, retrieval based solely on seman-tic similarity may fail to surface the most useful evidence. This raises two learning problems. The first is retrieval adaptivity : the model must deter-mine when additional evidence is useful instead of retrieving by default. The second is retrieval qual-ity alignment : once retrieval is invoked, the system must determine what evidence actually improves the final translation quality. To address them, we propose Selective Retrieval-Augmented Translation (SRAT), an agentic reinforcement-learning framework that treats rea-soning, retrieval, and translation as actions in a unified policy. Given a source input, the model’s action space at each step includes reasoning about the translation, generating a retrieval query and in-corporating the returned evidence, or terminating with the final translation. Rather than supervis-ing which examples should retrieve, we optimize complete trajectories using final translation qual-ity. Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) (Shao et al., 2024) allows trajectories with differ-ent retrieval decisions and queries to compete for the same input, thereby learning when retrieval is worthwhile and what to retrieve to improve final translation quality. Moreover, we develop two approaches for fur-ther aligning the retriever with downstream trans-lation quality rather than simple semantic similar-ity. First, a natural and principled approach is to jointly optimize the LLM translator and retriever end-to-end. To achieve it, we replace determinis-tic top-k selection during training with a Plackett– Luce ranking policy (Plackett, 1975), whose selec-tion log-probability allows trajectory-level transla-tion reward to update the retriever through a score-function gradient. However, this joint signal can be noisy: a trajectory contains many LLM policy decisions but only a few retrieval actions, mak-ing credit assignment to the retriever inherently high-variance. To address this, we further intro-duce translation-quality contrastive training, which fixes the translator, evaluates candidate retrieval evidence by their measured effect on translation quality, and converts these into direct contrastive supervision. This isolates the effect of retrieval from other policy decisions, providing the retriever with substantially more targeted training signals. We evaluate SRAT across three translation settings: idiom translation, knowledge-intensive translation, and document-level translation. The first two use offline retrieval corpora, while the document-level setting additionally evaluates SRAT with online retrieval. Across these settings, SRAT consistently outperforms never-retrieve and always-retrieve baselines, demonstrating the bene-fit of learning when to retrieve and what evidence to seek. Ablations show that quality-aligned re-triever training provides further significant gains when similarity-based retrieval is insufficient. We also find that the retrieval policy can be optimized with a frontier black-box translator, outperforming its direct translation and indicating that the adap-tive retrieval decision training can be decoupled from the translator and optimized as a modular component for external generation models. Our main contributions are threefold: • Selective retrieval as a translation decision. 

We characterize LLM-based RAG translation as a partial-evidence regime in which external knowl-edge is useful only conditionally. We formulate machine translation by LLM as a sequential pol-icy over reasoning, retrieval, and final translation, enabling the model to learn when and what to retrieve from translation outcomes rather than fixed routing rules. • Quality-aligned evidence retrieval. We pro-pose two retriever-training strategies. Joint Plackett–Luce policy-gradient training propa-gates trajectory-level translation reward to ev-idence retrieval, while translation-quality con-trastive training derives direct retriever supervi-sion from controlled retrieval evidence interven-tions. Both align the retriever toward the down-stream effect of retrieved evidence on translation quality rather than semantic similarity alone. • Evaluation across retrieval regimes. Ex-periments on idiom, knowledge-intensive, and document-level translation with offline and on-line retrieval consistently outperform fixed never-and always-retrieve strategies. The results fur-ther show that quality-aligned retrieval is signifi-cantly beneficial when similarity-based retrieval is insufficient, and that adaptive retrieval policy 2learning can be decoupled from the translator to improve frontier black-box models beyond their direct translation performance. 

2 Related Work 

Large Language Models for Translation. Early studies showed that general-purpose LLMs can deliver competitive translation performance, al-though results vary across languages, domains, and prompting strategies (Jiao et al., 2023; Hendy et al., 2023). Translation-specific continued pre-training and supervised fine-tuning further improve smaller open models; ALMA, for example, com-bines monolingual adaptation with high-quality parallel data (Xu et al., 2024). These efforts im-prove the underlying translator, but largely leave inference-time information acquisition outside the modeling problem. A parallel line of work intro-duces explicit deliberation into LLM-based MT. MAPS elicits multiple translation-relevant analy-ses before selecting among candidate outputs (He et al., 2024); TEaR adopts a translate–estimate– refine procedure (Feng et al., 2025); and DRT dis-tills extended reasoning traces for figurative text (Wang et al., 2025a). These studies suggest that additional computation can help difficult inputs, but the form and amount are typically fixed in ad-vance. SRAT instead learns when extra reasoning and retrieval really help for translation. 

Retrieval-Augmented Machine Translation. Re-trieval has long been used in MT through trans-lation memories and nearest-neighbor examples, while recent LLM-based systems draw on broader forms of external evidence. IdiomKB retrieves ex-planatory knowledge for idiomatic translation (Li et al., 2024), RAGtrans incorporates multilingual unstructured documents (Wang et al., 2025b), and cross-cultural MT has explored multilingual knowl-edge graphs (Conia et al., 2024). These results demonstrate the value of external knowledge when the source alone is insufficient. Yet most systems either retrieve by default or rely on predefined rout-ing, while retrieval is often optimized separately from the final translation objective. Our focus is in-stead on whether retrieved evidence improves trans-lation quality for a given input and model state. 

Adaptive Retrieval and Reinforcement Learning. 

Adaptive retrieval has been studied mainly in lan-guage modeling and question answering. FLARE triggers retrieval when generation becomes uncer-tain (Jiang et al., 2023); Self-RAG learns retrieval and reflection actions from supervised preference data (Asai et al., 2024); and Search-R1 uses rein-forcement learning to acquire multi-turn search-and-reason behavior from answer-level rewards (Jin et al., 2025b). These works show that retrieval can be integrated into a learned generation policy. Translation, however, differs in that a plausible out-put can often be produced without retrieval, while useful evidence may guide the rendering without explicitly containing the target translation. This makes selective retrieval and alignment with trans-lation quality especially important. 

3 Method 

Our method addresses two decisions induced by the partial-evidence regime: when external evidence should be acquired and what evidence should be re-turned when retrieval is invoked. We first formulate selective retrieval as part of the translation policy, and then introduce two strategies for aligning the retriever with downstream translation quality. 

3.1 Selective Retrieval as a Translation Policy 

Let x denote a source sentence, P (ℓs, ℓ t) the trans-lation instruction prompt given source and target languages ℓs and ℓt, and y⋆ a reference avail-able only during training. An external corpus 

D = {dj }Nj=1 contains potentially useful evidence such as idiom explanations, terminology and entity, background documents, or online information. We model the LLM translator as a policy πθ with initial state s0 = [ P (ℓs, ℓ t), x ]. Starting from s0,the policy generates a trajectory τ = ( a1, . . . , a T )

that may interleave reasoning, retrieval, and the final translation ˆy. Making information acquisition part of the trajectory allows retrieval to be decided dynamically rather than applied as a fixed prepro-cessing step. Specifically, the trajectory contains three types of action during the workflow: • Reasoning ( <think> ). The model may analyze ambiguity, figurative meaning, cultural context, entities, or terminology, or assess whether its current knowledge is sufficient for translation. • Retrieval ( <tool_call> ). When the current evidence is insufficient, the model generates a natural-language query qt and invokes the re-triever rϕ over D:

Dt ∼ rϕ(· | qt, D). (1) The returned document set is appended to the interaction state, st+1 = st ∥ [qt, D t], after which 3the model can reason the evidence, regenerate a query, or directly translate. The query can di-rectly target the missing information, for exam-ple, the meaning of an ambiguous expression in context or relevant background knowledge needed for translation. • Translation ( <answer> ). Once the model judges the available evidence sufficient, it emits final translation ˆy and terminates. This action can occur immediately after the initial state, so direct translation is a valid zero-retrieval trajectory. The policy therefore jointly learns whether to retrieve, what information to request, and how to use all evidence for translation. The objective is not to apply a fixed retrieval policy, but to retrieve only when it improves the final translation.

Because tra-jectories for the same input can differ in whether they retrieve, what they query, and how they make final translation, their relative rewards directly su-pervise selective retrieval without requiring labels specifying which inputs need external knowledge. 

Retrieval exploration curriculum. Optimiz-ing the outcome reward directly from an off-the-shelf model can collapse to a never-retrieve policy: early in training, the model’s self-generated queries are often poor and its retrieval-action formatting is frequently malformed, so direct translation consis-tently receives higher reward before useful retrieval behavior has a chance to emerge. We therefore use a three-stage curriculum. First, we explicitly gen-erate retrieval-augmented trajectories and retain the top 40% according to quality Q. Second, we perform supervised fine-tuning on these trajecto-ries to initialize the interaction format and query-generation ability. Third, we conduct RL with a temporary retrieval penalty to move the SFT pol-icy away from always retrieving, and then remove this shaping term and optimize Eq.(2) directly until convergence. The final policy is therefore free to re-trieve or translate directly according to downstream quality rather than a predefined retrieval rate. 

3.3 Learning What to Retrieve 

The policy above learns whether to retrieve and how to formulate queries, but a fixed retriever still ranks documents according to its pretraining ob-jective, typically the shallow semantic similarity. Since our objective is to retrieve documents to help make better translation, we instead optimize docu-ment selection according to translation quality. We consider two complementary approaches.

3.4 Inference 

At inference time, no reference translation or qual-ity evaluator is required. The learned policy de-cides whether retrieval is necessary, generates a query when needed and incorporates the returned evidence, and produces the final translation. We set a maximum number of retrieval calls and an output-token budget to bound worst-case latency. We report both translation quality and retrieval rate to measure the effectiveness and policy behaviors.

4.2 RQ1: Does Selective Retrieval Improve Translation? 

Tables 1, 2, and 5 (see in Appendix A.1) show a consistent advantage of selective retrieval across idiom, knowledge-intensive, and document-level translation. SRAT 1 achieves the strongest COMET, task-specific LLM-judge, and MQM results across all settings. Compared with the strongest SFT base-lines, SRAT improves I5 by 0.096 and MQM by 1.27 points on IdiomKB Zh →En, GRF by 0.019 and MQM by 0.82 points on RAGtrans En →Zh, and GRF by 0.020 and MQM by 0.42 points on Document-MT-LLM. The gains are particularly consistent on metrics that assess semantic adequacy and multidimensional translation quality. 

Learning when to retrieve matters. The com-parison with SRAT-N and SRAT-A isolates the value of the retrieval decision. Across all three benchmarks, SRAT consistently outperforms both never- and always-retrieve policies on semantic and LLM-judged metrics. Relative to SRAT-A, MQM improves by 20.6% and 17.4% on the two IdiomKB directions, by 8.0–12.8% on RAGtrans, and by 5.4% on Document-MT-LLM. These gains are achieved with substantially different learned retrieval rates: 30.6–77.0% on IdiomKB, around half of the inputs on RAGtrans, and 88.6% on Document-MT-LLM. This variation suggests that the policy adapts retrieval behavior to different translation settings rather than learning a fixed pref-erence for retrieval. Moreover, SRAT-N already provides substantial gains over the unfine-tuned model, while SRAT further improves upon it, in-dicating complementary benefits from RL-based translation learning and selective retrieval.

This is expected because our RL reward does not include BLEU, but instead directly optimizes COMET and task-specific LLM judges, whereas supervised fine-tuning on refer-ence translations can favor surface forms closer to the reference. BLEU measures n-gram overlap with a limited set of references (Papineni et al., 2002) and may under-reward valid paraphrases or more natural translations. 4.3 RQ2: When Does Utility-Aligned Retriever Training Help? 

We next examine whether the retriever can be better aligned with translation utility. To valiate retrieval quality, we additionally calculate retrieval accuracy: for each retrieval action, we ask Claude Sonnet 4.6 to judge whether the retrieved document is cor-rect and useful for translating the source, given the query, source, reference, and retrieved document. Retriever training is most beneficial when the un-derlying retrieval task is difficult. On IdiomKB, the default similarity retriever already achieves about 95% retrieval accuracy, and joint or contrastive training improves it only to 97–98%, yielding lim-ited downstream gains. On RAGtrans, the default retriever reaches only 56.3% and 72.6% accuracy on En →Zh and En →De. Contrastive training in-creases these to 70.7% and 81.4%, while improving MQM by 6.8% and 7.4%. This shows that utility alignment is particularly valuable when semantic similarity is an insufficient proxy for evidence use-fulness. We show such cases in Appendix E.3. 4.4 RQ3: Can Selective Retrieval Improve Strong Black-Box Translators? 

We next examine whether selective retrieval can improve a stronger black-box translator whose pa-rameters are inaccessible. Specifically, we freeze Claude Sonnet 4.6 and train a Qwen-based SRAT policy to decide when and what to retrieve, us-ing Claude’s final translation quality as reward. We compare against direct Claude translation and 

Claude-Ret , which prompts Claude itself to make retrieval decisions without policy training. Table 4 shows that directly prompting a strong LLM to retrieve is not consistently beneficial. On IdiomKB Zh →En, Claude-Ret reduces COMET from 0.826 to 0.821 and MQM from −0.92 to 

−0.99 ; on RAGtrans En →De, it similarly de-grades BLEU, COMET, and MQM. In contrast, SRAT-Claude provides more reliable gains. On IdiomKB, it improves most metrics over direct Claude with only 1.9–10.2% retrieval, although the gains remain modest given Claude’s strong para-metric knowledge on the relatively simple idiom-translation tasks. The benefit becomes substantially larger on the more challenging RAGtrans benchmark. SRAT-Claude improves MQM over direct Claude by 12.4% on En →Zh and 16.8% on En →De, together with consistent gains in BLEU, COMET, and GRF. We further observe the same overall trend with GLM 5 in Appendix A.5: prompted retrieval de-grades translation, whereas a trained SRAT policy can recover the loss and improve over direct GLM translation. These results show that retrieval pol-icy learning can be decoupled from the translator and generalized across frozen black-box LLMs, improving their translation outcomes without mod-ifying their parameters.

to retrieve for LLM-based translation. Across of-fline and online retrieval settings, SRAT consis-tently outperforms fixed never- and always-retrieve strategies. A.1 Results on Document-MT-LLM 

We show the evaluation results here on Document-MT-LLM (Wang et al., 2023) , a document-level En →Ru subtitle translation task. Unlike IdiomKB and RAGtrans, this setting has no predefined re-trieval corpus. This intro-duces additional challenges from both query gener-ation and noisy, open-ended search results. Table 5 shows that selective retrieval remains effective beyond fixed offline corpora. Nevertheless, its advantage over always retrieving shows that even in this retrieval-intensive setting, external evidence is not uniformly bene-ficial. These results extend the partial-evidence observation to open-ended retrieval, where the pol-icy must learn both whether additional context is needed and how to formulate an effective search query.

A.2 Technical Details 

We conduct all experiments on a cluster where each node is equipped with 8 A100 80GB GPU with 256 GB of RAM . For all models, we use the Adam op-timizer and tune the learning rate within the range ([1e-7, 5e-7, 1e-6, 5e-6, 1e-5]). By default, we fine-tune Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct using GRPO (Shao et al., 2024). GRPO is configured with a KL coeffi-cient of 0.001 and a clip ratio of 0.2. During rollout, we use a group size of 8 and a sampling temper-ature of 1.0. We enable tool-calling via SGLang (Zheng et al., 2024) to allow the LLM to gener-ate retrieval queries and set maximal turns to 5. For retrieval, we use the Flat E5 retriever (Wang et al., 2022) following prior work (Jin et al., 2025a). Flat indexing encodes the corpus into dense embed-dings and retrieves relevant documents via efficient embedding matching in the embedding space. If retrieval fails, the retriever returns a blank place-holder. On average, training the policy to conver-gence takes 68/47, 334/382, and 97 GPU hours on the IdiomKB (Zh2En/En2Zh), RAGtrans (En2Zh), and Document-MT-LLM (En2Ru) datasets, respec-tively. For all baselines, we follow the original papers for implementation details and hyperparameter tun-ing. We run each experiment with three random seeds and report the mean performance. Statisti-cal significance is evaluated using a paired t-test with p < 0.05 . All datasets used in our experi-ments are publicly available. All datasets used in our experiments are publicly available. They are ei-ther released under the MIT license, which permits research use, or come without a specific license, but their original papers explicitly state that pub-lic research usage is permitted. Our use of these datasets complies with their original license terms and respects the rights of their creators.

B.3 Robustness to LLM Evaluators 

Our main evaluation uses Claude Sonnet 4.6. To test whether the observed gains depend on a partic-ular judge, we re-evaluate all systems using Claude Opus 4.8 and DeepSeek 3.2. The gains there-fore persist across substantially different evaluator models, indicating that they are not artifacts of op-timizing toward one particular LLM judge. 

B.4 Robustness to a Stronger Backbone 

We replace Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct with Qwen3-8B to test whether selective retrieval remains effective with a stronger backbone. We evaluate RAGtrans En →De and IdiomKB En →Zh and report results in Table 12. B.7 Human Evaluation 

Finally, to assess whether the improvements ob-served under automatic evaluation are reflected in human judgments, we conduct a blinded human evaluation on En →Zh translation. We randomly se-lect 32 test examples, with 16 each from IdiomKB and RAGtrans, and collect translations from four systems: Direct, CSC, SRAT-A, and SRAT. We recruit 20 native Chinese speakers with advanced English proficiency as annotators. They indepen-dently evaluate the translations with system identi-ties hidden and system outputs randomly assigned to columns. Following the MQM criteria in Ap-pendix F, annotators assign error penalties across five dimensions: Accuracy, Fluency, Style, Termi-nology, and Non-translation. Scores are reported as negative penalties, with values closer to zero indicating better quality. Table 14 shows that human judgments sup-port the overall ranking observed under automatic evaluation: SRAT ( −1.94 ) outperforms SRAT-A (−2.77 ), followed by CSC ( −3.56 ) and Direct (−6.32 ). Compared with Direct, SRAT reduces accuracy and terminology penalties by 53.2% and 85.1%, respectively, while also improving fluency and style. Relative to SRAT-A, SRAT achieves better scores across all four dimensions, with the largest absolute improvement in accuracy, from 

−1.15 to −0.81 . Non-translation penalties are neg-ligible across systems. These results provide ad-ditional evidence that selective retrieval improves translation quality beyond an always-retrieve strat-egy, with benefits extending beyond terminology to overall accuracy and expression. Last, we provide more details on how the human evaluation was conducted. All 20 annotators were native Chinese speakers enrolled in master’s or PhD programs at a U.S. university, with advanced English proficiency. They were recruited through a shared link to an online annotation form. Par-ticipation was entirely voluntary and unpaid, and annotators provided informed consent through the form both to participate and to have their annota-tions collected and used for the research reported in this paper. Annotators received the same MQM evaluation instructions reproduced in Appendix F. The annotation protocol was determined exempt from further ethics review by the relevant institu-tional ethics review board. 

C Limitations 

Our evaluation covers several translation settings and language directions, while extending SRAT to a broader range of languages and specialized domains would further establish its applicability. 15 Our human evaluation provides complementary ev-idence to automatic metrics, but remains relatively limited in scale. In addition, retriever alignment is learned for a particular translator, making the transfer of retrieval policies and evidence prefer-ences across models a valuable direction. Finally, exploring SRAT on tasks that require combining evidence from more sources could further reveal the potential of sequential retrieval for translation on more complex and general scenarios. 

D Ethical Considerations 

SRAT aims to improve translation quality, but re-trieved sources may contain inaccurate or biased information that influences the resulting transla-tions. Careful source selection and human review remain important, particularly for sensitive or high-stakes content. When using online retrieval, queries may expose information from the source text to external services, motivating privacy-aware deploy-ment for confidential material. Translation quality may also vary across languages and communities, warranting broader evaluation before deployment. Finally, policy training and external model evalu-ations consume computational resources; reusing trained components and exploring more econom-ical adaptation strategies could help reduce their environmental impact.
