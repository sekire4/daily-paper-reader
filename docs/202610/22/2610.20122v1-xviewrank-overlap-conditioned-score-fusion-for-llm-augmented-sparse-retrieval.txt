# XViewRank: Overlap-Conditioned Score Fusion for LLM-Augmented Sparse Retrieval 

Xinxuan Lv, Minghan Li ⋆, and Guodong Zhou 

School of Computer Science and Technology, Soochow University, Suzhou, Jiangsu 215031, China 

xxlv@stu.suda.edu.cn, mhli@suda.edu.cn, gdzhou@suda.edu.cn 

Abstract. Large language models can reduce vocabulary mismatch in sparse retrieval by generating alternative views of a query. Once these views are searched independently, however, their ranked lists may be complementary, redundant, or affected by query drift, making fixed fu-sion suboptimal. We present XViewRank, a lightweight retrieval-side method for conditionally fusing BM25 results from an original query, a hypothesis view, and a step-back view. For each pooled candidate, XViewRank retains three normalized BM25 scores and three query-level overlaps between the retrieved result sets. A small nonlinear scorer uses the overlaps as context that changes how the view-specific scores are interpreted, without jointly encoding query and passage text. Under a matched-input protocol, XViewRank improves nDCG@10 and MAP nu-merically over reciprocal rank fusion on both TREC DL 2019 and 2020, with a statistically significant MAP gain on DL19. Additional analyses show that the clearest benefit occurs when the view rankings have low agreement, while the evidence on DL20 is weaker. The results support overlap-conditioned score fusion as a compact retrieval-side alternative to fixed fusion, while also delimiting where its advantage is empirically established. 

Keywords: Information retrieval · Query expansion · Rank fusion · Learning to rank · Large language models 

## 1 Introduction 

Sparse retrieval remains attractive for large-scale search because inverted indexes are fast, transparent, and easy to deploy [24, 20]. Its weakness is also well known: short user queries often miss the vocabulary and intent cues used in relevant passages [8]. Large language models offer a practical way to reduce this mismatch without rebuilding the index: given an original query, an LLM can generate alternative query views that express the same information need from different angles. The benefit of multiple query views is not automatic. A hypothesis-style view may introduce useful topical context, while a step-back view may reveal a

broader abstraction. At the same time, generated text may drift away from the original intent or duplicate information already captured by another view. After each view is searched with BM25, the system obtains several ranked lists with different score scales and different degrees of overlap [11]. Therefore, the retrieval problem is no longer only whether the LLM can generate useful expansion text. A second problem appears after retrieval: how should evidence from several query views be fused while preserving its source and the relationships among the view rankings? Existing fusion methods address parts of this problem, but leave a gap for interpretable multi-view sparse retrieval. Direct concatenation mixes all query text into one BM25 query, so the system can no longer tell which view produced a candidate. Score-level interpolation keeps view scores separate, but typically uses fixed weights that do not adapt to how much the views overlap for each query. Unsupervised rank fusion is robust to score-scale mismatch, but it treats agreement across views through a fixed rule. Text-based neural reranking can improve final ranking, but it changes the focus from retrieval-side fusion to judg-ing relevance with query and passage text. These choices are useful baselines, but they do not jointly model BM25 evidence from each view and overlap among the view result lists. We propose XViewRank, a lightweight method for fusing BM25 results from multiple LLM-generated query views. XViewRank keeps the sparse retrieval stage intact. It searches the original query, a hypothesis view, and a step-back view separately, pools the returned documents, and learns a fusion scorer from two interpretable signals. The first signal is BM25 evidence for each candidate from each view. The second signal is how much the view result sets overlap for the current query. XViewRank therefore operates on retrieval-side evidence without requiring a text-based neural reranker. It uses the relationship among the view rankings to condition how candidate-level score evidence is interpreted. This design keeps evidence from each view visible while allowing different queries to use different fusion patterns. A candidate with similar scores from highly overlapping views should not necessarily be treated the same as a can-didate supported by one view when the views are complementary. Because the overlap values are constant across all candidates of one query, an additive linear scorer cannot use them to change the within-query order directly. XViewRank therefore uses a nonlinear scorer, which permits overlap context to change the ef-fective contribution of each view-specific score. Query-balanced training reduces the effect of highly imbalanced sparse-retrieval candidate pools. We summarize our main contributions: 

– We formulate multi-view sparse retrieval as a matched-input fusion problem. The original, hypothesis, and step-back queries are searched independently, and every matched fusion method receives the same three ranked lists and pooled candidates. This protocol separates improvements due to fusion from improvements due to generating more or better query views. 

– We propose an overlap-conditioned nonlinear scorer that preserves three view-specific BM25 scores and uses three pairwise result-list overlaps as XViewRank 3

query-level context. The six-dimensional representation allows cross-view agreement to modify the contribution of each retrieval score without joint query and passage encoding or changes to the sparse index. 

– We show that the gain is concentrated in settings where fixed fusion has the greatest difficulty. XViewRank improves nDCG@10 and MAP numeri-cally over matched-view RRF on both TREC DL test years, with significant evidence for DL19 MAP, and its largest pooled gains occur among low-agreement queries. The smaller DL20 gains remain statistically inconclusive. 

## 2 Related Work 

2.1 LLM Query Views for Sparse Retrieval 

Query expansion has long been used to reduce vocabulary mismatch in sparse retrieval, including classical pseudo-relevance feedback, Rocchio feedback, and relevance models [25, 12, 24]. A recent survey traces how this line has evolved from statistical expansion to pretrained-model and LLM-based generation, and identifies controllability, robustness, and downstream utilization of generated content as continuing challenges [15]. Representative LLM-based methods gener-ate richer query-side text: Query2doc produces pseudo-documents for expansion [27]; HyDE-style approaches use hypothetical content to bridge the mismatch between queries and documents [9]; and hypothesis and step-back prompting expose complementary formulations of the same information need [30, 29]. Dual-layer prompt ensembles further show that combining system- and user-level in-structions can improve the robustness of LLM-based expansion and rank fusion [16]. Generated retrieval evidence is also used in iterative and domain-specific set-tings. S2G-RAG explicitly judges evidence sufficiency and remaining informa-tion gaps to control iterative retrieval for question answering [18], while GLIER couples generative legal inference with evidence ranking for legal case retrieval [14]. Related surveys emphasize that long-document retrieval introduces addi-tional challenges in representing and locating evidence across extended contexts [17]. These studies motivate careful use of generated evidence, but address it-erative QA, specialized legal retrieval, or long-document retrieval. XViewRank instead studies a complementary question in general passage retrieval: given sev-eral query views generated in advance, how should their sparse retrieval evidence be combined into one ranking? 

2.2 Rank Fusion and Cross-View Evidence 

Combining ranked lists is a classic information retrieval problem. Score fusion methods such as CombSUM combine normalized retrieval scores [7, 13], while reciprocal rank fusion is robust to score-scale mismatch [4]. Other probabilis-tic, segmentation-based, and voting-based fusion methods also aggregate ranked lists with fixed rules [19, 26, 22, 1]. Recent prompt-ensemble retrieval likewise 4 X. Lv et al. 

demonstrates that multiple generated formulations can be fused effectively [16]. However, generated views can be complementary for one query and redundant or drifting for another. XViewRank therefore learns from both view-specific score evidence and query-level view overlap, instead of applying one fixed rule to every query and view combination. 

2.3 Learning to Rank with Lightweight Signals 

Supervised ranking methods learn document order from relevance labels [2, 3, 28]. XViewRank follows this direction with retrieval-side features only: it does not encode passage text, but learns a fusion layer from signals that are cheap to compute once BM25 runs are available. Unlike general-purpose supervised fusion models over broad feature sets, XViewRank deliberately restricts the represen-tation to view-specific sparse scores and view-overlap context, so the learned behavior remains tied to the multi-view retrieval problem. 

## 3 Method 

Figure 1 gives an overview of XViewRank. The method has three stages: view-specific retrieval, feature construction, and learned fusion. 

3.1 Multi-View Candidate Generation 

For each query q, we construct three query views. The first view is the origi-nal query qori . The second view qhy is a hypothesis-style rewrite generated by an LLM. The third view qst is a step-back rewrite that expresses the informa-tion need at a more abstract level. We use these two generated views because they represent complementary reformulation directions: the hypothesis view adds concrete topical evidence that may appear in relevant passages, while the step-back view abstracts the information need to recover broader lexical matches. Let 

v ∈ { ori, hy, st } denote a view. Each view is searched independently with BM25 over the same passage index, producing a ranked list Dvq . The candidate pool is the de-duplicated union of the documents returned by the three views: 

Dq = Dori q ∪ D hy q ∪ D st q . (1) This design keeps the retrieval evidence from different views separate before fusion. A candidate may receive score evidence from all three views, only from the original query, or only from a generated view.

This representation is intentionally small and transparent. The score features in-dicate whether a candidate is strongly supported by each view, while the overlap features provide the query-level context needed to interpret that support. The overlap values are constant for all candidates of the same query, but they can still affect within-query ranking through the nonlinear scorer: the same score pattern can be mapped differently when the views are highly redundant than when they retrieve mostly complementary result sets. More formally, let s(q, d ) contain the three document-level score features and let o(q) contain the three query-level overlap features. The scorer has the conditional form 

sθ (q, d ) = fθ (s(q, d ), o(q)) . (5) Although o(q) is constant within a query, the nonlinear scorer allows it to change the local sensitivity ∂s θ (q, d )/∂ ˆsv (q, d ) to each view-specific score. Thus, overlap is not treated as direct document relevance evidence. It conditions how the model interprets candidate-level retrieval evidence. Section 4.5 verifies this behavior through linear controls, overlap perturbations, and input-gradient analysis. 

3.3 Fusion Scorer 

XViewRank uses a small multilayer perceptron to map the score-overlap feature vector to a relevance score: 

sθ (q, d ) = fθ (x(q, d )) . (6) The model has two hidden layers with 128 and 64 hidden units, ReLU activa-tions, and dropout rate 0.2. It is intentionally much smaller than a text-based reranker. The goal is not to model full semantic matching from passage text, but to combine retrieval evidence across views. 

3.4 Training Objective 

The fusion scorer is trained with weighted query-balanced binary cross-entropy. During training, Dq is the union of the top-1,000 BM25 results from the three views. A candidate is positive if it has a positive MS MARCO relevance label for the query, and unlabeled candidates in the pool are treated as negative. Let 

yq,d ∈ { 0, 1} denote this label. Since sparse retrieval candidate pools contain far more negatives than positives, we use a capped positive-class weight. Let P

and N be the total numbers of positive and negative training candidates in the training pool. The positive-class weight is γ = min( N/P, 200) . For a positive candidate, we multiply γ by 1/ log 2(rmin + 2) , where rmin is the best rank of the candidate among the three view-specific ranked lists; negative candidates receive weight 1. ## 4 Experiments 

4.1 Datasets and Evaluation 

We evaluate on the MS MARCO passage collection with TREC DL 2019 and TREC DL 2020 topics [6, 5]. Retrieval is performed with Pyserini BM25 [20]. Following our experimental protocol, BM25 uses k1 = 0 .9 and b = 0 .4. All methods return up to 1,000 documents per query. We report nDCG@10, MAP, and Recall@1000. For MAP and Recall@1000, relevance is binarized at grade 2 for TREC DL, following the strict relevance setting commonly used for binary passage-retrieval metrics. We also include a zero-shot transfer check on FiQA and NFCorpus, where the MS MARCO-trained scorer is applied without dataset-specific retraining. XViewRank is trained with MS MARCO passage training relevance labels and selected on a 2,000-query MS MARCO development sample. After removing training queries without a positive pooled candidate and excluding development-query overlap, the final training pool contains 6,317 queries and 9,040,208 query-document candidates, including 6,602 positives. Both generated views are avail-able for every retained training query. The development sample has no query overlap with the TREC DL 2019 or 2020 topics. TREC DL 2019 and 2020 are used only as held-out test sets, and their qrels are not used for model selection. Hyperparameters and model checkpoints are selected using the development set, and the same selected configuration is then applied to both test years. 

4.2 LLM Views and Implementation Details 

All LLM-generated query views and LLM expansion baselines use GPT-4o mini. For XViewRank, the hypothesis and step-back views are generated once, cached, and reused by every method requiring the same view text. The XViewRank scorer uses the score-overlap representation described in Section 3.2 and is se-lected exclusively on the 2,000-query development split; neither DL19 nor DL20 qrels are used for model or hyperparameter selection. The exact prompts, de-coding parameters, retrieval settings, training configuration, and evaluation con-ventions are reported in Appendix A. The main table reports one fixed development-selected configuration applied unchanged to both test years. 4.3 Baselines 

We compare with three groups of baselines. The first group contains sparse retrieval and classical query expansion: BM25, BM25 with RM3, BM25 with Rocchio-style feedback, and BM25 over a doc2query-T5 expanded Lucene in-dex [23]. The doc2query-T5 baseline represents document-side expansion, while XViewRank keeps the sparse index unchanged and learns to combine query-side retrieval views. The second group contains LLM expansion baselines. We include Zero-shot pseudo-doc, a pseudo-document expansion baseline inspired by Query2doc [27]; unlike the original few-shot Query2doc setting, it uses no in-context demonstrations. We also include Zero-shot CoT, a chain-of-thought-style query expansion baseline following prompt-based query expansion [10]. Finally, we compare with the original Exp4Fuse setting, which fuses the original query view with the hypothesis view. The third group contains fixed fusion baselines over the same matched view set used by XViewRank. In particular, RRF over matched views uses the original view, the hypothesis view, and the same step-back view as XViewRank; therefore its comparison isolates fixed fusion from learned fusion under identical inputs. We also include a dev-tuned weighted CombSUM baseline, where nonnegative weights for the three normalized view scores are selected on the development set and then fixed for DL19 and DL20. This setup separates two questions. The original Exp4Fuse baseline measures the established two-view LLM expansion setting. The matched-view fusion base-lines measure whether the same input views are better combined by fixed rules or by the learned XViewRank scorer.

Finally, does XViewRank improve ranking quality beyond a fixed fusion rule when the input views are held constant?

Do score and overlap signals matter? Table 4 validates the two signals used by XViewRank. The score-only variant uses only the three normalized BM25 score features. The document-features-only variant uses document-level view indicators such as rank, view presence, and view count, but removes the query-level view-overlap features. The extended-features variant combines those document-level indicators with overlap. The results show that score features alone are not sufficient, and removing overlap causes a large drop, especially on DL19. Adding broader document-level indicators also does not consistently improve over the score-overlap representation. This supports the central design choice: the model should know not only how strongly each view retrieves a candidate, but also how redundant or complementary the views are for the query. The particularly low DL20 result of the document-features-only variant de-serves clarification. Rank, presence, and view count describe where a document occurs, but they discard the magnitude of its normalized retrieval support. Their distributions are also more sensitive to changes in candidate-pool composition across test years. The result therefore does not imply that provenance indi-cators are intrinsically harmful; it shows that they are a weak substitute for view-specific score evidence and transfer less reliably on their own.

Does learned fusion improve over fixed fusion? The most direct fixed-fusion comparison is RRF over the same original, hypothesis, and step-back views. XViewRank improves over this baseline by 0.0207 nDCG@10 and 0.0212 MAP on DL19. The MAP gain reaches significance under a paired sign-flip test (p = 0 .0457 ), while the nDCG@10 sign-flip p-value remains above 0.05 despite a positive bootstrap interval. On DL20, the gains are 0.0098 nDCG@10 and 0.0081 MAP and are not significant. Recall@1000 remains nearly unchanged on both years. Therefore, the advantage of XViewRank comes mainly from reordering the shared candidate pool more effectively, rather than from retrieving many additional tail documents. Given the small number of topics and the several reported metrics, we treat the DL19 MAP result as limited evidence rather than a broad claim of statistical superiority. The scorer comparison in Table 7 provides another matched-view check. To-gether with the CombSUM and weighted CombSUM results in Table 1, it sug-gests that the gain is not merely due to pooling the three views, but to learning a nonlinear combination of score evidence under view-overlap context.

## 5 Discussion 

What is new in XViewRank? The individual ingredients of XViewRank, namely BM25 scores, Jaccard overlap, and a small MLP, are established. The contri-bution lies in their use as a matched-view conditional fusion formulation for LLM-generated sparse retrieval. Unlike concatenation, XViewRank preserves each score together with the query view that produced it. Unlike fixed RRF or CombSUM, its nonlinear scorer lets query-level agreement change how view-specific score evidence is interpreted. The matched-input protocol is essential because it separates the effect of fusion from the quality or number of generated views. 

When does overlap help? The repeated perturbation and gradient analyses in-dicate that overlap acts as coarse context rather than direct relevance evidence. Agreement-stratified and continuous analyses further show that the improve-ment is concentrated in low-agreement queries on DL19, where the views ex-pose heterogeneous candidate rankings; DL20 follows the same broad direction but does not provide significant continuous or permutation evidence. This ob-servation does not imply that arbitrary diversity is beneficial. Low agreement can arise from useful complementary formulations or from harmful query drift; XViewRank only receives retrieval-side evidence and cannot fully distinguish these causes. 

Deployment scope. The LLM generation cost is shared with the LLM-expansion baselines and can be amortized when views are cached. After the three sparse runs are available, XViewRank adds only a six-feature MLP evaluation per pooled candidate and does not require joint query and passage text encoding. Neural rerankers remain complementary when additional latency is acceptable, as illustrated in Appendix B. Accordingly, the main comparison in this work is against fixed and learned retrieval-side fusion under identical views, while the cross-encoder experiment serves only to locate XViewRank within a multi-stage pipeline. 

## 6 Limitations 

The study has several boundaries. First, DL19 and DL20 contain relatively few deeply judged topics, which limits the power of query-level significance tests. Only the DL19 MAP improvement over matched-view RRF is significant in the full-split comparison, and the DL20 gains should be interpreted as numerical rather than statistically established. The repeated permutation and continuous correlation analyses likewise support the overlap mechanism on DL19 but not on DL20. Second, the main controlled experiments use query views from one LLM configuration. Caching makes fusion comparisons fair, but does not establish robustness to a different generator or prompt distribution. Third, XViewRank requires labeled source-domain data to train the fusion scorer. The FiQA and 16 X. Lv et al. 

NFCorpus experiments show limited zero-shot transfer, but do not remove this cold-start requirement. Finally, Jaccard overlap describes set-level agreement and cannot identify whether a disagreement reflects useful complementarity or semantic drift. These limitations motivate broader cross-LLM, multi-seed, and larger-query-set evaluation, but they do not affect the matched-input compar-isons reported here. 

## 7 Conclusion 

We presented XViewRank, a lightweight conditional fusion method for LLM-augmented sparse retrieval. The method preserves normalized BM25 scores from the original, hypothesis, and step-back views, and uses result-list overlap to condition how those scores contribute to document ranking. Under matched inputs, XViewRank improves nDCG@10 and MAP numerically over fixed fusion on both TREC DL test years without jointly encoding query and passage text. The full-split statistical evidence is limited to DL19 MAP, and the smaller DL20 gains remain inconclusive. Our analyses further indicate that overlap is most useful as context when the views retrieve heterogeneous evidence, not as direct relevance evidence or as a reason to force query diversity. These results show that multi-view query expansion depends not only on generating useful views, but also on preserving and conditionally combining their retrieval evidence.
