Abstract 

Reasoning-conditioned embeddings can im-prove retrieval by progressively refining seman-tic constraints. However, additional reasoning may introduce over-generalized or semantically noisy abstractions that weaken retrieval speci-ficity. Existing methods largely supervise rea-soning through final retrieval outcomes, pro-viding limited guidance over the refinement process itself. We propose AURA, a frame-work that directly supervises intermediate rea-soning states according to how well they sepa-rate relevant documents from hard distractors. AURA further trains a lightweight utility esti-mator over prefix-conditioned embeddings to adapt reasoning depth at inference time. On the reasoning-intensive BRIGHT benchmark, AURA-4B achieves 34.7 average nDCG@10 while using 2.8 reasoning steps and 96 gen-erated tokens per query. Under matched de-ployment conditions, adaptive stopping reduces latency from 4.6 to 0.76 seconds relative to full-trajectory reasoning. Results on MS MARCO further suggest that this specialization need not substantially degrade conventional in-domain retrieval. 

1 Introduction 

Recent dense retrieval systems increasingly incor-porate reasoning through query rewriting, decom-position, reranking, and reasoning-conditioned em-beddings (Qin et al., 2025; Jiang et al., 2025; Asai et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025b; Muennighoff et al., 2024; Tang et al., 2025; Lan et al., 2025), moti-vated by the observation that semantic refinement can improve retrieval for complex queries. However, existing reasoning-based retrieval sys-tems generally treat additional reasoning as benefi-cial, applying it without distinguishing whether further refinement helps or hurts retrieval qual-ity. In practice, retrieval-oriented reasoning often exhibits diminishing returns. A key limitation of existing methods is that reasoning is typically supervised only through final retrieval performance . While effective for evaluating end-to-end success, these coarse signals provide little guidance over the reasoning process. More importantly, current retrieval systems cannot predict whether additional reasoning will improve retrieval before observing retrieval outcomes, and thus can only allocate computation reactively: they expand reasoning trajectories regardless of whether further refinement is likely to help. In this work, we formulate retrieval-oriented rea-soning as a utility-guided computation allocation problem. Rather than treating reasoning as an un-conditional token expansion, we evaluate whether each intermediate refinement improves the model’s ability to distinguish relevant documents from chal-lenging distractors (Figure 2). Under this perspec-tive, useful reasoning is determined not by trajec-tory length or textual sophistication, but by its mea-surable effect on retrieval discrimination. Building on this perspective, we propose 

AURA 1 (Adaptive Utility-aware Reasoning Re-triev Al), a framework for learning retrieval-oriented reasoning through utility supervision over intermediate reasoning states. During training, AURA uses relevance labels and retrieval candi-dates to estimate whether each refinement step im-proves retrieval discrimination, encouraging rea-soning trajectories that sharpen retrieval specificity while avoiding semantically noisy refinements. Since retrieval specificity is unavailable at infer-ence time, AURA additionally trains a lightweight utility estimator to predict the retrieval utility of each current reasoning state. Monitoring this esti-mate online allows AURA to stop when state utility ceases to improve, without exhaustively expanding the reasoning trajectory. We evaluate AURA on the 12 reasoning-intensive retrieval domains in BRIGHT (Hongjin et al., 2025) and on MS MARCO (Bajaj et al., 2016) as a conventional in-domain retrieval set-ting. AURA outperforms the compared reasoning-conditioned retrieval methods on BRIGHT. On MS MARCO, it remains comparable to a strong reasoning-trained retriever, suggesting no substan-tial in-domain degradation when extensive rea-soning is unnecessary. Controlled analyses fur-ther show that the gains cannot be explained by denser intermediate contrastive supervision or tra-jectory filtering alone, and that retrieval specificity is strongly aligned with corpus-level nDCG@10. Our contributions are summarized as follows: • We formulate reasoning-conditioned retrieval as an adaptive refinement process and show that reasoning utility is highly heterogeneous. • We propose AURA , a framework that super-vises intermediate reasoning states accord-ing to their retrieval specificity, and trains a lightweight utility estimator to dynamically allocate reasoning during inference. • We demonstrate improvements over the com-pared methods across 12 reasoning-intensive domains, together with a substantially better performance–cost trade-off under matched in-ference conditions.

2 Related Work 

2.1 Dense Retrieval and Embedding Models 

Modern dense retrieval commonly relies on bi-encoder architectures that map queries and doc-uments into a shared embedding space, where retrieval is performed through vector similar-ity (Karpukhin et al., 2020). Early embedding ap-proaches such as Sentence-BERT (Reimers and Gurevych, 2019) demonstrated the effectiveness of contrastive representation learning for semantic retrieval, followed by stronger instruction-tuned and large embedding models including INSTRUC-TOR (Su et al., 2023), E5 (Wang et al., 2022), and recent LLM-based embedding systems (Muen-nighoff et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024). Recent studies further explore using LLM hidden states directly as embeddings (Ma et al., 2024; Lee et al., 2025) or leveraging in-context learning to improve representation quality (Li et al., 2025a). Beyond supervised retrieval data, dense retrievers have also been trained using unsuper-vised contrastive objectives and synthetic supervi-sion (Lee et al., 2019; Izacard et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2024; Ji et al., 2025; Yoon et al., 2025). De-spite strong performance, most embedding models remain fundamentally single-pass encoders that construct representations without explicit semantic refinement or intermediate reasoning. 

2.2 Reasoning for Retrieval 

Recent work increasingly incorporates reasoning into retrieval systems through query rewriting, decomposition, iterative retrieval, and reasoning-augmented reranking (Qin et al., 2025; Jiang et al., 2025; Asai et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025b; Long et al., 2025). For example, ReasonIR (Shao et al., 2025) synthesizes reasoning trajectories and hard negatives to train retrieval models on reasoning-intensive benchmarks (Hongjin et al., 2025). More recently, reasoning has been incorporated directly into embedding formation: multimodal methods such as RITE (Liu et al., 2025b), LREM (Tang et al., 2025), and UME-R1 (Lan et al., 2025) gen-erate reasoning before constructing retrieval repre-sentations, with further gains from inference-time scaling through sampling multiple reasoning trajec-tories (Muennighoff et al., 2024). However, reasoning is not uniformly benefi-cial for retrieval. Query expansion has long been known to suffer from query drift (Mitra et al., 1998; Carpineto and Romano, 2012), and recent work shows that LLM-based reasoning can hurt dense retrieval due to semantic drift (Fan et al., 2026; Kotte, 2026). Similar observations have emerged for chain-of-thought prompting more broadly (Liu et al., 2025a; Hassid et al., 2025; Sui et al.). Despite these findings, existing methods either supervise reasoning only through final retrieval outcomes or select among complete trajectories via best-of-k,providing no direct supervision over intermediate refinement states nor adaptive reasoning allocation at inference time. AURA addresses both gaps. 

3 Methodology 

3.1 Unified Reasoning Embedding 

To ground reasoning-conditioned retrieval within a unified framework, AURA utilizes an autoregres-sive language model as its unified backbone fθ. To enable a single language model to simultaneously optimize autoregressive token generation and ex-tract high-quality dense retrieval representations, we propose a structured sequence format. Given a query x, the format is as follows:     

> Unified Sequence Format:
> [User]: Input: x. Generate step-by-step reasoning.
> [Assistant]: <think> r1, r 2, . . . , r T</think>

where r = ( r1, . . . , r T ) represents the generated reasoning trajectory parsed at sentence boundaries.

3.2 Inference-Time Utility Estimation 

Since retrieval specificity S(zt) depends on rele-vance labels and distractors, it is unavailable at in-ference time. To enable adaptive reasoning, AURA learns a utility estimator that predicts the retrieval specificity of the current intermediate state. For each state zt, we define its target utility as 

yt = S(zt), (7) which represents the observed state-level retrieval utility. The estimator 

Vϕ(zt) (8) is trained to preserve the ordering of state utility using a pairwise ranking loss: 

Lrank = − log σ(Vϕ(zi) − Vϕ(zj )) , yi > y j .

(9) Since adaptive stopping and state selection depend only on the relative ordering of reasoning states within a query trajectory, this objective targets or-dinal agreement rather than absolute score calibra-tion and is less sensitive to query- and negative-set-specific shifts in the scale of retrieval specificity. At inference time, reasoning terminates when the predicted state utility has not improved for p con-secutive steps, and the generated state with the highest predicted utility is used for retrieval. The utility estimator is a lightweight MLP over prefix-conditioned embeddings. 

4 Experiments 

4.1 Training Data Construction 

We build on the publicly released training data with 101K query-positive-negative samples from ReasonIR (Shao et al., 2025), which provides reasoning-intensive queries with paired positive documents and hard negatives synthesized from MS MARCO 2 seed documents. To obtain rea-soning trajectories for utility supervision, we sam-ple G=8 independent trajectories per query from DeepSeek-R1 (Guo et al., 2025), each truncated to 

> 2https://huggingface.co/datasets/microsoft/ms_ marco

a maximum of l=2 ,048 tokens. Each trajectory is segmented at sentence boundaries into intermedi-ate reasoning states. After the retrieval warm-up described below, we compute retrieval specificity 

S(zt) for every intermediate state using the labeled positive document and hard negatives provided by ReasonIR. Trajectories where no intermediate state improves over the initial query embedding are removed during label construction, retaining 67.5% of sampled trajectories (approximately 5.4 per query on average), for approximately 545K reasoning samples. 

4.2 Training Procedure 

Training separates label construction from utility-aware optimization. First, we warm up the unified model for one epoch with the multi-task objective but without utility-guided weighting. We freeze this warmed-up checkpoint to construct retrieval-specificity and state-utility labels. The utility es-timator Vϕ, a 3-layer 10M-parameter MLP with ReLU activations, is trained on these frozen em-beddings with a per-GPU batch size of 256 and a learning rate of 5 × 10 −4 for up to ten epochs, with early stopping based on loss convergence. We then enable utility-aware weighting and continue joint optimization of the unified model. AdamW is used throughout. This schedule avoids constructing labels from the backbone’s initial, non-retrieval-adapted representations. 

4.3 Implementation Details 

Experiments are conducted on 8 NVIDIA H100 GPUs, consuming approximately 100 GPU hours to converge. We adopt Qwen3-4B-Instruct as the backbone model and use a per-GPU batch size of 128 and a learning rate of 1×10 −5. The embedding dimension matches the backbone hidden size. We primarily report nDCG@10, together with gener-ated tokens, average reasoning steps, and matched-setting wall-clock latency.

4.5 Main Results 

Table 1 reports retrieval performance of differ-ent models measured by nDCG@10 on BRIGHT benchmark (Hongjin et al., 2025). First, Setting II consistently outperforms Set-ting I across retrievers. This result is aligned with recent reasoning-intensive retrieval literature and suggests that augmenting retrieval with external query rewriting can substantially improve retrieval quality. Notably, Setting II baselines use a sepa-rate external reasoning model (GPT-4) for query expansion while keeping the retriever unchanged. Across methods, this external reasoning strategy appears to provide the largest improvements for lexical retrievers such as BM25. AURA achieves the best overall performance among the compared systems with an average nDCG@10 of 34.7. Despite not relying on any ex-ternal reasoning model, AURA outperforms all Set-ting II baselines that are powered by GPT-4 query expansion. The gains are especially pronounced in the theorem-based subsets, where AURA shows the largest margin over existing approaches. The strongest external reasoning baseline, DIVER-Retriever-4B, achieves 32.1 with GPT-4 expanded queries, while AURA improves this to 34.7 (+2.6) using reasoning generated entirely within the re-trieval model itself. Compared with Setting I, the advantage of adap-tive reasoning is even larger. The strongest base-line without inference-time reasoning achieves 28.9, whereas AURA reaches 34.7 (+5.8 absolute, +20.1% relative). 

4.6 Alignment with Corpus-Level Retrieval 

Retrieval specificity is computed from labeled pos-itives and hard negatives during training, whereas the final evaluation ranks the full corpus. To test whether the local signal reflects the listwise ob-jective, we perform full-corpus BRIGHT retrieval for every available intermediate state in each natu-rally varying-length reasoning trajectory. For each query, we compute Spearman correlation across its states between retrieval specificity and corpus-level nDCG@10, and then average the query-level cor-relations. The resulting average correlation is 0.97. Moreover, the state selected by oracle specificity-based selection is also nDCG@10-optimal for 98.1% of queries. This close agreement indicates that retrieval specificity tracks corpus-level ranking quality rather than only in-batch discrimination. 

4.7 Retrieval on Traditional IR Benchmarks 

Table 3 presents results on MS MARCO passage retrieval (Bajaj et al., 2016). We evaluate on the official development set, TREC-DL’19, and TREC-DL’20, all of which use held-out queries disjoint from the training split. These splits provide an in-domain check outside the reasoning-intensive BRIGHT setting. AURA-4B is comparable to RaDeR-7B across all three splits despite using a smaller backbone. This result suggests that utility-aware reasoning supervision does not substantially degrade conventional in-domain retrieval relative to a strong reasoning-trained retriever; broader general-purpose evaluation is left to future work. 

4.8 Ablation Study 

Table 4 reports ablation results on BRIGHT. We examine five design dimensions of AURA, each targeting a specific question about the framework. 

Q1: Is the specificity score robust to negative aggregation? The choice of negative aggregation in S(zt) has minimal impact: mean top-3, max, and mean all negatives yield similar performance (34.7, 34.6, 34.4). This indicates that AURA is robust to this design choice. Mean over all negatives may dilute the signal from hard negatives, while using only the hardest single negative may be susceptible to outlier noise; the default mean top-3 offers a reasonable balance. 

Q2: Does utility-aware supervision improve over dense intermediate supervision? This is the central ablation: we ask whether the retrieval specificity signal provides meaningful supervision beyond simply applying contrastive training at more positions. We compare four different supervi-sion strategies: (1) final state only , which applies InfoNCE only at zT as in prior work; (2) random utility labels , which trains the utility estimator with randomly assigned labels; (3) monotonic utility la-bels , which assumes reasoning quality increases monotonically with depth; and (4) uniform-weight intermediate states , which applies InfoNCE at all intermediate states with equal weight, removing utility-based differentiation. Results show a clear ordering: final-state-only (31.2) < random (31.9) < monotonic (33.2) < uni-form (33.6) < AURA (34.7). Two findings are notable. First, uniform-weight supervision already improves substantially over final-state-only (+2.4), confirming that denser contrastive training is benefi-cial. However, AURA outperforms uniform weight-ing by a further 1.1 points, demonstrating that utility-aware differentiation among intermediate states provides supervision that cannot be achieved by simply increasing the number of contrastive training positions. Second, monotonic labels under-perform uniform weighting ( −0.4), indicating that the assumption of monotonically increasing reason-ing quality is actively harmful—consistent with the over-reasoning phenomenon motivating this work. Random labels degrade close to final-state-only, confirming that the pairwise ranking loss requires accurate ordering signal to function effectively. 

Q3: Are trajectory filtering and gradient isola-tion necessary? Retaining trajectories in which no intermediate state improves over the initial query changes nDCG@10 only marginally, from 34.7 to 34.6. Filtering therefore provides a small benefit by removing trajectories without a positive within-trajectory ranking signal, but does not ex-plain AURA’s overall improvement. In contrast, re-moving gradient isolation causes a substantial drop to 29.8. Retrieval gradients propagated through earlier prefixes therefore interfere with stable joint generation–retrieval optimization, supporting the gradient-isolation design. 

Q4: How well does the utility estimator ap-proximate oracle selection? At inference time, ground-truth retrieval specificity is unavailable, which motivates utility estimation. The utility esti-mator Vϕ achieves 34.7, 0.3 points below the oracle upper bound (35.0), where the oracle selects the reasoning state with the highest true S(zt) using ground-truth relevance labels. Random state selec-tion drops to 32.3. This narrow aggregate perfor-mance gap indicates that Vϕ provides an effective approximation of retrieval utility without access to relevance labels. Q5: Are simple adaptive stopping rules suf-ficient? We compare AURA with an LLM-confidence rule based on the mean token entropy of the current reasoning step, tuning both the thresh-old and stopping direction on development data. Its strongest variant obtains 32.8 nDCG@10 at 3.2 average steps, compared with AURA’s 34.7 at 2.8 steps. Thus, generation confidence alone does not reliably identify the reasoning state that is most discriminative for retrieval. 

Q6: When should reasoning stop? Early stop-ping consistently outperforms using the full reason-ing trajectory (33.0), validating that over-reasoning degrades retrieval quality. Among stopping strate-gies, patience p=1 (34.7) performs best; greedy peak stopping ( p=0 ) is comparable (34.6), while increasing patience to p=3 (33.7) allows too many low-utility steps through. The performance gap be-tween p=1 and no early stopping (+1.7) confirms that adaptive termination is essential. 

4.9 Inference-Time Compute Efficiency 

Table 5 compares inference-time reasoning cost. To isolate the effect of utility-guided stopping, we first compare AURA with and without early stop-ping under the same Qwen3-4B backbone, hard-ware, batch size, and software stack. Without early stopping, AURA generates 332 tokens (9.5 steps) per query, takes 4.6 seconds, and achieves 33.0 nDCG@10. Utility-guided stopping reduces gen-eration to 96 tokens (2.8 steps) and latency to 0.76 seconds, corresponding to a 3.5 × token reduction and approximately 6 × speedup, while improving performance to 34.7 (+1.7). The utility estimator itself adds approximately 1 millisecond per query. For reference, the strongest external reasoning base-line (DIVER + GPT-4 expansion) generates 479 tokens per query using a separate reasoning model yet achieves lower performance (32.1). We do not report its wall-clock latency because external-model serving is not matched to our deployment.

4.10 Qualitative Analysis 

Trajectory inspection shows that discarded later steps can be semantically valid rather than incorrect. In the insects-and-LEDs example in Figure 2, early reasoning identifies the proximate-versus-ultimate causation distinction, improving separation from hard negatives. Later steps add evolutionary and sensory-ecology background. Though useful, these additions repeat encoded constraints or general-ize the query toward hard negatives about evolu-tion and insect ecology. The estimator therefore stops once the discriminative constraint is encoded. This illustrates AURA’s central distinction: infor-mative text need not provide retrieval utility; stop-ping should depend on document discrimination rather than textual detail. 

5 Conclusion 

We presented AURA, a utility-guided framework that supervises intermediate reasoning states and stops generation when predicted retrieval utility ceases to improve. AURA achieves the strongest average BRIGHT performance among the com-pared methods while substantially reducing reason-ing cost, without an external inference-time rea-soner. Controlled ablations and full-corpus anal-ysis show that the gains arise from utility-aware supervision rather than denser supervision or trajec-tory filtering, and that retrieval specificity closely tracks nDCG@10. Results on MS MARCO sug-gest limited in-domain degradation, while utility-guided greedy expansion further improves the performance–compute trade-off. 

Limitations 

Despite its efficiency gains, AURA remains slower than single-pass retrieval because reasoning is gen-erated autoregressively. Its utility labels are con-structed once from a warmed-up checkpoint and may become less accurate as the embedding space evolves during subsequent optimization. Finally, our evaluation focuses primarily on reasoning-intensive English retrieval; broader validation on web search, multilingual retrieval, and generation tasks is needed to establish generality and charac-terize the remaining retrieval–generation trade-off.
