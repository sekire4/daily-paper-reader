Abstract 

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) is the dominant paradigm for grounding large lan-guage models in external corpora, but its similarity-driven, chunked pipeline has struc-tural blind spots: it cannot anticipate which passages a question requires and discards document-level context that resolves cross-passage references. We introduce evidence-based generation (EBG), requiring models to return an answer with line-level citations, and a joint Ans+Evid metric that scores correctness and grounding simultaneously. We reformu-late two corpora into evidence-grounded multi-document QA benchmarks - M ULTI HOP -RAG and QASPER-MP, a multi-paper adaptation of QASPER with realistic short paper cues, and pair them with a controlled synthetic two-hop haystack and an evidence-augmented No-LiMa spanning 10K–1M characters. Across ten proprietary frontier and three open-weight LLMs, EBG isolates two RAG limitations that long-context models avoid: similarity re-trieval surfaces any relevant chunk far more reliably than all required chunks for multi-fact queries, and chunking destroys document meta-information needed to resolve identity-bearing queries. EBG also exposes grounding failures invisible to answer-only evaluation, including incomplete citations in two-needle settings and a widening gap between Ans and Ans+Evid 

when context contradicts parametric knowl-edge. Data and code are available at https: //github.com/debobanerjee/LC-EBG .

1 Introduction 

Large Language Models (LLMs) can rapidly pro-cess large text volumes to identify information rel-evant to a query. This capability suits applications such as legal discovery, medical analysis, financial due diligence, literature review, etc., which require synthesis from large document collections. 

> *Equal contribution.

The predominant paradigm for grounding LLM outputs in external knowledge is retrieval-augmented generation (RAG; Lewis et al., 2020), which retrieves passages via embedding similar-ity and conditions the generator on those passages alone. While effective for single-fact lookups , this design has several structural limitations. First, each retrieved chunk is stripped of its surrounding con-text: the model cannot see where a paragraph falls within a document or how it relates to neighbor-ing sections. Second, document-level metadata (authorship, date, section hierarchy) is typically lost during chunking. Third, and most critically, similarity-based retrieval cannot anticipate which combination of passages will be needed for ques-tions requiring integration across multiple locations. This is particularly acute for multi-hop questions ,where the answer depends on chaining facts that may appear in distant, semantically dissimilar pas-sages (Karpukhin et al., 2020; Li et al., 2024). Our goal in this paper is not to advocate replac-ing RAG with long-context (LC) models outright, but to make these structural limitations measurable. RAG systems are typically evaluated on answer correctness alone, which conflates two very differ-ent failure modes: the retriever fails to surface the right passages, or the reader fails to use them. To separate the two, we propose evidence-based gen-eration (EBG) , a paradigm where the model must produce both an answer and explicit references to the input lines that support it. EBG inherits RAG’s built-in verifiability (Lewis et al., 2020), but ex-tends it to long-context settings where the model is given the full document neighborhood and must 

select its own evidence. The joint Ans+Evid met-ric pinpoints where a pipeline breaks: in retrieval, in grounding, or in reading. Recent work shows LC models can match or exceed RAG accuracy given sufficient context (Li et al., 2024; Laiten-berger et al., 2025), but without provenance these comparisons are inconclusive; EBG closes that gap. arXiv:2610.26951v1 [cs.CL] 18 Oct 2026 We make the following contributions: • EBG framework. We define the evidence-based generation task and a joint Ans+Evid metric, and evaluate ten proprietary frontier and three open-weight LLMs under matched LC and RAG con-ditions across 10K–1M character contexts. • Evidence-grounded benchmarks. We reformu-late two corpora into evidence-grounded multi-document QA benchmarks—a re-instrumented MULTI HOP -RAG with line-level evidence labels, and QASPER-MP, a multi-paper QASPER adaptation with realistic paper cues, and pair them with a controlled two-hop syn-thetic haystack and an evidence-augmented N O-LIMA. The first two stress retrieval; the last two stress long-context reading. • Characterizing RAG’s limitations. Using EBG, we isolate two failure modes of similarity-based RAG that LC sidesteps: (i) retrieval re-covers any relevant chunk far more reliably than all required chunks for multi-fact queries, and (ii) chunking discards document meta-information that identity-bearing queries depend on. On M ULTI HOP -RAG, the retrieval ceiling at realistic top-k budgets, not the reader, is the binding constraint. • Diagnostic analysis of LC grounding. Beyond the RAG comparison, EBG reveals that citation acts as a reasoning scaffold, that models cite incomplete evidence in two-needle settings, and that grounding degrades sharply when in-context evidence contradicts parametric knowledge. 

2 Related Work 

2.1 Long-Context Language Models 

Advances in transformer architectures have ex-panded context windows from thousands to hun-dreds of thousands of tokens through improved po-sitional encoding such as ALiBi (Press et al., 2022), RoPE extensions (Su et al., 2024), and memory-augmented attention (Wu et al., 2022). Training data matters equally, as pretraining corpora rarely contain long-range dependencies; Zhuang et al. (2025) construct data with long-distance references encouraging use of distant context. Yet larger win-dows do not automatically yield better reasoning. Du et al. (2025) show accuracy degrades with con-text length even under perfect retrieval, suggesting a processing bottleneck. Similarly, Liu et al. (2024) show performance drops when information appears mid-input rather than at boundaries. 

2.2 Evaluating Long-Context Reasoning 

Early long-context evaluations relied on needle-in-a-haystack tests (Kamradt, 2024), which mainly measure retrieval of planted information rather than reasoning. Subsequent work argues that such evalu-ations set the bar too low, since strong performance often reflects shallow heuristics rather than gen-uine understanding (Liu et al., 2024; Schuster et al., 2025; Modarressi et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2024; Yen et al., 2025). Recent benchmarks shift toward more demanding evaluations: ∞Bench (Zhang et al., 2024) studies degradation beyond 100K char-acters, HELMET (Yen et al., 2025) shows that benchmark choice substantially alters rankings, and NoLiMa (Modarressi et al., 2025) removes lexical shortcuts by requiring semantic matching between queries and evidence. However, these benchmarks still score models on final answers alone, leaving open whether correct answers stem from actual use of relevant context or from priors, surface cues, or chance. Unlike these answer-only benchmarks, our work focuses on grounding by requiring models to return both answers and supporting input lines, dis-tinguishing genuine reasoning from lucky guessing and enabling a more faithful assessment of whether models use long contexts effectively and reliably. 

2.3 Retrieval-Augmented Generation 

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) (Lewis et al., 2020) remains the dominant approach for grounding LLMs in external knowledge. Built on dense retrieval (Karpukhin et al., 2020) and multi-passage readers such as Fusion-in-Decoder (Izac-ard and Grave, 2021), RAG provides provenance by construction since retrieved passages serve as evidence. Recent work revisits RAG alongside long-context models, highlighting a trade-off be-tween the two paradigms (Li et al., 2024; Laiten-berger et al., 2025): long-context prompting gener-ally improves accuracy but at substantially higher cost, motivating hybrid systems that route queries by difficulty (Li et al., 2024). Controlled compar-isons also show well-tuned single-stage RAG can match elaborate multi-stage pipelines once token budgets are equalized (Laitenberger et al., 2025), while adaptive retrieval and self-reflection improve factuality and citation quality (Asai et al., 2024). Existing work primarily evaluates these approaches on accuracy or efficiency. We instead focus on grounding, distinguishing whether evidence was available from whether the model actually used it. 3 Evidence-based Generation (EBG) 

We formalize evidence-based generation (EBG) 

as a grounded question-answering paradigm that extends standard answer-only evaluation by requir-ing models to generate both answers and explicit citations to the supporting input evidence. 

3.1 Answer-only vs Evidence-based Generation 

Existing long-context evaluations, including No-LiMa (Modarressi et al., 2025), use answer-only evaluation, omitting the grounding needed to vali-date model reasoning. EBG extends this formula-tion by requiring models to generate both explicit 

evidence references to supporting input lines and the final answer , yielding a stricter evaluation set-ting in which correctness depends on both accurate reasoning and faithful grounding. To illustrate the distinction, consider a haystack containing the sentence “Then Stuart mentioned that he has been vegan for years.” at line 1,284 and the question “Which character cannot eat Bran-dade?” - a two-hop query requiring the inference that Brandade contains fish and is therefore incom-patible with veganism . The answer-only and EBG settings differ in both the system instruction and the required output format.                    

> Answer-only Prompt (User)
> You will answer aquestion based on the following book snippet: {haystack} Use the information provided in the book snippet to answer the question. Your answer should be short and based on either explicitly stated facts or strong, logical inferences. Question: Which character cannot eat Brandade? Return only the final answer with no additional explanation or reasoning.
> Model Response
> Stuart

Under answer-only evaluation, the response 

“Stuart” is simply marked correct. But under EBG, the same response is evaluated using three complementary metrics: answer accuracy ( Ans ), evidence accuracy ( Evid ), and their conjunction (Ans+Evid ). While Ans preserves standard answer correctness, Ans+Evid additionally requires the model to cite the supporting evidence at line 1,284. Together, these metrics separate answer correctness from evidence-grounded correctness.                        

> EBG Prompt (System + User)
> [system] Your job is to answer the question entirely from the context and provide areference. Your answer should cite all lines the answer is based on.
> [user] <context>{haystack}</context> Answer the question based on information only from the context. If the question is not answerable from the context, answer NA. Your response should comprise only the answer and all lines the answer is based on in json format. For example: { “lines”: [25, 412],“answer”: “John” } Question: Which character cannot eat Brandade?
> Model Response
> { “lines”: [1284], “answer”: “Stuart” }

This decomposition exposes silent failures hid-den by answer-only evaluation: a model may arrive at the correct answer while citing irrelevant lines, or retrieve the correct evidence while producing the wrong answer. The joint Ans+Evid metric there-fore serves as the strictest measure of evidence-based reasoning, requiring both accurate reasoning and faithful grounding. We also evaluate vanilla Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting, following Modarressi et al. (2025), and a CoT-based EBG prompting variant. The prompt templates are in Appendix A. 

3.2 RAG under EBG 

In this work, we evaluate RAG systems within the EBG framework by requiring models to not only answer using retrieved chunks, but also to explicitly cite the supporting evidence. This yields two RAG variants: • Answer-only RAG: Standard formulation re-quiring answer correctness only. • Answer + Evidence RAG: EBG formulation requiring correct answer and correct citation. This unified view allows us to decompose end-to-end performance into two factors: • Evidence availability: whether the retriever sur-faces the relevant needle(s) (Hit@k), • Evidence utilization: whether the model cor-rectly uses and cites the retrieved evidence (long-context accuracy). Overall, our formulation shows that RAG and EBG are not competing paradigms but complemen-tary components : retrieval determines what evi-dence is available , while EBG determines whether that evidence is correctly used and cited .4 Benchmarks 

To systematically evaluate EBG and RAG under EBG, we construct four complementary bench-marks: a fully synthetic benchmark, an evidence-augmented version of NoLiMa, MultiHop-RAG, and QASPER-MP (an adapted version of QASPER). Together, they evaluate EBG under both controlled synthetic settings and realistic long-context scenarios. 

4.1 Synthetic Dataset 

The synthetic benchmark provides precise control over haystack content and evidence placement. It consists of a distractor fact pool, a curated nee-dle set, and a haystack generation procedure. This controlled construction allows us to vary context length independently of task difficulty while retain-ing exact ground-truth evidence locations. 

Distractor fact pool. We construct a fixed pool of 66,834 unique single-sentence facts using iter-ative LLM prompting followed by deduplication, ensuring that distractors are not confusable with in-serted needles. The large pool also limits repeated distractors across independently generated trials and provides sufficient diversity for constructing long haystacks. 

Needle set. We manually curate 10 two-hop nee-dles , each containing: i) two atomic supporting facts, ii) a compositional question requiring both 

facts, and iii) accepted answer surface forms. The needles span relations such as company–product as-sociations, founder–specialty links, and birthplace 

lookups. By requiring composition across two in-dependently placed facts, the benchmark tests both evidence retrieval and integration rather than sim-ple single-fact recall. 

Haystack assembly. For a target length L, dis-tractor facts are shuffled and accumulated until reaching L characters, after which the two needle facts are inserted at independently sampled posi-tions and recorded as gold evidence. Haystacks are formatted as line-numbered i: fact entries and evaluated under the EBG prompt template (§ A). Each trial uses a newly generated haystack and a uniformly sampled needle. Consequently, perfor-mance differences cannot be attributed to a fixed ordering of distractors or evidence positions. Ap-pendix B.1 provides a representative example. 

Sanity check. At L=10 K ( ≈2K tokens), all eval-uated frontier models achieve at least 0.98 joint 

Ans+Evid accuracy over N =100 trials, confirming that the inserted needles are unambiguous and read-ily solvable when context length is modest. Any degradation at larger L therefore reflects failures in long-context evidence retrieval or integration rather than intrinsic task ambiguity. This makes the syn-thetic benchmark a controlled probe of how model reliability changes as relevant evidence becomes increasingly sparse within the surrounding context.

To stress the retrieval pipeline on the dimensions it is meant to handle, we reformulate two existing corpora into evidence-grounded multi-document question answering (QA) benchmarks. Both are designed to make RAG’s retrieval-vs.-reading split observable: gold evidence is labeled at the line (sen-tence or paragraph) level, so the same scoring code works for LC and any retriever-ordered context. 

MULTI HOP -RAG (Tang and Yang, 2024). The official release contains 609 news articles (4.6M characters, 1.2M tokens with the cl100k_base to-kenizer) and 2,556 queries in four templated types: 

inference , comparison , temporal , and null_query 

(unanswerable). Each non-null query carries an 

evidence_list of 2–4 gold facts , each consisting of a source article title and an exact textual span. Removing the 301 null_query items leaves 2,255 usable queries ; for the pilot reported in this paper, we uniformly sample 50 queries (seed 0), natu-rally stratified across the three answerable types (ninf =13 , ncomp =21 , ntemp =16 ). For the LC condition, we construct an oracle-containing per-query mini-haystack comprising ev-ery gold-evidence article plus 20 distractor arti-cles sampled from the remainder of the corpus. The articles are concatenated in random order and split into sentence-per-line entries, produc-ing contexts of approximately 241K characters on average. For the RAG condition, we perform 

open-corpus retrieval over the full 609-article cor-pus: we divide the corpus into 256-token slid-ing windows (6,296 chunks), embed the chunks with text-embedding-3-large , and rank them by cosine similarity to produce top-K contexts for 

K ∈ { 4, 10 , 25 , 50 , 100 , 200 }.The two conditions are therefore not pool-matched: LC evaluates reading and evidence uti-lization when all gold source articles are guaranteed present, whereas RAG must first retrieve the re-quired evidence from the full corpus. Accordingly, this experiment diagnoses the contribution of open-corpus retrieval failures to end-to-end performance; it is not a direct LC–RAG comparison under identi-cal candidate pools. In both conditions, gold facts are re-mapped to line numbers inside the assembled context; in RAG, a gold fact that is not retrieved has no corresponding gold line. Appendix D reports the corresponding retrieval ceilings (Hits@ k for any 

vs. all gold facts) and reproduces the answer-only RAG baselines from the original paper. 

QASPER-MP. QASPER (Dasigi et al., 2021) contains 5,049 information-seeking questions over 1,585 NLP papers, each annotated by domain experts with paragraph-level evidence and ashort free-form or extractive answer (including 

Unanswerable ). The original task is single-document and, therefore, does not exercise the re-trieval pipeline. We construct QASPER-MP (multi-paper) by pairing each gold paper with ten distractor pa-pers sampled from the QASPER pool, yielding an 11-paper haystack per query, and rewrite ev-ery question to include a realistic paper cue - in 

∼50% of cases a first-author surname (e.g., “Smith et al.”), and otherwise a 2–3 word verbatim title fragment - split by a hash of the question id. This forces the system to resolve document identity from a realistic cue rather than the full paper title. The task jointly tests document identification, evidence localization, and answer generation. Since distrac-tors come from the same NLP-paper collection, they provide domain-matched context. A success-ful system must identify the relevant paper and supporting paragraph(s) before answering, while retaining QASPER’s expert-annotated answers and evidence. For LC, papers are serialized in an XML-like format that preserves titles, abstracts, and sec-tion structure ( <paper> , <title> , <abstract> ,

<section> ); paragraphs are line-numbered, and the same paragraph-level gold labels are used for evidence scoring. For RAG, we evalu-ate both a flat variant (concatenate the top-

K retrieved paragraphs in rank order) and a 

structured variant that re-emits the surrounding 

<paper> /<title> /<abstract> scaffolding and in-serts “ ... ” markers for discontinuities, so that retrieved fragments retain their document prove-nance. We evaluate QASPER-MP at two scales. For proprietary models, we conduct a pilot on N =50 

queries, comprising 38 answerable and 12 unan-swerable examples. For open-weight models, we expand the evaluation to N =200 queries, provid-ing broader benchmark coverage and a more stable estimate of aggregate performance. Because the proprietary-model results are based on the smaller pilot, comparisons across the two model groups should be interpreted with the corresponding dif-ference in sampling uncertainty in mind. RQ1: LC-EBG addresses two RAG limitations. 

On M ULTI HOP -RAG, queries require 2–4 evi-dence facts spread across distinct articles, so faith-ful answering requires the retriever to surface all re-quired facts within its top-k budget. The conditions are therefore not pool-matched; this comparison diagnoses the cost of open-corpus re-trieval relative to reading when the gold source articles are guaranteed to be available. We first reproduce the answer-only RAG base-lines from the original benchmark under our pipeline ( text-embedding-3-large + 256-token windows; Appendix D) and measure annotated-gold retrieval coverage directly. 6 Conclusion and Future Work 

We introduced evidence-based generation (EBG) ,a framework that requires models to produce both an answer and line-level citations, together with a joint Ans+Evid metric that measures correctness and grounding simultaneously. By separating re-trieval from evidence-grounded reasoning, EBG ex-poses failures obscured by answer-only evaluation. We evaluate these failures using evidence-grounded versions of M ULTI HOP -RAG and QASPER-MP, a controlled synthetic two-hop haystack, and an evidence-augmented NoLiMa spanning 10K–1M characters. This evaluation suite spans both real-istic and controlled settings, allowing retrieval, reasoning, and grounding errors to be examined under complementary conditions. 

Across ten proprietary frontier and three open-weight LLMs, EBG reveals two key RAG limi-tations: (i) similarity retrieval finds any relevant chunk more reliably than all evidence required for multi-fact queries, imposing a retrieval ceil-ing at realistic top-k budgets; and (ii) chunking can discard document-level meta-information needed by identity-bearing queries, causing RAG to over-refuse on retrieval misses. EBG further exposes grounding failures hidden by answer-only metrics: models often cite incomplete evidence, and the gap between answer and joint accuracy widens when in-context evidence conflicts with parametric knowl-edge. These findings show that correct answers alone can substantially overstate a model’s ability to faithfully use long-context evidence. They also highlight evidence attribution as a useful diagnostic for distinguishing retrieval limitations from down-stream reasoning failures. Consequently, EBG provides a more informative basis for compar-ing RAG and long-context systems than eval-uations that collapse retrieval, reasoning, and attribution into answer accuracy alone. 

These results do not imply that “LC replaces RAG”; rather, LC and RAG exhibit distinct failure modes that EBG helps localize, motivating evalua-tions that measure both answer accuracy and evi-dence fidelity. The appropriate architecture may therefore depend not only on answer quality, but also on which sources of failure are accept-able for a given task and context regime. Future work includes scaling EBG to the full benchmarks and testing document-aware chunking. Richer evi-dence attribution and the observed reasoning bene-fits of evidence generation also motivate investigat-ing grounding objectives as part of model training. 

Limitations 

Our evaluation focuses on synthetic and semi-synthetic long-context benchmarks designed to iso-late grounding and evidence attribution behavior under controlled conditions. While these settings enable precise analysis, they do not capture the full complexity of real-world domains such as legal, scientific, or medical reasoning. In addition, our evidence metric operates at the line level and there-fore measures coarse-grained provenance rather than fine-grained span-level attribution. The bench-marks also assume that the reference evidence is sufficiently complete and unambiguous, whereas real-world questions may admit multiple valid evi-dence sets or interpretations. Our RAG analysis estimates end-to-end perfor-mance through a decomposition of retrieval and long-context accuracy rather than exhaustive evalu-ation of full production RAG pipelines across all configurations. We therefore do not systematically explore retrieval variants such as query rewriting, hybrid search, or adaptive context selection, which could alter the observed retrieval ceilings. Finally, because we evaluate proprietary frontier and open-weight models through API access, results may vary as deployed systems evolve over time. 

Ethics and Broader Impact 

This work studies evidence grounding and prove-nance in long-context language models. Our bench-marks are constructed from synthetic or publicly available text and do not contain sensitive per-sonal information. We do not train new founda-tion models; all experiments are conducted through inference-only evaluation of existing systems. We believe evidence-based evaluation can im-prove transparency and verifiability in LLM-based systems by encouraging models to explicitly iden-tify the context used to support their answers. Such capabilities may be particularly important in high-stakes applications such as legal analysis, scien-tific search, and medical document understanding, where unsupported or hallucinated answers can have significant consequences. At the same time, citation generation should not be interpreted as a guarantee of faithful reasoning. A model may cite irrelevant or incomplete evidence while producing a plausible answer. We therefore view evidence-based generation as a complemen-tary framework rather than a complete solution to factuality or interpretability in language models.

D Long-context EBG vs. RAG on MultiHop-RAG 

This appendix analyzes an oracle-containing long-context (LC) reading condition and an open-corpus retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) condition on the M ULTI HOP -RAG benchmark (Tang and Yang, 2024). The LC condition guarantees that ev-ery gold-evidence article is available to the reader together with 20 distractor articles, whereas the RAG condition must retrieve the required evidence from the full 609-article corpus. The purpose of this comparison is therefore to diagnose the contri-bution of open-corpus retrieval failures to end-to-end performance; it is not a pool-matched com-parison of LC and RAG under identical candi-date document sets. The motivation is direct: our synthetic two-hop benchmark and the NoLiMa-adapted benchmark are constructed haystacks, which leaves open the question of whether the trends we observe carry over to realistic, web-derived multi-document QA where RAG is the nat-ural baseline. The filtered M ULTI HOP -RAG subset (§4.3) of 2,255 queries tests both EBG (line-level evidence) and RAG (chunked corpus). We run experiments on a 50-query pilot set, sampled with seed 0 and stratified across three types: ninf =13 , ncomp =21 ,and ntemp =16 . We use the same queries, prompts, and scoring throughout, while LC and RAG differ in candidate-document pools as described below. 

LC haystacks. For each query, we assemble a mini-haystack of (i) all gold-evidence docu-ments and (ii) 20 distractor documents sampled without replacement from the remaining corpus. Documents are randomly concatenated, split into sentence-level lines, and rendered in the lineno: text format used by our synthetic benchmark. Gold evidence facts are re-mapped to line numbers so EBG labels match the prompt. Mini-haystacks average ∼ 241 K characters ( ∼ 58 K tokens), within every model’s context window. This is the LC (full) 

condition. 

RAG haystacks. For each query, we construct RAG contexts at K ∈ 4, 10 , 25 , 50 , 100 , 200 . The retriever searches the full 609-document corpus, divided into 256-token sliding windows (6,296 chunks). We embed chunks and queries with 

text-embedding-3-large , rank by cosine simi-larity, and use the top-K chunks as the RAG con-text. Each chunk is prefixed with [CHUNK <rank> | <title>] , and rendered with the same sentence-per-line numbering as LC. Gold evidence facts are re-mapped to lines in the retrieved context; unre-trieved facts have no corresponding gold line. The conditions are not pool-matched: LC has all gold source articles plus 20 distractors, whereas RAG must locate the required evidence across all 609 articles. This design deliberately preserves the native evidence-access setting of each approach in practice, enabling a realistic comparison between them under their respective retrieval assumptions.

D.6 Discussion 

We highlight four takeaways for the manuscript. 

Takeaway 1: LC EBG dominates RAG for an-swer accuracy, at every realistic retrieval bud-get. Under the answer-only prompt, the oracle-containing LC condition exceeds open-corpus RAG@10 by 22 –44 pp and exceeds RAG@200 by 0–10 pp. The same qualitative pattern holds un-der EBG. Because the two conditions do not share a candidate-document pool, these numbers quan-tify the combined cost of document discovery and passage retrieval relative to evidence-guaranteed reading; they are not a pool-matched estimate of an architecture-level LC–RAG effect. 

Takeaway 2: RAG’s retrieval bottleneck is multi-hop coverage, not single-hop recall. Hits@10 (any gold) is 82% but Hits@10 (all gold) is only 19% (Table 4); on inference queries the all-gold number falls to 8%. The principal source of the ob-served gap relative to the oracle-containing LC con-trol is that, at small K, open-corpus RAG rarely re-trieves the complete union of annotated gold facts. 

Takeaway 3: Citations as a reasoning scaffold replicate outside the synthetic setting. Ans ac-curacy under EBG is higher than under the answer-only prompt for two of three models, with a striking 

+14 pp improvement for Sonnet-4.5 (Table 3). This is the RQ1 effect that was masked on our synthetic benchmark by ceiling performance. 

Takeaway 4: The grounding gap is a real-data phenomenon. The Ans − Joint gap in the LC condition is 50–74 pp depending on model, and is largest on inference queries. Models routinely produce a correct multi-hop answer while citing only one of the two required hops - the same asymmetric-retrieval failure mode we documented on the synthetic benchmark, here observed on nat-uralistic web data.

D.8 Limitations and next steps 

The frontier-model experiments use a 50-query pi-lot, so the reported estimates remain subject to sam-pling variation, particularly for small question-type slices such as the 13 inference queries. Evaluating all 2,255 usable queries would provide tighter con-fidence intervals. We also hold the retriever fixed to 

text-embedding-3-large with 256-token sliding windows. Reranking, hybrid sparse–dense retrieval, query expansion, longer chunks, and document-aware indexing could improve the open-corpus RAG results. For the evaluated retriever, corpus-wide all-gold Hits@ 10 is 19% over the 2,255 us-able queries; on the 50-query pilot, all-gold cover-age at K=10 is 28% . Under the assumptions that the gold annotations are exhaustive and that every annotated fact is necessary, these coverage rates strongly constrain faithfully grounded performance at this retrieval budget. A key design limitation is that the M ULTI HOP -RAG LC and RAG conditions are not pool-matched. The LC condition is an oracle-containing reading control : every gold-evidence article is guar-anteed to be present together with 20 distractor ar-ticles. The RAG condition instead performs open-corpus retrieval over the full 609-article corpus. Consequently, the reported LC–RAG gaps com-bine the effects of full-corpus document discovery, passage retrieval, and downstream reading; they should not be interpreted as an intrinsic ranking of LC and RAG under matched evidence access. A complementary pool-matched RAG condition that retrieves from the same per-query gold-plus-20 candidate set would separate within-pool chunking and ranking effects from the cost of full-corpus doc-ument discovery. We leave this controlled compari-son, together with evaluation of stronger retrieval pipelines and the full query set, to future work.

E Long-context EBG vs. RAG on QASPER-MP 

This appendix documents a second compari-son of long-context evidence-based generation (LC EBG) against retrieval-augmented genera-tion (RAG), this time on a multi-paper adapta-tion of QASPER (Dasigi et al., 2021) that we call QASPER-MP. Whereas M ULTI HOP -RAG (Ap-pendix D) tests multi-document reasoning on news, QASPER-MP tests it on scientific papers, where the gold question–answer–evidence triples were written assuming a single known paper. To make the task non-trivial under retrieval, we (i) place each gold paper alongside ten distractor papers, (ii) rewrite the question so it identifies the target paper by a realistic short reference (an author name or a 2–3 word topic cue), and (iii) evaluate in both LC and RAG conditions over the same 50-query stratified pilot. The headline finding is that the aggregate score (Answer-F1 or LLM-judge, averaged over all ques-tions) makes LC and RAG look roughly tied, but this tie hides an answerability asymmetry: LC EBG beats RAG by 5–22 judge points on answerable questions for every frontier model tested , while RAG “wins” on unanswerable questions because it over-refuses. The over-refusal mechanically matches the QASPER Unanswerable gold label on the 24% of pilot questions where that is the correct answer, pulling the aggregate up. 

E.1 Setup Dataset. We use the QASPER dev split ( N =1,005 questions over 281 papers). Each question is annotated with an answer type (extractive ,

abstractive , boolean , or none - meaning unan-swerable from the paper) and an evidence set : the list of paragraphs from the paper that support the answer. Up to three annotators contribute per ques-tion; we follow the official QASPER evaluator and take the max F1 across annotators. 

50-query pilot. We sample 50 questions, strat-ified by answer type, so that the composition matches the dev-split distribution: 13 boolean, 13 abstractive, 12 extractive, 12 unanswerable. This split (38 answerable, 12 unanswerable) is the denominator for every condition below. 

Multi-paper haystacks (LC condition). For each pilot query we build an 11-paper haystack con-sisting of the gold paper plus 10 distractor papers drawn uniformly from the dev split. Each paper is rendered with explicit document boundaries: 

> <paper> <title> Paper title </title> <abstract> Paper abstract </abstract> <section> Section name </section>
> Paragraph 1 of that section Paragraph 2 of that section . . .
> </paper>

Lines are concatenated and numbered i: <line> 

so the model can cite specific lines as evidence (consistent with our EBG protocol). The median LC haystack size is ∼32K characters; the largest is ∼56K. The gold-evidence paragraph(s) for each query are re-mapped to line indices in the assem-bled haystack. 

RAG condition. We embed every QASPER dev paragraph and every (disambiguated) query with OpenAI text-embedding-3-large , and re-trieve the top-K paragraphs by cosine similarity. We evaluate four retrieval variants, factored as 

{flat , struct } × { pool11 , full }:

flat_pool11. Top-K paragraphs retrieved from the same 11-paper pool as the LC haystack (pool-matched candidate corpus). The reader sees a flat list of retrieved paragraphs with a one-line 

[CHUNK <rank> | <title>] marker before each. 

struct_pool11. Same retrieval, but paragraphs are regrouped by their source paper and wrapped in the same XML layout used for LC. Within a paper, paragraphs are sorted by their orig-inal index, and discontinuities (gaps where intermediate paragraphs were not retrieved) are marked with a single ... line. 

flat_full / struct_full. Same as above but retriev-ing from the full dev corpus ( N ≈ 6,000 para-graphs) rather than the 11-paper pool. This is the realistic open-corpus RAG setting: the retriever must locate the target paper among hundreds of similar NLP papers using only the short cue in the rewritten question. 

Models. We evaluate five frontier reasoning mod-els: GPT-5 , GPT-5.5 , Claude Opus 4.6 , Claude Sonnet 4.5 , and Gemini 3 Flash (preview). All runs use the same query set, the same retrieval indices, and the same EBG prompt template. Ci-tation rules in the prompt forbid citing structural lines ( <title> , <abstract> , <paper> , [CHUNK , or 

... ); these lines are present only as context. 

Question disambiguation. QASPER questions were written to be unambiguous given the paper; many become ambiguous in a multi-paper haystack (e.g., “What evaluation metrics were used?” be-comes pathological with 11 candidate papers). “TF-IDF Features” ). Cues are intentionally short and realistic, match-ing what a researcher would type into a search box. This makes the retrieval problem genuinely harder than indexing on full titles or descriptive paraphrases.

E.3 Disambiguation Details and Cue Length Sensitivity 

We initially experimented with longer “descrip-tive” cues generated by GPT-5 from each paper’s title and abstract (e.g., “In the cross-lingual zero-shot NMT paper” ). These cues are unrealistically informative: they often include domain phrases that do not appear in the title at all, which makes retrieval implausibly easy. Embedding-based re-trieval coverage with descriptive cues was 60.0% (all-needles@10, full corpus). Switching to the short, realistic cues described in Appendix E.1 dropped coverage to 48.0% - a 12-point hit - re-flecting the fact that “In Smith et al.” or “In the BERTSUM paper” is a much weaker retrieval sig-nal than a paraphrased domain summary. We use the short, realistic cues throughout this appendix because they correspond to how re-searchers actually query systems in practice; the 

+12pp coverage gain on descriptive cues would inflate RAG numbers relative to what users would experience.

E.5 LLM-Judge Evaluation 

To control for token-F1 brittleness on paraphrases, we additionally score outputs with an LLM judge: • Judge model: GPT-5. • Inputs: the original question, all annotator gold answers, and the model prediction. • Output: a single number in {0, 0.5, 1}. The judge is instructed to be lenient about format-ting and elaboration but strict about content; partial credit (0.5) is reserved for answers that capture some but not all required information. “Unanswerable” vs. “Unanswerable” scores 1.0; “Unanswerable” vs. a real answer (or the reverse) scores 0.0. Judgments are cached by content hash so re-peated scoring is free. Across all conditions, Judge scores are systematically higher than Answer-F1 (the judge is lenient about phrasing), but the order-ing of conditions is preserved. The LC vs. RAG gap on answerable questions is larger under the judge than under Answer-F1, because the judge does not penalize Sonnet-4.5 and Gemini-3-Flash for their characteristic boolean elaborations.

E.7 Strengthening RAG: metadata-in-chunk retrieval 

The full-corpus RAG deficit above is partly a re-trieval failure rather than solely a limitation of the downstream reader. After v2 disambiguation, each query carries only a short, typically 2–3 word cue identifying the relevant paper. In the baseline in-dex, however, candidate chunks are embedded as raw paragraph text, without any explicit represen-tation of document identity. The retriever must therefore infer the connection between a sparse paper-level cue in the query and a paragraph whose local content may never mention the paper title or otherwise reveal its source. This mismatch is especially consequential in the full-corpus setting, where evidence from the gold paper competes with paragraphs drawn from the entire ∼281-paper dev corpus. Even when the query contains enough in-formation to identify the intended document, the embedding space has no direct document-level fea-ture with which to exploit that signal, making it easy for the gold paper - and consequently the rele-vant evidence within it - to fall outside the retrieval budget. A natural way to give RAG a fairer chance is therefore to expose document identity directly to the retriever by injecting metadata into the in-dexed chunks. We rebuild the retrieval index so that every paragraph is embedded together with its source paper’s title, using the representation “title \n\n paragraph ”. This modification creates an explicit lexical and semantic bridge between the short paper cue supplied by v2 disambiguation and every paragraph originating from that paper, while preserving the paragraph text needed to rank evidence within the document. We then re-run the hardest retrieval condition, flat_full@10 , at 

n=200 for all five readers. Importantly, this in-tervention is confined to retrieval: generation-side rendering, prompting, context construction, and reader settings are unchanged. Thus, any down-stream improvement can be attributed to changing the representation used for ranking rather than to providing the reader with additional metadata or altering the generation procedure. Metadata-in-chunk produces a large improve-ment in evidence retrieval. On the 200 -query set, the fraction of queries for which the full gold-evidence set is recovered within the top-10 in-creases from 43 .5% to 61 .0% , a gain of 17 .5 per-centage points. E.8 Summary 

QASPER-MP corroborates the picture in Ap-pendix D: under the pool-matched pool11 condi-tion, long-context EBG and RAG end up at compa-rable aggregate scores, but the mechanism is asym-metric. RAG’s higher refusal rate inflates its score on unanswerable questions; on the answerable sub-set - where retrieval quality actually matters - LC EBG beats every RAG variant for every one of the five frontier models we tested. The gap is largest for the strongest readers (Opus-4.6 and GPT-5.5 each show +0 .13 judge points LC over best RAG on an-swerable questions), suggesting that LC EBG ben-efits disproportionately from stronger long-context comprehension. The gap widens further when the retrieval corpus expands from the apples-to-apples 11-paper pool to the realistic full-corpus setting, where LC outperforms the best RAG configuration by 5–13 judge points.
