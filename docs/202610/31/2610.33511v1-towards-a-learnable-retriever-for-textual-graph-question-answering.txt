Towards a Learnable Retriever for Textual Graph Question Answering

1University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign
2Wayne State University

Abstract
Textual graph question answering (GraphQA) and graph-
based retrieval-augmented generation (GraphRAG) have
gained increasing attention as a way to ground large language
models (LLMs) on structured knowledge. Despite extensive
efforts, existing methods still face two key gaps. The first is
an architectural gap: existing methods typically fine-tune an
LLM-based generator, but do not also make the retriever it-
self a learnable LLM for graph evidence selection within one
end-to-end retrieve-then-generate pipeline. The second is a
retrieval-supervision gap: existing retrievers rarely use gold
answer signals to supervise evidence selection. As a result,
they may fail to recall evidence nodes required for reason-
ing, resulting in insufficient coverage of the gold answer set;
meanwhile, naive attempts to boost recall often introduce ex-
cessive irrelevant information that compromises compactness
and overwhelms the LLM. To address these gaps, we pro-
pose TaLenT, an LLM-based end-to-end retriever-generator
framework for textual GraphQA. To bridge the architectural
gap, TaLenT pairs a learnable LLM-based retriever for evi-
dence selection with a fine-tuned LLM-based generator within
one unified retrieve-then-generate pipeline. To bridge the
retrieval-supervision gap, we leverage gold answer signals
as retriever supervision. Specifically, we stratify graph nodes
into gold answers, answer-related nodes, and irrelevant noise.
We then adopt a weighted pseudo-label loss to prioritize gold
answers and preserve answer-related nodes for coverage, while
discarding irrelevant noise for compactness. Experimental re-
sults across three GraphQA datasets demonstrate that TaLenT
consistently improves downstream performance.

Introduction
Large language models (LLMs) have achieved impressive
progress in general-purpose generation and reasoning (Thop-
pilan et al. 2022; Bang et al. 2023), but their outputs are
bounded by what is encoded in model parameters. Trained
on large-scale open-domain corpora collected before a fixed
cutoff date, LLMs may hallucinate or omit critical evidence
when a query requires precise, up-to-date, or domain-specific
knowledge (Zhao et al. 2026; Wang et al. 2023b).
To mitigate these limitations, Retrieval-Augmented Gen-
eration (RAG) has emerged as a dominant paradigm that
grounds LLM outputs in external evidence by dynamically
retrieving relevant knowledge snippets for context-aware
generation (Lewis et al. 2020; Shi et al. 2024; Gao et al.
2023). While conventional RAG methods (Lewis et al. 2020;
Shi et al. 2024) mainly retrieve evidence from unstructured
text corpora, recent work has shifted toward textual graphs
as a more expressive and structured knowledge source. This
trend has spurred the development of Graph-based RAG
(GraphRAG) and Graph Question Answering (GraphQA).
By exploiting the explicit semantics and relational structure
of textual graphs, graph-based RAG methods support more
grounded reasoning and complex information synthesis (IV
et al. 2019; Luo et al. 2025; He et al. 2024).
A graph-based RAG pipeline generally consists of two
stages: graph evidence retrieval and answer generation. On
the generation side, existing methods often use an LLM-
based generator and fine-tune it over retrieved graph contexts.
This makes graph evidence retrieval a critical step, since the
generator can only reason over the evidence selected for it.
Conventional retrieval methods in GraphRAG can be cat-
egorized into non-parametric, graph neural network (GNN)-
based, and LM-based approaches. Non-parametric retriev-
ers construct subgraphs using graph search algorithms or
predefined rules (Taunk et al. 2023; Yasunaga et al. 2021).
GNN-based methods encode graph structure with GNNs and
identify task-relevant components to form the retrieved sub-
graph (Mavromatis and Karypis 2024). LM-based methods
often use LMs to assist graph retrieval, for example by read-
ing structured data, generating retrieval cues, or using LM
reasoning to guide evidence selection (Baek, Aji, and Saffari
2023; Jiang et al. 2023).
However, with these retrieval designs, existing graph-
based RAG pipelines still leave an architectural gap. While
the generator is often fine-tuned as an LLM-based module,
the retriever itself is usually not made a learnable LLM for
graph evidence selection. Instead, evidence selection is still
handled by non-parametric, GNN-based, or LM-assisted pro-
cedures. As a result, the retriever and generator are not both
trained as LLM modules within one end-to-end retrieve-then-
generate pipeline.
Beyond thisarchitectural gap, these conventionalretrievers
also face a retrieval-supervision gap. They rely primarily on
query similarity while rarely using gold answers to supervise
evidence selection. Query similarity can favor nodes match-
ing query terms over answer-critical evidence, leading to two
limitations: coverage, where a query-based retriever may fail
to cover the gold answer set in the retrieved subgraph, and

In this work, we address both gaps by proposing TaLenT,
an LLM-based end-to-end retriever-generator framework for
textual GraphQA. To bridge the architectural gap, TaLenT
makes the retriever itself a learnable LLM for graph evi-
dence selection, and pairs it with a fine-tuned LLM-based
generator within one unified retrieve-then-generate pipeline.
Within the full pipeline, we first apply a rule-based prepro-
cessing step to construct a candidate subgraph, providing a
high-recall pool of nodes under the LLM context budget.
The learnable LLM-based retriever is then trained to select
relevant evidence nodes, and the generator is fine-tuned on
contexts constructed from the trained retriever’s outputs. To
bridge the retrieval-supervision gap, we use gold answer sig-
nals as retriever supervision to improve both retrieval cov-
erage and compactness. To improve retrieval coverage, we
train a retriever based on a weighted pseudo-label objec-
tive, incorporating gold answer signals to guide the retrieval
process. To promote answer-critical evidence, we prioritize
gold answer and down-weight other rest signals to improve
retrieval compactness. Intuitively, the loss assigns the highest
weight to gold answers to ensure coverage, assigns a much
smaller weight to answer-related nodes to retain useful con-
text, and ignores irrelevant noise to enforce compactness. In
this way, TaLenT improves evidence selection before gen-
eration, leading to better downstream QA performance. Our
contributions are summarized as follows:
• Idea. We introduce an LLM-based end-to-end retriever-
generator framework for textual GraphQA, where a learn-
able LLM-based retriever and a fine-tuned LLM-based
generator are placed within one unified retrieve-then-
generate pipeline. This design fills the architectural gap
by making the retriever itself a learnable LLM for graph
evidence selection, rather than only fine-tuning the down-
stream generator.
• Method. We train the learnable LLM-based retriever with
a weighted pseudo-label objective that leverages gold
answer signals. By prioritizing gold answer nodes and
down-weighting weakly related nodes, the objective fills
the retrieval-supervision gap and leads to better down-
stream QA performance.
• Evaluation. We conduct extensive experiments on three
GraphQA benchmarks to validate both retrieval effective-
ness and end-task QA performance. Our method outper-
forms the best competitor by at least 3.18%.

Preliminaries
Notation. We use calligraphic letters to denote sets, e.g., V
and E for node and edge sets. A textual graph is represented
as G = (V, E), where each node v ∈V has a textual attribute
text(v), e.g., an entity name or concept, and each edge e =
(u, r, v) ∈E denotes a relation r connecting the source node
u to the destination node v. The textual attribute of an edge
is denoted as text(e). We use S to denote the subgraph of
G, where S ⊆G indicates that its node set VS ⊆V and its
edge set ES ⊆E. We use q for the query and a for the final
answer from the generator. We use a⋆to denote the token
sequence of the gold answer in the dataset and use V⋆as
the set of nodes appearing in the gold answer, i.e., the gold
answer set. We denote the retriever and generator by Rθ and
Gϕ, respectively.
2.1
Problem Setup
We consider a general retrieve-then-generate setup (He et al.
2024) for textual graph question answering (GraphQA). Each
instance in the dataset consists of a natural-language query q
and a textual graph G = (V, E) as the knowledge base. Due to


the large scale of G, it is impractical to directly serialize G into
an LLM prompt, hence the system first retrieves a relevant
subgraph, conditioned on which an answer is generated.
Formally, a retriever Rθ extracts a subgraph S from the
textual graph G based on the query q:

3
Method
Overview. TaLenT is an LLM-based end-to-end retriever-
generator framework for textual GraphQA. Its two core
trainable LLM modules are: (i) a learnable nodes retriever
Rθ trained with weighted pseudo-label supervision (Section
3.1), and (ii) an answer generator Gϕ fine-tuned with stan-
dard Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) (Section 3.2). The full
TaLenT pipeline is illustrated in Figure 2. Given a query q
and a full textual graph G, a rule-based PCST preprocessing
step first constructs an initial candidate connected subgraph
S that fits the LLM context budget. We then train a learn-
able retriever Rθ with weighted pseudo-label supervision to
produce an answer-relevant node set ˆV (Section 3.1). Using
answer-relevant nodes ˆV, we construct textual contexts and
fine-tune a generator Gϕ with standard supervised learning
(Section 3.2).
3.1
Retriever Training
As discussed earlier, PCST and other query-similarity based
retrievers often fail to cover the answer-related nodes and in-
clude irrelevant noise, resulting in limited coverage and poor
compactness. We therefore use gold answer signals to guide
retriever training, encouraging prioritization of gold answers
while down-weighting other signals. We first describe TaL-
enT’s retriever prompting and then introduce the weighted
pseudo-label objective.
Retriever prompting. We implement our learnable retriever
Rθ as an instruction-following LLM (with LoRA) that per-
forms node selection based on a provided candidate sub-
graph. Given a query q and the PCST subgraph S, we prompt
the LLM to produce the nodes that are most helpful for an-
swering q. The LLM outputs should be exactly one line in
the format of “name1|name2|...”. Each name such as
name1 and name2 represents the names of the nodes Rθ
finally selects. This constrained format makes the output di-
rectly interpretable as a retrieved nodes set, which we denote
by ˆV = Rθ(q, S). A concrete retriever prompt example is
provided in Appendix E.1. It is worth noting that since our
retriever Rθ is an LLM, it leverages its inherent parametric
knowledge during the selection process. Consequently, the
output node set ˆV may include nodes that were not present
in the initial candidate subgraph S (i.e., ˆV̸ ⊆VS). In our
method, ˆV encompasses the complete set of nodes generated
by Rθ, reflecting both the information from S and the LLM’s
internal knowledge.
Fine-tuning with weighted pseudo-label supervision. Re-
call that existing works in GraphRAG use retrievers that are
largely query-similarity driven, which leads to two limita-
tions: coverage and compactness. Our goal is to train the
retriever to output the node set ˆV that achieves both high
coverage and compactness: it should reliably include nodes
that appear in the gold answer set V⋆(coverage) while avoid-
ing redundant or weakly related candidates (compactness).
The gold answer provides precisely the missing supervision,
i.e., the gold signal, for retrieval. To inject gold signals into
retrieval, we leverage the dataset-provided gold answers and
stratify candidate nodes into three levels: (i) gold answers,
which are candidate nodes that overlap with the gold answer
set V⋆, and provides evidence that the retriever should prior-
itize to support correct reasoning, (ii) answer-related nodes,
which are candidate nodes within S but are not gold an-
swers, and may still be helpful for providing context, though

3.2
Generator Training
Generator Prompting. Given the predicted node set ˆV =
Rθ(q, S) output by the trained Rθ, we form the generator in-
put as a prompt that concatenates the query q with subgraphs
derived from ˆV. These subgraphs contain (i) the retrieved
nodes in ˆV and (ii) their associated edges ˆE that appear in S.
Concretely, we include edges for which at least one endpoint
is in ˆV:
ˆE = {(u, r, v) ∈E : u ∈ˆV or v ∈ˆV}.
(14)
We then serialize the retrieved nodes ˆV together with the
selected edges ˆE into a serialized graph text and prepend the
query q to form the generator prompt. Importantly, this seri-
alized graph text is not required to form one whole connected
subgraph. This design avoids forcing unnecessary “bridge”
nodes or edges solely to maintain connectivity, which often
increases irrelevant context and hurts compactness. Instead,
we allow the retrieved evidence to appear as multiple dis-
connected components, as long as it covers the nodes in ˆV.
Moreover, as we mentioned in Section 3.1, ˆV may include
nodes that come from Rθ’s parametric knowledge and do not
appear in the initial candidate subgraph S, we retain these
nodes as additional isolated nodes in the serialized graph
text. A concrete generator prompt example together with the
graph serialization format is provided in Appendix E.2.

3.3
Inference and Evaluation
At inference time, we apply the full retrieve-then-generate
pipeline using the retriever and generator fine-tuned on the
training (and validation) splits. For each test instance (q, G),
we first apply the fixed PCST-based preprocessing step to
construct the candidate subgraph S, then run the learned
retriever to obtain ˆV = Rθ(q, S). We materialize (ˆV, ˆE)
into a textual graph context and feed it to the generator to
produce the final answer a = Gϕ(q, ˆV, ˆE). We report task
metrics computed from the generator outputs on the test split.

Experiments
4.1
Experiment Setup
Datasets and Metrics. To evaluate our method on textual QA
tasks, we conduct experiments on three textual graph ques-
tion answering datasets: ExplaGraphs and WebQSP from
the GraphQA benchmark (He et al. 2024), and CWQ (Tal-
mor and Berant 2018). Specifically, (1) ExplaGraphs’s task
is to determine whether two arguments are supportive or
contradictory. (2) WebQSP is a knowledge graph question
answering dataset whose primary task is to answer ques-
tions that require multi-hop reasoning. (3) CWQ is a more
compositional KGQA benchmark than WebQSP, with more
complex questions and longer reasoning chains. For evalu-
ation, we adopt Accuracy for ExplaGraphs and Hit@1 for
WebQSP and CWQ. Dataset statistics and additional dataset
details are provided in Appendix A.
Baselines. We compare against two groups of baselines.
(1) Inference-Only methods that include Zero-shot, Zero-
CoT (Kojima et al. 2022), CoT-BAG (Wang et al. 2023a),
KAPING (Baek, Aji, and Saffari 2023), Graph-based Infer-
ence, and StructGPT (Jiang et al. 2023). (2) Prompt-Tuning
methods that include Frozen LLM + Prompt Tuning (PT),
GraphToken (Perozzi et al. 2024), G-Retriever (He et al.
2024), and TaoNa-A (Yan et al. 2025). For G-Retriever, we
include a variant that fine-tunes the LLM-based generator
with LoRA (G-Retriever w/ LoRA). Additional discussion
of our differences from other answer-aware or answer-related
methods and recent graph retrieval baselines not included in
Table 1 is provided in Appendix D.
Experiment pipeline. For the main experiments, we use the
open-source LLaMA2-Chat-7B model (Touvron et al. 2023)
as the backbone for both the retriever and the generator, and
conduct all experiments on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU.
We follow the same train/validation/test splits as prior work
for each dataset. For WebQSP and CWQ, since the full KG
exceeds the context budget, we first construct a PCST can-
didate subgraph with kv=ke=10 and then apply our learn-
able retriever. During retriever training, we use the weighted
pseudo-label objective with strong-positive weight 1. The
weak-positive weight is set to λ = 0.001 for WebQSP and
λ = 0.01 for ExplaGraphs and CWQ. Additional implemen-
tation details, including hyperparameter configurations and
computing infrastructure, are provided in Appendix B. Ap-
pendix C reports sensitivity analyses for λ and PCST size
parameters kv and ke. A backbone ablation with Llama-3-
8B (Grattafiori et al. 2024) is provided in Appendix G.

of TaLenT. First, TaLenT moves beyond rule-based, query-
driven evidence selection by introducing a learnable LLM-
based retriever for task-adaptive graph evidence selection
within an end-to-end retriever-generator framework. Sec-
ond, it trains this retriever with gold-answer-guided weighted
pseudo-label supervision to improve evidence coverage and
compactness, thereby benefiting downstream generation.

5
Ablation Study
We conduct two ablation studies on WebQSP to illustrate (i)
the effect of pseudo-label retriever training (ii) the effect of
learnable retriever.
5.1
On the Effect of Pseudo-label Training
Retrieval quality. Table 3 compares TaLenT’s retriever
against an ablated variant, which uses the same backbone
LLaMA2-Chat-7B model but keeps it frozen (no training)
and directly prompts it to select helpful nodes from the
PCST candidate subgraph. Results show that the frozen re-
triever fails to recover gold nodes required for answering,
with Hit@1, Precision, and Recall of 36.61%, 14.97%, and
26.26%, respectively. In contrast, TaLenT’s retriever explic-
itly prioritizes gold answer nodes during retriever fine-tuning
using pseudo label training, substantially improving coverage
with a Hit@1 of 80.28%, a precision of 24.55% and a recall
of 67.84%, indicating that injecting gold signals into retriever
training is crucial for selecting answer-relevant nodes.
Downstream QA performance. We next examine whether
the retrieval improvement translates into better end-task QA.
Table 4 summarizes QA Hit@1 under three systems: (i) TaL-
enT with a frozen retriever (ablate training on retriever), (ii)
TaLenT without the retriever module (directly feeding the
PCST candidate subgraph to the generator), and (iii) the
full TaLenT system. Switching from a frozen retriever to
our pseudo-label trained retriever improves QA Hit@1 from
68.37% to 76.97%. This gain is consistent with Table 3:
pseudo-label training recovers substantially more gold nodes,
providing the generator with more reliable evidence.
5.2
On the Effect of Learnable Retriever
We further isolate the contribution of the retriever by re-
moving it from the pipeline and directly feeding the PCST
candidate subgraph into the generator, i.e., without Rθ or
ˆV selection. As shown in Table 4, removing the learnable
retriever reduces QA performance from 76.97% to 72.50%,
indicating that an explicit retriever provides clear benefits for
downstream QA. Notably, using a frozen retriever performs
even worse (Hit@1 of 68.37%) than removing the retriever
entirely, suggesting that retrieval is not inherently beneficial
when gold answer node coverage is low, as noisy context
can mislead the generator. This result further underscores
the importance of our weighted pseudo-label training, which
substantially improves retrieval quality for end-task QA.
6
Related Work
Retrieval Augmented Generation. RAG grounds LLMs
with external evidence to reduce hallucinations and im-
prove faithfulness (Sun et al. 2024; Gao et al. 2023). Clas-
sic RAG systems follow a straightforward index-retrieve-
generate workflow (Ma et al. 2023), while many recent vari-
ants strengthen retrieval with additional processing before
and after retrieval. For example, pre-retrieval techniques such
as query transformation, expansion, and rewriting are of-
ten adopted to better match the search space (Zheng et al.
2024; Gao et al. 2023), and post-retrieval reranking is com-
monly used to refine the evidence set (Blagojevi 2023). More
broadly, modular RAG systems use LLMs to iteratively refine
retrieval queries or evidence selection (Yu et al. 2023).
RAG over Graphs. Extensive works study how to combine
graph-structured information with LLMs (Li et al. 2024;
Wang et al. 2023a). Prior efforts span general graph modeling
(Liu et al. 2024; Lei et al. 2023), multimodal architectures
that incorporate graph-like structures (Yoon et al. 2023; Li
et al. 2023). Representative applications include fundamental
graph reasoning (Zhao et al. 2023), node prediction (Sun
et al. 2025; Chen et al. 2024), graph-level prediction (Qian
et al. 2023). A related line of work discusses GraphRAG
/ GraphQA, where the system selects graph evidence and
conditions an LLM on graph context to answer questions (IV
et al. 2019; Luo et al. 2025).
7
Conclusion
We address two key gaps in existing GraphRAG systems:
an architectural gap and a retrieval-supervision gap. To ad-
dress both gaps, we propose TaLenT, an LLM-based end-
to-end retriever-generator framework for textual GraphQA.
To bridge the architectural gap, TaLenT makes the re-
triever itself a learnable LLM for graph evidence selection
and pairs it with a fine-tuned LLM-based generator within
one unified retrieve-then-generate pipeline. To bridge the
retrieval-supervision gap, we train TaLenT’s retriever with
a gold-answer-guided weighted pseudo-label loss that pri-
oritizes answer-related signals to improve coverage while
suppressing noise for compactness. Extensive experiments
on three GraphQA benchmarks demonstrate that TaLenT
consistently improves downstream QA performance.

Additional Dataset Details
A.1
Datasets
Datasets and Metrics. To evaluate the effectiveness of our
method on textual QA tasks, we conduct experiments on three
textual graph question answering datasets, ExplaGraphs and
WebQSP from the GraphQA benchmark (He et al. 2024),
and CWQ (Talmor and Berant 2018). Specifically, (1) Ex-
plaGraphs is developed for generative commonsense reason-
ing, with a specific focus on the construction of explanation
graphs for stance prediction in debates. By providing de-
tailed and unambiguous commonsense-augmented graphs, it
enables an evaluation of whether arguments either support
or refute a specific belief. ExplaGraphs’s primary task is
to determine whether the arguments are supportive or con-
tradictory. Its node denotes commonsense concepts and the
edge denotes Commonsense relations. In this dataset, the
gold answer is a binary label (i.e., ‘support’ or ‘counter’),
which does not provide explicit supervision for the retriever.
Therefore, we leverage the LLaMA2-Chat-7B model (Tou-
vron et al. 2023) to generate pseudo gold reasoning node
sets, and treat these annotated nodes as pseudo-label super-
vision V⋆for training the learnable retriever. Due to the small
graph size of the graphs in ExplaGraphs, we directly use the
entire graph as the candidate context for retrieval. Following
GraphQA (He et al. 2024), we evaluate the end task with Ac-
curacy. (2) WebQSP is a large-scale multi-hop knowledge
graph QA dataset that consists of 4,737 questions. The pri-
mary task is to answer questions that necessitate multi-hop
reasoning. It employs a subset of Freebase (Bollacker et al.
2008), with a specific focus on facts within 2 hops of the
entities identified in the questions. The nodes denote Entities
in Freebase and the edges denote Relations in Freebase. The
gold answers are lists of Freebase entities, which naturally
correspond to node lists and can directly serve as V⋆with-
out requiring additional annotation. For metrics, we report
Hit@1, counting a prediction as correct if the generated an-
swer matches any of the answers in the gold answer list. (3)
CWQ (ComplexWebQuestions) is a compositional KGQA
dataset constructed from WebQSP by generating more com-
plex queries and rewriting them into natural language (Tal-
mor and Berant 2018). It contains 34,689 questions. Com-
pared with WebQSP, CWQ contains more complex ques-
tions and longer reasoning chains. Like WebQSP, CWQ is
grounded in Freebase, where nodes denote entities and edges
denote relations. The gold answers are lists of Freebase en-
tities and can therefore directly serve as V⋆. We evaluate
CWQ with Hit@1. Dataset statistics are summarized in Ta-
ble 5. See Appendix A.2 for Pseudo-Gold Nodes Annotation
for ExplaGraphs.
A.2
Pseudo-Gold Nodes Annotation for
ExplaGraphs
ExplaGraphs provides gold supervision only as a stance la-
bel (support/counter), rather than node-level evidence anno-
tations. To make our learnable retriever pseudo label super-
vision applicable to this dataset, we introduce a lightweight
preprocessing step on the training and validation splits: we
use Llama-2-7b-chat to annotate a set of pseudo-gold
reasoning nodes for each example. Concretely, we provide
the model with (i) the full textual explanation graph, (ii) the
two arguments, and (iii) the gold stance label, and prompt
it to select nodes from the graph that form the critical rea-
soning path for deciding whether the arguments support or
counter each other. The selected nodes are then used as V⋆
in our weighted pseudo-label objective for training the re-
triever. This annotation is used only to train our retriever: the
generator is still fine-tuned with the original task supervision
(predicting support vs. counter), and at inference time the
pipeline does not require access to any node annotations.
We further conduct two sanity checks on these pseudo-
gold node annotations. First, we manually inspected LLM an-
notations and found these LLM-selected nodes to be broadly
consistent with human judgment. For example, for the argu-
ment pair “Women should be allowed in military combat.”
and “Females as soldiers should be acceptable.”, whose gold
label is support, the annotator selects critical nodes includ-
ing strength, military combat, and females as “pseudo-gold”
nodes, which we found to be reasonable and aligned with the
human interpretation of key evidence needed to justify the
support relation.
Second, we perform a cross-annotator consistency check
using GPT-4o-mini. Specifically, we re-annotate the Ex-
plaGraphs dataset with GPT-4o-mini using the same an-
notation prompt as used for Llama-2-7b-chat, and com-
pare the resulting node sets. We report two simple agree-
ment statistics. (1) Any-overlap rate: in 80.44% of graph
instances, the two annotators share at least one selected
node. (2) Reference coverage: when treating the origi-
nal Llama-2-7b-chat annotations as the reference, the
GPT-4o-mini annotations cover 83.75% of those nodes
on average. These results suggest that the pseudo-gold node
annotations are not arbitrary: a second annotator recov-
ers most of their core evidence, while manual inspection
further indicates that the selected nodes are reasonable
for stance prediction. These sanity checks suggest that the
Llama-2-7b-chat pseudo-gold annotations are reason-
ably consistent and provide useful supervision for training
the ExplaGraphs retriever.

Discussion of Answer-Aware or
Answer-Related Methods and Additional
Recent Baselines
Table 8 summarizes several key distinctions between TaL-
enT and recent related baselines. Here, in this compar-
ison, LLM-based end-to-end refers to a unified retrieve-
then-generate pipeline that pairs a learnable LLM-based re-
triever for graph evidence selection with a fine-tuned LLM-
based generator (i.e., the retriever itself is made a trainable
LLM, rather than only fine-tuning the downstream gener-
ator). Coverage-compactness indicates whether the method
explicitly aims to improve answer-relevant evidence coverage
while suppressing irrelevant noise. KGQA indicates whether
the method is directly applicable, as-is, to open-ended KGQA
benchmarks such as WebQSP and CWQ. Label-prediction
GraphQA indicates whether the method is directly appli-
cable, as-is, to graph QA tasks whose outputs are discrete
labels rather than entity answers, such as ExplaGraphs-style
support/counter prediction.
Answer-aware or answer-related methods.
Some related
methods do exploit answer-aware or answer-related signals,
but they do so in task formulations that differ substantially
from ours.
QA-GNN (Yasunaga et al. 2021).
QA-GNN is designed
for multiple-choice QA rather than our open-ended GraphQA
setting. Given a question and an answer choice a ∈C, it
forms a QA context [q; a], links entities mentioned in the
question and answer choice to a KG, and performs joint
LM+GNN reasoning over a constructed subgraph for that
question-choice pair. Therefore, QA-GNN is conditioned on
candidate answer choices, rather than using dataset gold an-
swers to supervise a retriever. Its main focus is answer-choice
scoring in multiple-choice QA, not training a retriever for
open-ended graph QA generation. Accordingly, it does not
satisfy our LLM-based end-to-end criterion, does not explic-
itly optimize the coverage-compactness trade-off, and is not
directly applicable as-is to either open-ended KGQA bench-
marks such as WebQSP/CWQ or label-prediction graph QA
tasks such as ExplaGraphs.
GNN-RAG (Mavromatis and Karypis 2024).
GNN-
RAG targets KGQA. It uses training question-answer pairs to
supervise a GNN that scores answer candidates over a dense
KG subgraph, and then retrieves the shortest paths connect-
ing question entities to those candidates as context for a
tuned LLM. Thus, GNN-RAG does use answer supervision
during training, but in the form of answer-candidate scoring
and path retrieval for KGQA, rather than weighted pseudo-
label supervision for graph evidence selection. In particular,
it does not contain a trainable LLM-based retriever, nor does
it explicitly optimize the coverage-compactness trade-off. It
is directly applicable to KGQA, but not to label-prediction
graph QA tasks such as ExplaGraphs, whose outputs are
discrete labels rather than KG entities.
Other recent graph retrieval baselines.
We also discuss
several other recent graph retrieval methods that are useful
points of comparison but differ substantially in task setting
and retrieval target.
RoG (Luo et al. 2024).
RoG is a planning-retrieval-
reasoning framework tailored to KGQA. It first generates
relation paths as plans, then retrieves valid reasoning paths
from the KG and reasons over them with the same LLM.
Although RoG jointly trains its planning and reasoning mod-
ules, its retrieval step itself is plan-guided KG path retrieval
rather than a trainable LLM-based retriever under our defi-
nition above. Moreover, RoG is designed for KGQA where
answers are expected to be KG entities reachable via KG
paths. Therefore, it does not satisfy our LLM-based end-
to-end criterion, does not explicitly optimize the coverage-
compactness trade-off, and is not applicable as-is to label-
prediction graph QA tasks such as ExplaGraphs.
CG-RAG (Hu et al. 2025).
CG-RAG targets research
question answering over citation graphs. Its retrieval module
ranks relevant chunks by combining sparse and dense re-
trieval signals with graph encoding over contextual citation
graphs, and the retrieved chunk subgraphs are then used for
generation. This differs from our setting in both graph type
and retrieval unit: CG-RAG performs chunk-level retrieval
over citation graphs, rather than node-level evidence selec-
tion over a given QA graph. Accordingly, it neither provides
an LLM-based end-to-end retriever-generator pipeline in our
sense nor explicitly optimizes coverage-compactness, and it
is not directly applicable as-is to either KGQA benchmarks
such as WebQSP/CWQ or label-prediction graph QA tasks
such as ExplaGraphs.
SentGraph (Liang et al. 2026).
SentGraph is a sentence-
level graph-based RAG framework for multi-hop QA over
document collections. It constructs hierarchical sentence
graphs with explicit logical dependencies and performs
graph-guided sentence retrieval and path expansion. In con-
trast, our setting assumes a given QA graph whose nodes and
edges already define the reasoning space, such as Freebase
entities/relations in WebQSP/CWQ or commonsense con-
cept graphs in ExplaGraphs. Thus, SentGraph differs from
our setting in graph granularity, retrieval target, and bench-
mark formulation. It also does not provide an LLM-based
end-to-end retriever-generator pipeline in our sense, does
not explicitly optimize coverage-compactness, and is not di-
rectly applicable as-is to either KGQA benchmarks such as
WebQSP/CWQ or label-prediction graph QA tasks such as
ExplaGraphs.
Overall, the distinctive feature of TaLenT is not merely
that it leverages answer-related signals, but that it combines
a unified LLM-based end-to-end retriever-generator pipeline
with weighted pseudo-label supervision, thereby addressing
both the architectural gap and the retrieval-supervision gap
while explicitly improving the coverage-compactness trade-
off. Moreover, the framework applies to both open-ended
KGQA benchmarks (e.g., WebQSP and CWQ) and label-
prediction GraphQA tasks (e.g., ExplaGraphs).

More Related Works
Graphs and Large Language Models
A growing body of
work studies how to combine graph-structured information
with LLMs (Li et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2023a). Prior ef-
forts span general graph modeling (Liu et al. 2024; Lei et al.
2023), multimodal architectures that incorporate graph-like
structures (Yoon et al. 2023; Li et al. 2023). Representa-
tive applications include fundamental graph reasoning (Zhao
et al. 2023), node prediction (Sun et al. 2025; Chen et al.
2024), graph-level prediction (Qian et al. 2023). A related


line of work discusses GraphRAG / GraphQA, where the
system selects graph evidence and conditions an LLM on
graph context to answer questions (IV et al. 2019; Luo et al.
2025).
Parameter-Efficient
Fine-Tuning
Parameter-efficient
fine-tuning (PEFT) methods enable adapting large pretrained
models with limited trainable parameters. Representative
techniques include prompt tuning (Lester, Al-Rfou, and
Constant 2021), prefix tuning (Li and Liang 2021), and
low-rank adaptation such as LoRA (Hu et al. 2022). Related
adapter-style methods (e.g., LLaMA-adapter (Zhang et al.
2024)) have also supported rapid adaptation and inspired
multimodal instruction-following systems such as MiniGPT-
4 (Zhu et al. 2023) and NExT-Chat (Wu et al. 2024). PEFT
has recently been explored in graph+LLM settings as well,
including GraphLLM (Chai et al. 2025) and GraphToken
(Perozzi et al. 2024) for graph reasoning, and GNP (Tian
et al. 2024) for knowledge-graph QA.
