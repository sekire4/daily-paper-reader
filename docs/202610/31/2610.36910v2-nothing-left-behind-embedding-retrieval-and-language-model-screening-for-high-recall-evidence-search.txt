Nothing Left Behind: Embedding Retrieval and Language Model
Screening for High Recall Evidence Search
Giuliano Martinelli*, Abelardo Carlos Martinez Lorenzo, Satvik Garg, Linxi Wang,
Riccardo Orlando, Piriyakorn Piriyatamwong, Samuel Paul Fraiberger†
Development Impact AI Lab, World Bank Group
*Lead author. †Correspondence: sfraiberger@worldbankgroup.org
Abstract
AI systems that ground their answers from re-
trieved documents usually need only a few rele-
vant passages, but applications such as system-
atic reviews and legal discovery need nearly all
of them, because their conclusions rest on the
full body of evidence. These applications have
traditionally searched with curated taxonomies,
which can only find what their vocabulary de-
scribes. We compare taxonomy-based retrieval
with an alternative neural retrieval based on sen-
tence embeddings and followed by language
model screening in ImpactAI, an evidence syn-
thesis application built on 7,354 randomized
trials. Measured against the studies included
in 29 systematic reviews, the share of studies
found rises from 45% to 85%, and the screen-
ing model agrees with human annotators as
well as annotators agree with each other. This
gain comes at a cost in speed: at the median,
retrieving and screening evidence for a query
takes 77 seconds, against 6 seconds for tax-
onomy retrieval. The results suggest finding
evidence with embeddings, organizing it with
taxonomy labels, and setting how many candi-
dates to screen according to the time available
for each query.

Introduction
Language models that answer from retrieved doc-
uments, the setup known as retrieval augmented
generation (RAG) (Lewis et al., 2020), usually
need only a few relevant passages to answer a
question. Some applications, however, need ev-
ery relevant document, because their output is a
conclusion about the whole body of evidence. A
systematic review, for example, pools all trials of
an intervention to estimate its effect, so a missed
trial can shift both the estimate and the conclu-
sion drawn from it. Similarly, in legal discovery a
party must produce every document responsive to
a request, and in patent search a single overlooked
publication can invalidate a patent. Using software
to find documents for this kind of review is known
as technology assisted review, and has been studied
in both legal and medical settings (Cormack and
Grossman, 2014; Kanoulas et al., 2017). In all of
these applications, missing a relevant document is
costly, but so is returning too many, because every-
thing returned must still be read.
These applications have traditionally relied on
curated taxonomies. Each document is tagged with
labels from a controlled vocabulary, each query
is mapped to the same labels, and the documents
that carry them are returned. Systematic review
guidance still recommends this, combined with
free text search (Lefebvre et al., 2025), for good
reasons: labels normalize terminology, make each
search inspectable, and group results for compari-
son and pooling. Until recently, taxonomies were
also the only reliable option, since embedding re-
trievers (Karpukhin et al., 2020) performed poorly
on specialized collections (Thakur et al., 2021).
The weakness of this approach is that the same
labels serve both to find documents and to orga-
nize them. Labels compress every document and
every query into a few categories, which makes
them good for organizing but limits them for find-
ing. A question narrower than its label returns
everything under that label, so many of the results
do not answer it. Worse, a concept missing from
the vocabulary cannot be matched at all, and no
amount of additional searching will find it. The
share of relevant documents a taxonomy can find is
therefore capped by the coverage of its vocabulary.
The alternative separates the two jobs. Docu-
ments and queries are embedded in the same vector
space, the documents closest to the query are re-
trieved, and a language model then screens each
of these candidates against the full question. This
follows the multistage retrieval pattern, in which a
fast model selects candidates and a slower, more
accurate one examines them (Wang et al., 2011;
Nogueira and Cho, 2019), here with a language

2.1
Evidence and Task
ImpactAI is built on 7,354 studies across sec-
tors including health, education, agriculture, pub-
lic finance, and social protection, each report-
ing a randomized controlled trial (RCT). A study
usually tests one or more interventions on sev-
eral outcomes, so ImpactAI splits each study
into intervention–outcome pairs, each holding the
study’s results for one intervention on one outcome
(Table 1). The corpus contains 74,826 pairs, and the
same structured evidence underlies Query2Effect,
a benchmark for predicting effects from natural
language questions (Martinelli et al., 2026).
Pairs are the unit of retrieval. Given a question,
the task is to return every pair that addresses it, and
each returned pair carries its results, along with
the population, context, and design of its study.
Retrieving pairs instead of whole studies matters
because a study often reports several results, of
2


which only some answer a given question. Each
pair also stays linked to its study, so the share of
relevant evidence found can be measured in studies
as well as in pairs.
2.2
Taxonomy Retrieval
The first design, shown in Figure 1a, uses one tax-
onomy both to find and to organize evidence. Of-
fline, every pair is tagged with entities, the con-
cepts of a development economics catalog. At
query time, a language model identifies the phrases
in the question that name a concept and assigns
each a type, such as intervention, outcome, pop-
ulation group, or geography.
For each phrase,
the system then retrieves up to 50 candidate en-
tities of that type, combining semantic similar-
ity with BM25 keyword matching (Robertson and
Zaragoza, 2009), and a language model selects the
entity that best fits the phrase in the context of the
full question. Finally, the pairs tagged with the
selected entities are returned.
This design has the benefits described in Sec-
tion 1: results arrive grouped by shared categories,
users can see which concepts their question was
linked to, and a lookup returns every pair tagged
with a given label. It also has the limits described
there. In Figure 1a, for example, a question about
rapid diagnostic tests for malaria is linked to the
broader concept of malaria treatment, and returns
all the evidence tagged with it. In addition, because
the pipeline chains phrase detection, typing, entity
selection, and tagging, an error at any step loses
evidence. Finally, for a fixed catalog the result is a
fixed set, so no amount of additional searching can
add to it.
2.3
Semantic Retrieval and Relevance
Filtering
The second design, shown in Figure 1b, separates
finding from organizing. Each pair is represented
by its text, the intervention and outcome names
and descriptions together with the study context,
which is embedded once, offline. At query time,
the question is embedded into the same space, and
the K pairs closest to it form the candidate set,
where K is a fixed number. Retrieval thus draws
on the text of the evidence itself, with no taxonomy
in between.
Closeness in the embedding space, however, is
only a proxy for relevance. Close candidates of-
ten share a topic with the question but differ in
the intervention, outcome, population, or context
the user asked about. A language model therefore
screens each candidate: given the question and a
pair’s text, it decides whether the pair answers the
question, and pairs that do not are discarded.
Screening is applied only to the K closest pairs
because it is far more expensive than retrieval.
Comparing a question with every precomputed em-
bedding is fast, whereas each screening decision
requires a language model call at query time. Re-
trieval therefore narrows the corpus to a number
of candidates that screening can afford, following
the usual logic of a cascade: a cheap step over
everything, then an expensive step over a few. Sec-
tion 4.4 measures how this cost grows with K.
The two steps play distinct roles, which is why
we evaluate them separately. Retrieval sets the ceil-
ing on what can be found: screening cannot recover
a pair that was never retrieved, and when more than
K pairs are relevant, some are necessarily missed.
Screening then decides how much of the retrieved
evidence is kept, and how much irrelevant material
remains. Taxonomy labels can be applied to the
resulting set afterward, to group and compare what
was found.
3
Experimental Setup
Following Section 2, we evaluate the semantic
design one step at a time, then as a whole. We
first measure retrieval quality, which bounds what
screening can keep, then how well screening agrees
with human judgment, then how much evidence the
full pipeline recovers, and finally how long it takes.
Table 2 summarizes the datasets used at each step.
Two kinds of questions recur throughout: user ques-
tions, submitted to ImpactAI during its beta, and
synthetic questions, each generated from one study
in the corpus.
3.1
Models
We compare four embedding models for retrieval:
Gemini embeddings (Lee et al., 2025), Voy-
age (Voyage AI, 2025), E5-large-v2 (Wang et al.,
2022), and Granite (Awasthy et al., 2025). Screen-
ing uses Gemini 2.5 Flash (Comanici et al., 2025),
which taxonomy retrieval also uses for phrase de-
tection and entity selection. The deployed taxon-
omy service timed in Section 4.4 uses Gemini 3
Flash Preview instead.

3.3
Screening Agreement
Screening is evaluated on 100 user questions and
100 synthetic questions. For each question, an an-
notator labeled the 30 pairs closest to it under Gem-
ini embeddings as relevant, potentially relevant, or
not relevant. Most of these candidates are on topic,
with 24.0 and 23.8 of the 30 labeled relevant or po-
tentially relevant on average, so the test focuses on
the fine distinctions screening has to make. Gemini
2.5 Flash screens the same pairs, and we compare
its decisions with the human labels, counting only
relevant as positive.
To put this agreement in context, ten additional
user questions were labeled independently by two
annotators, and we measure their agreement on the
276 of 300 pairs that received both labels. An-
notators were assigned per question, so the two
annotators in Table 4 are not the same two people
throughout. In both comparisons, we report Co-
hen’s κ, which measures agreement beyond what
chance would produce, along with Pearson correla-
tion and F1 scores.
3.4
Full Pipeline
The full pipeline is evaluated against systematic re-
views, which serve only as a reference and play no
part in the system. The authors of a systematic re-
view search the literature exhaustively and decide,
study by study, which ones answer the review’s
question. Many of the studies they include are also
in ImpactAI, so asking the system a review’s ques-
tion reproduces the search a review team would run,
with the authors’ decisions as the expert answer.
From 29 reviews, we derived 348 questions: the
questions the reviews address, and narrower ver-
sions of them. The relevant studies for each ques-
tion are the studies the review included that are
also in the ImpactAI corpus. Reviews were not
required to have all their studies in the corpus; in-
cluded studies outside it are simply left out of the
reference. For the narrower questions, a language
model helped decide which of these studies apply.
Each question has 4.07 relevant studies on average.
Returned pairs are mapped to their studies, and
we report the share of relevant studies found, aver-
aged over questions (macro recall), for K of 100,
500, 1,000, and 2,500. Results at smaller K are
computed from the 2,500 closest pairs. We also re-
port a combination of the two designs, which adds
the taxonomy results, unscreened, to the screened
semantic results. A review’s list contains only the
studies its authors included, so a returned study
outside the list is not counted as an error. These

3.5
Latency
We time each module on 30 questions from the
systematic review benchmark, run one at a time:
taxonomy retrieval, semantic retrieval alone, and
semantic retrieval with screening, at the same four
values of K. Taxonomy retrieval and K = 2,500
are timed on all 30 questions, while times at smaller
K are estimates calibrated on three of them. In the
timed configuration, screening groups candidates
from the same study, up to 15 per prompt, with
a separate decision for each pair. This configura-
tion is more recent than the one used in the recall
and agreement experiments. We report the mean,
median, and 95th percentile.

4.3
Full Pipeline
Semantic retrieval with screening finds nearly twice
as many of the studies included in systematic re-
views as taxonomy retrieval: 85.0% at K = 2,500,
against 45.1% (Figure 2). The gain comes from
retrieval: before screening, the candidates already
contain 96.7% of the relevant studies. Screening
then discards some relevant studies along with ir-
relevant ones, lowering recall by 11.7 points.
Recall grows with the number of candidates, but
with diminishing returns. After screening, it rises
from 61.3% at K = 100 to 82.7% at K = 1,000,
and only to 85.0% at K = 2,500, although the last
step more than doubles the candidates to screen.
The two designs also find partly different stud-
ies. Adding the taxonomy results, unscreened, to
the screened semantic results raises recall by 12.6
points at K = 100, to 73.8%, but by only 2.9
points at K = 2,500, to 87.9%. The taxonomy is
therefore most useful as a complement when the
candidate budget is small.
Finally, the review lists measure recall but not
precision. In user feedback on 45 questions, users
rated 94.7% of the returned pairs as relevant, so the
pairs that reach users are mostly on target.

4.4
Latency
Screening accounts for almost all of the time, and
that time grows with the number of candidates (Ta-
ble 5). Semantic retrieval alone takes between 1.1
and 1.5 seconds per question at the median, what-
ever K. With screening, the median rises from 6.9
seconds at K = 100 to 33.3 seconds at K = 1,000
and 77.2 seconds at K = 2,500, against 6.0 sec-
onds for taxonomy retrieval. At this rate, screening
all 74,826 pairs would take roughly 40 minutes per
question, which is why retrieval must narrow the
set first.
Read together with recall, these times show what
depth costs. At K = 100, semantic retrieval with
screening takes about as long as taxonomy retrieval
(6.9 against 6.0 seconds) and finds more of the
relevant studies (61.3% against 45.1%). Going
from K = 1,000 to K = 2,500, by contrast, adds
2.3 points of recall and 44 seconds per question.
Because the two services use different models (Sec-
tion 3), these times compare deployed modules
rather than the designs themselves.
5
Discussion
Use meaning to find evidence and labels to or-
ganize it.
Semantic retrieval with screening finds
nearly twice as many of the studies included in
systematic reviews as taxonomy retrieval. Because
it matches the full question against the text of the
evidence, it can find concepts that are missing from
the catalog or narrower than its labels, which is the
likely source of the gain. Labels remain useful once
the evidence is found: they normalize terminology,
make results inspectable, and group them for com-
parison, which pooling requires. They also remain
the right tool when a user wants everything under
a known category, and at small candidate budgets
6


they add studies that semantic retrieval misses. The
two designs are therefore best used together, each
for the job it does well.
Depth is the main design choice.
With seman-
tic retrieval in place, the remaining choice is how
many candidates to screen. Each additional can-
didate costs time while recall gains shrink, so a
comprehensive search, such as one feeding a meta-
analysis, may justify a long wait, whereas an ex-
ploratory search can use a smaller budget. Two
directions could lower this cost: setting the depth
for each question, since broad questions have more
relevant pairs than narrow ones, and screening with
smaller, faster models.
Maintenance and scale.
The two designs also
differ in upkeep. A taxonomy must be extended
and the evidence retagged as the collection grows,
while semantic retrieval only needs new embed-
dings, along with monitoring of the screening
model, whose decisions are harder to audit as a
whole than explicit labels. As the collection grows,
broad questions will also have more relevant pairs,
so a fixed budget will miss a larger share of them,
and depth, with its cost, will need to grow too.
6
Conclusion
Applications such as systematic reviews, legal dis-
covery, and patent search need every relevant docu-
ment, and a taxonomy caps how many it can find.
In ImpactAI, embedding retrieval followed by lan-
guage model screening raised the share of system-
atic review studies found from 45% to 85%, with
screening that agrees with people as well as peo-
ple agree with each other, at the cost of a longer
wait that the number of candidates controls. Our
evidence comes from one collection, but the mech-
anism is general: wherever labels are used to find
documents, separating finding from organizing is
likely to raise recall in the same way.
Limitations
Our results come from one collection of RCTs in
development economics, and both the taxonomy la-
bels and the text of each pair depend on the quality
of the information extracted from each study. The
systematic review benchmark measures how many
of a review’s included studies are found within this
collection, not coverage of the literature as a whole,
and its narrower questions rely on study assign-
ments made with the help of a language model.
The retrieval benchmarks are labeled by a Gemini
model over a pool built partly from Gemini embed-
dings, which may favor them (Zobel, 1998), and
the human samples are small, particularly the ten
questions labeled by two annotators. The latency
comparison covers deployed modules with differ-
ent configurations: the timed taxonomy service
uses Gemini 3 Flash Preview, and the timed screen-
ing configuration is more recent than the one used
to measure recall and agreement. Throughput un-
