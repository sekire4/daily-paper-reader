ADAPTING

LANGUAGE

Samarth Varshney

ABSTRACT
Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) lets large language models (LLMs) an-
swer knowledge-intensive questions from retrieved evidence, but adapting a
general-purpose RAG system to a specialized domain such as medicine is diffi-
cult when only an unlabeled corpus is available. Self-training approaches such
as SimRAG address this by letting the LLM write its own question–answer pairs
from domain passages and fine-tuning on the pairs that survive a round-trip con-
sistency filter. We observe that these approaches treat every retained example
identically: they never ask whether the student already knows the answer, al-
ready extracts it correctly from the retrieved context, or fails—and if so, whether
the failure lies with the reader or with the retriever. To tackle this, we propose
TriageRAG, a failure-aware self-training approach that probes the student on each
self-generated question with and without retrieved context, triages it into one of
four categories (KNOWN, SOLVED, CONTEXT-FAILURE, RETRIEVAL-FAILURE),
and routes each category to the training signal that addresses it, including a para-
metric route that teaches the reader to answer when retrieval misses. Probing
is cheap and repeated every round, so the training mixture follows the student,
and the triage histogram doubles as a label-free diagnostic of where the domain
gap lies. Experiments on 11 datasets spanning three domains with a Qwen3-4B
backbone, and on CS-Bench with Qwen3-8B, demonstrate that TriageRAG out-
performs a budget-matched SimRAG by 1.2–6.4 points and is the only method
that recovers the closed-book level in every case where retrieval hurts the base
model. A single ablation identifies the mechanism: round-trip filtering removes
every training context that lacks the evidence, and a reader that never sees such
contexts learns to over-trust noisy retrieval.

INTRODUCTION
Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) Lewis et al. (2020); Gao et al. (2023); Asai et al. (2024)
grounds the answers of large language models (LLMs) in retrieved evidence, which adapts them
to long-tail knowledge without retraining Ovadia et al. (2024) and reduces hallucination Shuster
et al. (2021). Adapting a general-purpose RAG system to a specialized domain such as medicine is
nevertheless hard: reader and retriever both face a distribution shift, and labeled domain QA data is
scarce or private, so fine-tuning has to rely on synthetic data. SimRAG Xu et al. (2025) is a strong
recent recipe. It fine-tunes an instruction-tuned LLM on general retrieval-oriented data, prompts the
same model to write short-span, multiple-choice and claim-verification questions from unlabeled
domain passages, keeps the pairs whose source passage is retrieved in the top-k for the generated
question (round-trip consistency), and fine-tunes on the survivors with retrieved contexts.
We start from an observation about what such a pipeline does not look at: every retained pseudo-
labeled pair receives the same treatment. Nothing in the loop asks whether the student already knows
the answer without context, whether it already extracts it correctly from the retrieved passages, or
whether it fails—and if so, whether the failure belongs to the reader (the evidence is in the context
but the answer is wrong) or to the retriever (the evidence never reached the context). These problems
have different remedies: contextual fine-tuning teaches a reader to use evidence that is present, but
it cannot help when the evidence is absent. Worse, the round-trip filter removes exactly the pairs

We propose TriageRAG1, which lets the student’s own behaviour decide how each self-generated
example is used (Figure 1). After synthesizing question–answer pairs as in SimRAG, TriageRAG
probes the student on every pair with and without retrieved context, triages it into KNOWN, SOLVED,
CONTEXT-FAILURE or RETRIEVAL-FAILURE, and routes each category to a different treatment:
contextual supervised fine-tuning (SFT) for context failures; a parametric route that trains the reader
to produce the verified answer despite an unhelpful retrieved context—the situation it faces whenever
retrieval misses—for retrieval failures; and small consolidation shares for the rest. Probing costs a
fraction of fine-tuning, so the diagnosis is repeated every round and the mixture follows the student.
The triage histogram is itself a label-free diagnostic of where the domain gap lies.
We evaluate on 11 datasets from medicine, natural science and computer science (CS) with a Qwen3-
4B backbone, and on CS with Qwen3-8B as well, against SimRAG, a RAFT-style mixture and a
random-selection control that share the same Stage-I model, synthetic pool, filter, recipe and budget.
Our contributions are:
• A student-aware view of self-training for domain RAG in which each self-generated question
is classified by failure type with cheap closed-book and open-book probes, yielding a label-free
diagnostic of the domain gap.
• TriageRAG, which routes the four categories to different training signals and iterates, with SFT
only and no reward model, judge or labeled data.
• Experiments on three domains and two backbone sizes in which TriageRAG outperforms a budget-
matched SimRAG by 1.2–6.4 points, with three training seeds and paired bootstrap intervals on
every headline comparison; an ablation that identifies the parametric route as the ingredient behind
the gap; and a controlled negative result on tuning the retriever with retrieval failures.

Self-training and self-improvement.
Self-training fits a student on teacher-produced labels and

classifies the type of failure of a corpus-grounded RAG system to choose among training signals,

Knowledge-aware fine-tuning.
Gekhman et al. (2024) and Kang et al. (2025) show that fine-
tuning on facts the model does not know encourages hallucination; KaFT Zhong et al. (2025) weights
domain SFT examples by the conflict between parametric and contextual knowledge; Longpre et al.
(2021) and Mallen et al. (2023) study when to trust memory over context. These works use labeled
data. TriageRAG applies knowledge probing to self-generated data and adds the dimension that
matters for RAG—whether the evidence was retrieved—so that reader and retriever failures are
treated differently.
3
METHODOLOGY
3.1
PROBLEM SETUP
In a RAG problem, a retriever R returns the top-k passages D = {d1, . . . , dk} from a corpus C
for a query q, and the LLM Mθ generates an answer a from (q, D). We aim to improve the QA
capability of Mθ in a specialized domain where only the unlabeled corpus C is available. Following
SimRAG, we first fine-tune the model on retrieval-oriented general-domain data (Stage-I) and then
adapt it to the domain with self-generated pseudo-labeled tuples (Stage-II). TriageRAG changes
Stage-II: instead of treating all retained tuples identically, it diagnoses the student on each tuple and
routes the tuple to the training signal that fits the diagnosis. The retriever is kept frozen in the main
configuration; Section 3.4 discusses a retriever route.
3.2
STAGE-I: RETRIEVAL-ORIENTED FINE-TUNING
We fine-tune an instruction-tuned backbone on a scaled-down version of SimRAG’s Stage-I blend
(16,000 examples; Appendix A): general context-aware QA in short-span, yes/no and multiple-
choice formats, and the two skills used in synthesis, answer generation (candidate answer spans
from a passage) and answer-conditioned question generation.
3.3
STAGE-II: FAILURE-AWARE SELF-TRAINING
Each round t takes the current student Mt (M1 is the Stage-I model) through four steps.
(1) Synthesis.
For a sampled passage d ∈C the student proposes candidate answer spans a ∼
Mt(· | d) and then an answer-conditioned question q ∼Mt(· | a, d). As in SimRAG we generate
three formats: short-span questions; multiple-choice questions, which reuse a span question and add
three distractor spans drawn from other passages, matched on whether the answer is numeric; and
claims that are supported or refuted by d. We keep a tuple (q, a, d) only if the student recovers a
when given the gold passage d (self-answerability). This label-noise filter is applied identically to
every method we compare. We do not gate on round-trip retrieval consistency: whether the source
passage is retrieved is recorded as a signal and used by the triage. The synthesis is run once per
domain and the pool is shared by all methods and rounds.

(3) Triage.
With a threshold τ (0.75 throughout) each tuple falls into exactly one category (the
tests are applied in this order):
• KNOWN: pcb ≥τ; the student already knows the answer without context.
• SOLVED: pcb < τ, h = 1, pob ≥τ; the reader uses the retrieved evidence correctly.
• CONTEXT-FAILURE: pcb < τ, h = 1, pob < τ; the evidence is in the context but the reader fails
or is unstable.
• RETRIEVAL-FAILURE: pcb < τ, h = 0; the evidence was not retrieved, so no contextual training
can make the question answerable from D.
The category histogram is a diagnostic of the domain gap (Section 5.3).
(4) Routing and training.
Given a budget of B Stage-II examples per round, we fill per-category
quotas of 60%/20%/15%/5% for CONTEXT-FAILURE/RETRIEVAL-FAILURE/SOLVED/KNOWN; an
unfilled quota is topped up from the remaining context failures, then solved items, without dupli-
cates. In our pools the CONTEXT-FAILURE category is smaller than its quota in every domain, so
every context failure is used and the shortfall is filled with SOLVED items (realized shares in Ap-
pendix C). Each category receives a different treatment:
• Contextual SFT for CONTEXT-FAILURE: the target is a given (q, D); when the category exceeds
its quota, tuples are sampled without replacement with probability ∝1 −pob, so the items the
student gets most wrong are preferred.
• Parametric SFT for RETRIEVAL-FAILURE: the target is a given (q, D) where D does not contain
the evidence. The answer is verified against the source passage, so the label is trustworthy, but
the reader must produce it from parametric knowledge and learns that a retrieved context can be
unhelpful—the condition it meets at test time whenever retrieval misses.
• Consolidation for SOLVED and KNOWN: contextual SFT at small shares, plus a fixed replay of
general-domain context-QA examples, to preserve answer formats and general skills.
The mixture is used for one epoch of LoRA fine-tuning Hu et al. (2022); the merged model becomes
Mt+1 and re-probes the pool in the next round.
3.4
A RETRIEVER ROUTE
RETRIEVAL-FAILURE tuples are also natural retriever training data: (q, d) is a positive pair and the
top-ranked retrieved passage is a mined hard negative. In a variant of TriageRAG we fine-tune the
dense retriever on these pairs with a contrastive loss after each round and re-index. Section 5.2
shows that this degrades both retrieval and accuracy, so our main configuration keeps the retriever
frozen.

EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
4.1
TASKS AND DATASETS
We evaluate on 11 datasets from three domains (details in Appendix B). For the medical domain we
use the five datasets of the MIRAGE benchmark Xiong et al. (2024)—PubMedQA Jin et al. (2019),
BioASQ Tsatsaronis et al. (2015), MedQA Jin et al. (2021), MedMCQA Pal et al. (2022) and the
medical subsets of MMLU Hendrycks et al. (2021)—and add the text subset of MedXpertQA Zuo
et al. (2025), a recent expert-level benchmark with up to ten options. For the scientific domain we
use ARC-Challenge Clark et al. (2018), SciQ Welbl et al. (2017) as four-way multiple choice, and
the 14 scientific subsets of MMLU used by SimRAG. For computer science we use the English
multiple-choice (MC) and assertion (AS) questions of CS-Bench Song et al. (2025), which can be

4.2
BASELINES
Because our question is how self-generated data should be used, all Stage-II methods share the
Stage-I model, the synthesized pool, the self-answerability filter, the budget B, the general replay
and the training recipe, and differ only in which examples are selected and how their contexts are
constructed. (1) No domain adaptation: the backbone closed-book, the backbone with RAG, and
the Stage-I model with RAG (equivalent to “SimRAG w/o Stage-II”). (2) Self-training baselines at
equal budget: SimRAG‡, our re-implementation, which keeps tuples whose source passage is among
the top-k (round-trip consistency) and samples them uniformly with retrieved contexts; Random
selection, which samples B tuples uniformly from the full pool with retrieved contexts and isolates
the effect of selection from that of volume; and RAFT-style‡ Zhang et al. (2024), which places the
gold passage among k−1 retrieved distractors for 80% of the examples and uses distractors only for
the remaining 20%. (3) Ablations of TriageRAG (Section 5.2). We do not compare with GPT-4-
distilled readers or with domain LLMs trained on task data overlapping our test sets; all rows use
the same retriever and prompt.
4.3
IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS
Backbones are Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 and Qwen3-8B in non-thinking mode Qwen Team (2025).
All fine-tuning uses LoRA (r=64, α=128, all linear layers), one epoch, learning rate 10−4 with
cosine decay, global batch 64 and maximum length 3,072. The retriever is bge-base-en-v1.5 Xiao
et al. (2024) with a flat FAISS index Johnson et al. (2021) and k=5 for probing, training and evalua-
tion. Synthesis uses 12,000 medical, 8,000 science and all 5,089 CS passages of at least 40 words at
temperature 0.7, yielding pools of 40,540, 32,686 and 20,669 self-answerable tuples. Probing uses
n=4 samples per condition at temperature 0.7 and τ=0.75. The per-round budget is B=12,000 for
medicine and 6,000 for science and CS (30%, 18% and 29% of the respective pools, so that selec-
tion matters), plus 2,000 general replay examples. TriageRAG runs T=3 rounds; SimRAG runs one
round as in the original paper and, as a control, three. Evaluation is zero-shot with greedy decoding;
parse failures count as wrong (Appendix E). One medical round of TriageRAG (4B) takes about 15
minutes of probing with vLLM Kwon et al. (2023), 80 minutes of LoRA training (two A100s) and
10 minutes of evaluation. Prompts are in Appendix G.

5.1
MAIN RESULTS

make four observations.

on average in every domain and the best or second best on 8 of the 11 datasets.

all three cases (87.6 vs. 87.4; 68.2 vs. 68.0; 70.1 vs. 69.8 for Qwen3-8B), random selection does so

5.2
ABLATION STUDIES
Effect of the parametric route.
The single ablation that separates TriageRAG from SimRAG is
the treatment of the RETRIEVAL-FAILURE examples. In Table 4 we keep the triage, the budget and
the frozen retriever, and only replace the parametric route by the “obvious” alternative of inserting
the gold passage into the context of these examples, so that the reader is again trained exclusively on
contexts that contain the evidence. On CS-Bench this variant drops from 68.2 to 61.9, exactly the
level of SimRAG (61.8) and of the Stage-I model (61.9); in the scientific domain it drops from 87.6
to 84.3, below SimRAG (85.7); and in the medical domain from 64.8 to 63.7, again the SimRAG
level (63.7). Figure 2 (bottom) makes the pattern visible across all three domains: mixtures in
which no training context lacks the evidence (SimRAG, the ablation) sit at or below the Stage-I
model; mixtures that contain such contexts (random selection, RAFT-style with 20% distractor-only
examples, TriageRAG with its 20% parametric route) sit within a point of or above the closed-book
model. A reader fine-tuned only on contexts that contain the answer learns to trust the context; in
a domain where the retriever misses often, that reader is worse than no retrieval at all. Round-trip
filtering, introduced in SimRAG to improve label quality, has the side effect of removing precisely
the examples that teach a reader when not to trust its context.
Effect of the triage.
Replacing the triage by uniform selection from the same pool (“w/o triage”)
costs 0.7 points in medicine, 0.6 on CS-Bench and 0.1 in science with the 4B backbone, and 2.3 on
CS-Bench with the 8B backbone; adding the round-trip filter on top (“= SimRAG”) costs a further
0.5, 5.7 and 1.8. In medicine both selections gain alike on the exam-style datasets relative to Stage-
I (three-seed means within a point of each other on MedQA, MedMCQA and MMLU-Med); the
difference between them is PubMedQA, which random selection lowers by 3.5 points on average
(77.6, 81.0, 76.8 vs. 82.0 for Stage-I) and TriageRAG preserves (81.6, 82.6, 82.2; Figure 3).

5.3
STUDY ON THE TRIAGE
What the triage reveals.
Figure 2 and Table 6 (appendix) show the category shares: about 41%
of the self-generated questions in medicine and CS, and 57% in science, are KNOWN: the Stage-I
student answers them closed-book at least three times out of four, so they carry little information,
yet SimRAG spends a proportional part of its budget on them. In CS and science the retriever finds
the evidence for 84–89% of the questions and the student solves most of them; CONTEXT-FAILUREs
are 4–6% of the pool and RETRIEVAL-FAILUREs 7%. In medicine the source passage is retrieved
for only 48% of the questions and 29% of the pool are RETRIEVAL-FAILUREs, so a round-trip
gate discards half of the pool and, more importantly, all the examples in which the context lacks
the answer. Multiple-choice and claim items are overwhelmingly KNOWN; 80–94% of the failures
come from short-span questions, so SimRAG’s format diversity mostly supplies format practice
rather than new knowledge.
Why round-trip filtering hurts.
Figure 2 (bottom) plots each mixture’s accuracy against the share
of its examples whose training context lacks the evidence: zero for SimRAG by construction and
at most 2% for the ablation without the parametric route; the miss rate of the sampled tuples for
random selection (46% in medicine, 16% in science, 10% in CS); 20% for RAFT-style and 21–22%
for TriageRAG. In the two domains where naive retrieval hurts, this share separates the mixtures into
two groups—at most 2% (Stage-I level) and 10% or more (within a point of the closed-book level
or above)—and the ablation falls onto the SimRAG point. The same mechanism fits the per-dataset
pattern (Figure 3): exam questions are the case in which the retrieved passages are topically related
but lack the answer, so the reader must combine them with what it knows.

CONCLUSION
We introduce TriageRAG, a failure-aware self-training framework for adapting retrieval-augmented
LLMs to specialized domains from an unlabeled corpus: it probes the student closed-book and
open-book on every self-generated question, triages it by failure type and routes it to the training
signal that fits. Across 11 datasets in three domains, and at two backbone sizes on CS-Bench, it
consistently outperforms a budget-matched SimRAG, and a single ablation explains why: round-
trip filtering removes every training context that lacks the evidence, and a reader that never sees
such contexts over-trusts noisy retrieval. The triage doubles as a label-free diagnostic of where a
domain gap lies; making retrieval failures useful to the retriever itself remains open.

LIMITATIONS
Our rounds re-probe a pool synthesized once, so the informative examples are consumed early and
accuracy plateaus; regenerating the pool between rounds is future work. Our 4B/8B backbones,
16k-example Stage-I blend and PubMed slice make absolute numbers incomparable with SimRAG’s
8B/27B models on full PubMed, textbooks and Wikipedia, and MedXpertQA is at chance for every
configuration. Headline cells have three seeds and paired intervals; the other cells are single runs,
and CS-Bench differences below one point should be read as ties. The parametric route teaches the
reader to answer, not to abstain, when the context is unhelpful, and our negative retriever-tuning
result covers one recipe (Appendix F expands each point).

STAGE-I DATA
The Stage-I training set is a uniform sample of 16,000 examples from a 20,930-example general
blend that mirrors the three components of SimRAG’s Stage-I data: (1) context-aware QA—SQuAD
Rajpurkar et al. (2016) short-span questions (3,000), BoolQ Clark et al. (2019) yes/no questions
(3,000) and OpenBookQA Mihaylov et al. (2018) multiple-choice questions with the supporting
fact as context (3,000); (2) answer-span generation—SQuAD passages with all their annotated an-
swer spans as the target (2,930); and (3) question generation—SQuAD answer-conditioned question
generation (3,000) and Natural Questions Kwiatkowski et al. (2019) question generation from a
passage without a given answer (6,000). We omit SimRAG’s general instruction-following data be-
cause our backbones are already instruction-tuned. One epoch of LoRA training (250 steps at global
batch 64) took 21 minutes on four A100s for the 4B backbone and 67 minutes on two A100s for
the 8B backbone. The 2,000 general replay examples used in every Stage-II round are context-QA
examples drawn from the same blend.

BASELINE AND SYNTHESIS DETAILS
Synthesis.
Passages with fewer than 40 words are skipped. Candidate answer spans must appear
verbatim in the passage and have at most eight words. Generated questions must end with a question
mark and must not contain the answer string. Multiple-choice distractors are candidate spans from
other passages that do not occur in the source passage, matched on the presence of digits and within
three words of the answer’s length; option order is shuffled. Claims alternate between supported
and refuted per passage. Self-answerability uses greedy decoding with the source passage as the
only context. The pools contain 40,540 (medical; from 12,000 passages), 32,686 (science; 8,000
passages) and 20,669 (CS; 5,089 passages: 11,908 span, 4,997 multiple-choice, 3,764 claim) self-
answerable tuples.
SimRAG‡.
Tuples whose source passage is among the top-5 retrieved passages for the generated
question are kept (48%, 78% and 87% of the medical, science and CS pools); B of them are sampled
uniformly and trained with the retrieved passages as context, plus the 2,000 general replay examples.
Random selection.
B tuples are sampled uniformly from the full pool and trained with the re-
trieved passages as context.
RAFT-style‡.
B tuples are sampled uniformly; for 80% the gold passage replaces one of the
retrieved passages at a random position, for 20% only retrieved passages other than the gold one are
used. Targets are the short answers rather than GPT-4 chain-of-thought answers, so that all methods
share the same label space.
Realized TriageRAG mixture.
With the 60/20/15/5% quotas, the round-1 mixtures contain
2,324 CONTEXT-FAILURE (all of them), 2,400 RETRIEVAL-FAILURE, 6,676 SOLVED and 600
KNOWN tuples in medicine (B=12,000; 19/20/56/5%); 1,960 (all), 1,200, 2,540 and 300 in sci-
ence (33/20/42/5%); and 914 (all), 1,200, 3,586 and 300 on CS (B=6,000; 15/20/60/5%), plus the
2,000 general replay examples in every case. The measured share of training contexts that lack the
evidence is 22.0%, 20.7% and 20.6% (medicine, science, CS) for TriageRAG, 46.2%, 15.7% and
10.3% for random selection, 19.8% and 19.4% for RAFT-style (medicine, CS), and 2.0%, 0.7% and
0.6% for the ablation without the parametric route.
Retriever tuning.
bge-base-en-v1.5 is fine-tuned for one epoch on the round’s RETRIEVAL-
FAILURE pairs (question, source passage) with a multiple-negatives ranking loss over in-batch neg-
atives plus one mined hard negative per pair, the top-ranked retrieved passage other than the source
(batch 64, learning rate 2 × 10−5, maximum length 256); the corpus is then re-embedded and re-
indexed.

ADDITIONAL RESULTS
Effect of rounds.
Figure 4 in Appendix E plots the average accuracy per round (all numbers in
Table 9). Because TriageRAG re-probes the pool with the updated student, the mixture shifts: in the
medical domain the KNOWN share rises from 40.9% to 44.2% and 45.2% over the three rounds and
the RETRIEVAL-FAILURE share falls from 29.2% to 27.1% and 26.8%, i.e. the student has absorbed
part of what it was trained on; on CS-Bench the CONTEXT-FAILURE share falls from 4.4% to 2.7%.
Accuracy, however, moves little after the first round: medicine plateaus (64.8, 64.7, 64.5), science
peaks at round two (87.6, 88.0, 87.5) and so does CS-Bench (68.2, 68.8, 68.3). With a fixed pool the
informative examples are largely consumed in round one; the retriever-tuned variant is the exception
on CS (65.9, 67.7, 68.0), where later rounds partially recover from the damaged index. Iterating
SimRAG lowers medical accuracy (63.7, 63.5, 63.0) and gains at most 1.5 points on CS (61.8, 63.4,
62.8), never approaching TriageRAG. Re-probing is therefore most useful when the pool is refreshed
between rounds.

and Figure 4 plots them for the 4B backbone.
Answer parsing.
Letters are parsed from the first line of the output (patterns A, (A), A., “answer
is A”); yes/no/maybe and true/false are matched as whole words. Unparsable outputs are counted as
wrong. Across all evaluations of fine-tuned models the number of unparsable outputs was at most
3 per dataset; the untuned backbone with retrieval produced 86 unparsable outputs on MedMCQA
and 32 on CS-Bench MC (it sometimes answers with a sentence instead of a letter), which slightly
disadvantages the “+ RAG” rows.

Seeds and intervals.
The headline round-1 cells were replicated with three training seeds and

The parametric route teaches the reader to produce a verified answer
when the context is unhelpful; it does not teach abstention, and we did not measure calibration or
behaviour on unanswerable questions.
Retriever route.
Our negative result covers one reasonable recipe (contrastive fine-tuning of bge-

mixing with the original training data may reverse it.
