Abstract
Dense retrievers provide grounded evidence
that mitigates hallucinations in outputs from
Large Language Models (LLMs). Real-world
queries are often verbose, ambiguously framed,
or require multi-step reasoning, introducing ir-
relevant details that weaken semantic matching
for dense retrievers. Query rewriting is a widely
adopted pre-retrieval technique that leverages
LLMs to reformulate queries and bridge the
semantic gap between such queries and the re-
trieval target. Existing rewriting approaches
either rely on expensive proprietary models,
substantial training data, or iterative retrieval
and rewriting, while largely applying a uniform
rewriting strategy regardless of the retrieval cor-
pus. We observe that effective query rewrit-
ing instructions are not universal: the optimal
strategy depends on the retrieval target, rather
than the model alone. We introduce SkillQR, a
query rewriting framework that automatically
discovers reasoning strategy sets from the re-
trieval target and dynamically routes a query to
the appropriate rewriting strategy at inference
time. Without relying on training data, SkillQR
achieves competitive performance compared to
fine-tuned rewriting models on the BRIGHT
and R2MED benchmarks, while also providing
lower inference latency than iterative rewriting
approaches.

Introduction
Retrieval is a core component of modern search
and retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) sys-
tems (Karpukhin et al., 2020; Su et al., 2023; Wang
et al., 2024b; Sengupta et al., 2026). Retrieval qual-
ity directly determines the evidence available for
downstream generation (Lewis et al., 2020; Xu
et al., 2024, 2025).
In real-world applications,
however, user queries rarely appear in a retrieval-
friendly form.
Instead, they are often indirect,
ambiguously framed, and expressed through sur-
face context rather than the underlying information
need (Asai et al., 2023). Such queries may contain
irrelevant details or omit critical structure, which
hinders effective retrieval. This challenge is es-
pecially pronounced in reason-intensive retrieval,
where successful retrieval requires inferring the la-
tent reasoning that connects the query to relevant
evidence (Chen et al., 2025; Su et al., 2025; Shao
et al., 2025). As a result, even strong dense retriev-
ers can fail when the surface form of the query does
not align with the retrieval target.
Query rewriting (Wang et al., 2023; Yao et al.,
2025; Ma et al., 2023) is a natural approach to
this problem. The key idea is to transform the
original query into a form that better reflects the
information needed for retrieval, such as implicit
concepts, intermediate reasoning steps, or struc-
tural constraints. Recent work shows that such
reformulation can substantially improve retrieval
for complex and reasoning-intensive queries, mak-
ing rewriting a practical interface between users
and retrievers (Qin et al., 2025; Zhong et al., 2025;
Lai et al., 2025). Despite these advantages, existing
query rewriting systems remain limited in both effi-
ciency and adaptability. Many approaches rely on
expensive proprietary LLMs or on reinforcement
learning (RL)-based training with substantial cu-
rated data (Chen et al., 2025; Qin et al., 2025).
Others depend on iterative retrieval-and-rewrite
procedures or repeated corpus interaction, which
further increases inference cost and latency (Lei
et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025a). As a result, current
methods can be costly to build, difficult to trans-
fer across corpora, and impractical to deploy in
privacy-sensitive or resource-constrained settings.
Beyond these practical limitations, existing ap-
proaches largely keep the rewriting strategy fixed
across retrieval settings, such as an instruction to
"generate relevant knowledge for the query", with
improvements coming mainly from stronger mod-
els or additional training. We argue that this is
fundamentally incomplete: the optimal rewrite de-

pends not only on the query, but also on the target
corpus. For example, the query "how to imple-
ment binary search" requires fundamentally differ-
ent rewrites when targeting theoretical computer
science papers versus Python Stack Overflow an-
swers. However, this dependence on the retrieval
target is largely overlooked in prior work. This
motivates a corpus-aware view of query rewriting,
in which the rewriting strategy should adapt to
the corpus rather than remain fixed.
Together, these challenges highlight the need
for a lightweight and adaptive query rewriting
framework that improves retrieval through better
strategy design rather than heavier models or iter-
ative procedures. Motivated by this perspective,
we propose SkillQR, a training-free query rewrit-
ing framework that automatically discovers corpus-
grounded reasoning strategies and dynamically ap-
plies them at inference time. SkillQR extracts a
compact set of rewriting strategies from the target
corpus and routes each query to the appropriate
strategy, enabling rewriting that is aligned with the
retrieval target. Our contributions are summarized
as follows:
• We show that the rewriting strategy impacts
retrieval performance, and that it should be
conditioned on the retrieval target.
• We propose SkillQR, a corpus-guided query
rewriting framework that automatically dis-
covers reusable rewriting skills without requir-
ing relevance labels or model fine-tuning. 1
• We evaluate SkillQR against existing rewrit-
ing methods on two reason-intensive retrieval
benchmarks, BRIGHT and R2MED, demon-
strating performance comparable to previous
methods using fewer resources.

Related Work
2.1
Query Rewriting for Reason-Intensive IR
The BRIGHT benchmark (Su et al., 2025) eval-
uates reasoning-intensive retrieval across twelve
subjects, ranging from programming to natural sci-
ences. It shows that even state-of-the-art dense
retrievers remain insufficient when the reasoning
gap is not addressed. R2MED (Li et al., 2025b)
is a similar benchmark focusing on the medical
1Our source code will be made publicly available upon

domain content. Current research addresses reason-
intensive retrieval in three ways: (1) Reasoning-
aware retrievers, such as DIVER (Long et al.,
2025), Reason-Embed (Chen et al., 2025), and Rea-
sonIR (Shao et al., 2025), which utilize LLMs to
generate hard negatives for contrastive learning,
training retrievers to inherently bridge the reason-
ing gap; and (2) Reranking, where specialized rank-
ing models refine the relevance order of retrieved
outputs (Song et al., 2025b; Ji et al., 2024). (3) Our
work focuses on query rewriting as a complemen-
tary approach that can be combined with advances
in both dense retrieval and reranking. Representa-
tive methods include TongSearch-QR (Qin et al.,
2025) and Reasoner-Rewriter (Chen et al., 2025),
which use reinforcement learning or specialized
fine-tuning to produce retrieval-effective rewrites.
Existing query rewriting systems suffer from sev-
eral limitations. The basic approach relies on ex-
pensive proprietary models such as GPT-4o (Su
et al., 2025), which hinders long-term deployment
and raises privacy concerns when sensitive queries
must be sent to third-party services. Training a
customized query rewriter for complex query un-
derstanding is the predominant approach (Qin et al.,
2025; Chen et al., 2025; Zhong et al., 2025). While
more cost-effective at inference time, these ap-
proaches demand substantial training resources
and carefully curated datasets, often limiting their
transferability across domains. Lastly, training-
free methods such as ThinkQE (Lei et al., 2025)
employ pseudo-relevance feedback (PRF) by itera-
tively retrieving and adding top-ranked documents
in LLM prompts to rewrite queries. This process in-
creases online rewriting latency, as both the prompt
length and generated output grow with each itera-
tion, while also relying on strong reasoning LLMs,
making it less practical in resource-constrained de-
ployment settings.

with subsequent work exploring skill refinement

et al., 2025) and recurring user requirements (Yang
et al., 2026). Together, these works highlight skills
as a lightweight and flexible approach to agent
adaptation that can reduce reliance on manually

3.4
Inference-Time Rewrite Generation
At inference time, rewrite agent WLLM first routes
the query by selecting the most appropriate skill
s∗∈Sf for query q by evaluating the match be-
tween the query’s domain and each skill in Sf. The
selected skill is then used to prompt the rewriter
WLLM for query expansion. We provide the full
routing and generation prompt in Appendix A.4.
We first perform an initial retrieval using the
original query q with the dense retriever R, obtain-
ing a set of top-ranked documents DR(q). These
documents are used as pseudo-relevance feedback
(PRF) for the rewriter. Conditioned on the selected
skill s∗and the PRF documents DR(q), the rewriter
WLLM generates an expanded query guided by skill
s∗. This process aims to articulate the latent reason-
ing trajectory T connecting the query to relevant
documents. The final query is constructed as:
q∗= concat(q, WLLM(q|DR(q), π(s∗)))
where π(s∗) denotes the prompt instantiated with
the selected skill.

Experiments
4.1
Datasets and Baselines
We evaluate SkillQR on the BRIGHT (Su et al.,
2025) and R2MED (Li et al., 2025b) benchmarks,
reporting averaged and per-subject NDCG@10.
We compare against the following baselines: (1)
ThinkQE (Lei et al., 2025) is an answer-driven
query rewriting method built on QWEN-R1-Distill-
14B (DeepSeek-AI, 2025), a foundation model
with enhanced thinking ability. It iteratively al-
ternates between retrieval and rewriting for three
rounds. At each rewriting step, ThinkQE uses the
top-5 retrieved documents as PRF to generate the
answer for the query. Following the upgrade in
Diver-QExpand (Long et al., 2025), we replace
ThinkQE’s original BM25 retriever with the dense
retriever that the rewriting system supports to ob-
tain the PRF documents. (2) TongSearch-QR (Qin
et al., 2025), a Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct-based (Yang
et al., 2024) query rewriter finetuned with RL on
30k curated data samples. (3) Reasoner-Rewriter
(RRewriter) (Chen et al., 2025), a Qwen-based
rewriter also finetuned with RL, but on 68k samples
curated directly from BRIGHT documents.
To assess generalization, we evaluate all query
rewriting methods on two retrievers:
Diver-
Retriever (Long et al., 2025) and Reason-
Embed (Chen et al., 2025), both of which are
leading dense retrievers on the reason-intensive
retrieval tasks. Model and prompt details appear in
Appendix A.
4.2
SkillQR Implementation Details
We employ QWEN-R1-Distill-14B (DeepSeek-AI,
2025) to create proposer and judge agents for all
offline skill discovery processes. Skills are discov-
ered from embeddings generated by the system’s re-
triever (e.g., Reason-Embed or Diver-Retriever). In
SkillQR, we apply MiniBatchKMeans (Pedregosa
et al., 2011) to partition each subject-specific re-
trieval corpus into K clusters, where K is dynam-
ically set to approximately the square root of the
corpus size. We then discover skills using at most
M = 3 refinement rounds and at most NS = 5
skills. We set 3 rounds to bound offline compute
cost, and cap 5 skills to ensure the skill set remains
compact so routing errors reflect skill quality rather
than context overload in the router. We evaluate
sensitivity to the cluster count K and skill cap NS
in Appendix C.1. Skill discovery time is a one-
time offline cost. The full BRIGHT benchmark is
mined in approximately 2 hours on a single H100
GPU, with the largest subject LeetCode (400k doc-
uments) completing in 21 minutes (Appendix B).
At inference time, SkillQR uses a single LLM,
either Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct or QWEN-R1-Distill-
14B, for both skill routing and query rewriting,
requiring no additional model components beyond
the chosen backbone. As in ThinkQE, we use the
top-5 documents retrieved with the original query
as PRF for query rewriting.

4.4
R2MED for Performance Transferability
Table 4 reports SkillQR’s performance on R2MED,
evaluating its transferability to the medical domain.
When using the 14B thinking-enhanced model
backbone, SkillQR achieves statistically compa-
rable performance on both retrievers with less in-
ference cost, reaching 48.9 and 49.3 NDCG@10
on Diver and Reason-Embed, respectively, against
ThinkQE’s 49.3 and 49.1. This confirms that Skil-
lQR’s efficiency advantage on BRIGHT transfers
to the medical domain without sacrificing retrieval
quality, indicating that applying corpus-grounded
skills can substitute for iterative refinement even
when the corpus distribution shifts substantially.
At 7B-scale, SkillQR achieves statistically
significant improvements over TongSearch and
RRewriter on two retrievers, achieving a 46.9
NDCG@10 with Diver against RRewriter’s 44.9.

Discussions
5.1
Skill Effectiveness
Table 5 investigates the quality of automatically
discovered skills. We remove the PRF documents
from the rewriter’s input to better isolate the effect
of the skill applied. On average, SkillQR outper-
forms the generic rewriting strategy, yielding a 1.6
gain and matching the human-designed skills. This
confirms that the discovered skills are effective and
may reduce the need for manual, holistic corpus
inspection. Notably, the human-designed skills
default to the generic instruction for all StackEx-
change subjects, implicitly assuming that domain-
specific strategies are unnecessary for these topics.
SkillQR discovers distinct strategies across all sub-
jects, and the resulting improvements on subsets
such as Rob (+1.9) suggest that the human’s as-
sumption is incorrect. This demonstrates that Skil-
lQR can uncover strategies overlooked by human
experts. The skill effectiveness also transfers to the
Reason-Embed retriever in Appendix C.3.

6
Conclusion
We propose SkillQR, a training-free query rewrit-
ing framework that selects rewriting strategies from
a skill set to align queries with corpus characteris-
tics, achieving performance comparable to strong
learned baselines while maintaining a favorable effi-
ciency–effectiveness trade-off. SkillQR highlights
the retrieval corpus as an overlooked source of
8


rewriting signal, beyond stronger models or larger
training sets. Our findings motivate corpus-aware
strategy design for reason-intensive retrieval.
Limitations
While SkillQR achieves competitive performance
with strong efficiency, there are limitations. Skil-
lQR relies on LLM-based proposer, judge, and
rewriter agents, whose reasoning capabilities may
be affected by pretraining biases, limitations in do-
main knowledge, and hallucinations due to long
context. Our skill router analysis also shows a gap
between the LLM router and the theoretical upper
bound, suggesting that better query-to-skill match-
ing could yield further gains in reason-intensive
retrieval. Future versions of SkillQR may incorpo-
rate richer routing strategies and a dynamic skill
refresh design for an evolving corpus. These im-
provements will contribute to a stronger system for
reason-intensive retrieval.

To determine if aliasing occurs when recording a sound wave with a

According to this theorem, the sampling rate must be at least twice the
highest frequency component in the signal to avoid aliasing. In this case,
the highest frequency is 1000 Hz, and the sampling rate is 2000 samples

Therefore, no aliasing will occur in this scenario. To further understand the

To address the given mathematics question, the fundamental concepts to
consider are: 1. **Sampling Theorem**: This theorem states that a
continuous-time signal can be perfectly reconstructed from its samples if the
sampling rate is at least twice the highest frequency component of the signal.

Frequency**: The Nyquist frequency is half of the sampling rate. It is the

undersampled, meaning the sampling rate is less than the Nyquist rate. In
this case, high-frequency components of the signal are incorrectly
represented as lower frequency components, leading to distortion in the
reconstructed signal. 4. **Relationship Between Frequency and Sampling

rate of 2000 samples per second, the Nyquist frequency is 1000 Hz. Since

occur in this scenario.
