GLOBE: Evolving Structured Agentic Memory for E-commerce Product
Optimization in Generative Search
Hyunseo Kim, Suil Choe, Sukkyoung So, Daeyeon Yi
AI Lab, CTO Division, LG Electronics, South Korea
{hyunseo00.kim, suil.choe, sukkyoung.so, daeyeon.yi}@lge.com
Abstract
Optimizing product listings for generative
search requires language agents not only to
rewrite effectively but also to continually
self-improve by retaining successful strategies
across diverse categories. However, existing
approaches often treat structured listings as
free-form text, leaving effective strategies im-
plicit in prompts, search trajectories, or rewrit-
ten outputs rather than accumulating them as a
reusable agentic memory. To address this limi-
tation, we introduce GLOBE, an agentic frame-
work that drives continual self-improvement
by storing optimization knowledge in an evolv-
ing handbook of explicit, field-scoped behav-
iors. In each episode, an Editor applies relevant
behaviors and receives rank-based feedback,
which a Reflector converts into behavior up-
dates. Crucially for dependable execution in
real-world settings, a Moderator removes re-
dundant proposals, a Curator commits accepted
changes, and consistently harmful behaviors
are deactivated. This cycle transforms episodic
feedback into a persistent, field-scoped opti-
mization memory. Experiments show that struc-
tured behaviors outperform monolithic prompt
optimization, while feedback-driven evolution
and moderation further improve effectiveness
and reduce downside risk. The learned behav-
iors also remain effective on unseen product
categories, suggesting that agentic memory evo-
lution supports interpretable and transferable
product optimization.

Introduction
Language agents increasingly improve themselves
not by updating weights, but by accumulating ex-
perience in an external memory that is continu-
ally refined through feedback (Shinn et al., 2023;
Wang et al., 2023). Building on these advances,
agent-centric frameworks are expanding into e-
commerce, as large language models (LLMs) shift
search toward generative engines that directly com-
pare and rank products by reasoning over rich tex-
tual contexts (Li et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2023; Hou
et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2025b). In this environ-
ment, an agent optimizing a product listing must
shift from conventional search engine optimization
methods (Davis, 2006; Gudivada et al., 2015) to
strategic content structuring, as its success heavily
depends on how favorably the downstream genera-
tive engine interprets the presented information.
A key challenge in this real-world setting lies
in the structured nature of e-commerce product
data. Product listings are not arbitrary free-form
documents but consist of distinct fields, such as
titles, feature bullets, and descriptions, each serv-
ing a different functional role. Nevertheless, prior
methods commonly optimize a listing through a sin-
gle prompt that rewrites the document as a whole
(Bagga et al., 2025; Yuan et al., 2026). This mono-
lithic approach lacks structural boundaries, treating
optimization as unstructured text generation. As
illustrated in Figure 1, relying on generic prompt
optimization leads to critical structural errors, be-
cause the agent cannot restrict specific editing op-
erations to their appropriate target fields.
Beyond this lack of structural control, current
approaches fail to generalize successful strategies
across multiple optimization episodes. Even if
a specific rewrite tactic successfully improves a
product’s rank in one category, monolithic prompt
optimization cannot abstract this success into a
reusable principle. As further demonstrated in Fig-
ure 1, when a generic prompt attempts to transfer
a durability strategy to new categories, it fails to
adapt the context, forcing overfitted keywords (e.g.,
applying "rubber insulation" to leather boots) rather
than grounding the edit in existing product facts.
Consequently, effective strategies must be repeat-
edly rediscovered, making optimization dependent
on fragmented and costly trial and error. Product
optimization therefore requires an explicit agentic
memory mechanism that transforms transient rank-
ing outcomes into persistent, generalizable editing

To address these limitations, we introduce
GLOBE (GeneraLizable Optimization Behavior
Evolution), an agentic framework that represents
product optimization knowledge as a structured be-
havior handbook. Rather than relying on a single
unconstrained rewrite instruction, GLOBE decom-
poses optimization into explicit and reusable edit-
ing behaviors. Each behavior specifies a concrete
editing operation, the listing fields in which the op-
eration may be applied, and metadata summarizing
its historical ranking outcomes. For example, a be-
havior may instruct the agent to specify the material
of a likely wear point, strictly limiting this addi-
tion to the feature field. This formulation separates
what should be changed from where the change
is permitted, providing a more interpretable and
structurally bounded interface for product-listing
optimization.
GLOBE further transforms transient ranking
feedback into persistent optimization knowledge
through an iterative deployment-and-evolution cy-
cle. The current handbook is repeatedly applied to
batches of query-product pairs, producing episode-
level feedback from the resulting rank changes. Re-
curring success and failure patterns are then used
to refine the handbook, after which the updated
behaviors are redeployed in subsequent batches.
By repeatedly alternating between handbook de-
ployment and feedback-driven refinement, GLOBE
converts episode-specific ranking outcomes into
persistent and reusable optimization knowledge.
This formulation provides two complementary
advantages. First, field-scoped behaviors explicitly
specify both the editing operation and the product
fields in which it may be applied, making individual
strategies more structured and inspectable. Second,
the evolving handbook preserves and reuses knowl-
edge acquired from past ranking episodes rather
than discarding it after each rewrite. We therefore
investigate whether field-scoped behavior repre-
sentations improve ranking effectiveness, whether
feedback-driven handbook evolution reduces harm-
ful outcomes, and whether the learned optimization
knowledge transfers to unseen product categories.

• We propose GLOBE, an agentic product-

2
Related Work
2.1
Generative Engine Optimization for
E-commerce
Generative Engine Optimization (GEO) aims to
improve the visibility of target items in genera-
tive search by modifying their textual representa-


tions. Initial approaches showed that strategically
injected text sequences can influence item expo-
sure and output rankings (Kumar and Lakkaraju,
2024; Jin et al., 2026). Subsequent prompt-based
methods formulate GEO as document rewriting,
using LLMs to modify content according to the
inferred preferences of a target generative en-
gine (Bagga et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025). Recent
agentic frameworks further automate this process
by iteratively searching for effective document-
modification strategies (Yuan et al., 2026). De-
spite this progression, existing approaches largely
optimize documents through free-form, document-
level generation, offering limited control over indi-
vidual fields of structured product listings. More-
over, effective strategies typically remain embed-
ded in prompts, search trajectories, or rewritten
outputs rather than being retained as explicit and
reusable knowledge.
2.2
Feedback-Driven Knowledge Evolution
Effective optimization requires systems to not only
generate high-quality outputs but also learn from
past successes and failures to avoid repeatedly re-
discovering the same strategies. Driven by this
need, a growing line of research studies how feed-
back can be transformed from a transient correc-
tion signal into reusable knowledge (Kim et al.,
2025a). Early self-refinement methods use self-
or peer-generated critiques to revise the current
output (Madaan et al., 2023; Schick et al., 2022).
Cross-episode approaches go beyond local revi-
sion by abstracting successful and failed trajec-
tories into reusable reflections or lessons (Shinn
et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2024). At the system
level, automatic prompt optimizers use natural-
language critiques, textual feedback, or trajectory-
level reflection to revise the instructions governing
future executions (Pryzant et al., 2023; Yuksek-
gonul et al., 2024; Agrawal et al., 2026). Recent
memory-centric systems further externalize such
abstractions into persistent handbooks, evolving
playbooks, or learned memory architectures (Di-
dolkar et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025; Xiong et al.,
2026). Together, these works demonstrate a pro-
gression from local output correction to persistent
knowledge evolution, but largely focus on open-
ended reasoning, coding, or planning. In contrast,
structured product optimization requires reusable
knowledge to be field-scoped and learned from in-
direct, relative ranking feedback.
3
Proposed Method: GLOBE
GLOBE maintains a structured agentic memory of
reusable product-optimization behaviors through
an iterative deployment-and-evolution cycle, illus-
trated in Figure 2.
At iteration n, the current
handbook Hn is deployed across a batch of K
query-product pairs. Each deployment produces
an episode that records the rewritten listing, the
behaviors applied by the editor, and the resulting
rank change. After the batch is completed, GLOBE
aggregates these episodes to refine the handbook,
producing Hn+1. The updated handbook is then de-
ployed in the next iteration, allowing optimization
knowledge from individual ranking outcomes to
accumulate as explicit, reusable editing behaviors.

indicates a higher position.
Unlike free-form documents, each product p is
represented as a structured listing consisting of

3.3
Handbook Evolution
After collecting all K episodes under the current
handbook Hn, GLOBE aggregates their ranking
outcomes and updates the handbook at the batch
level. For each episode, the h+, h−, or h0 count
of every applied behavior is incremented depend-
ing on whether the target product’s rank improves,
degrades, or remains unchanged, respectively. Be-
cause the individual contribution of each behavior
cannot be isolated, we assign the episode’s over-
all directional outcome to all applied behaviors.
The accumulated statistics are then used to recom-
pute each behavior’s score and active status for the
next iteration. Using the aggregated episode logs
and updated behavior statistics, GLOBE evolves the
handbook through three components: 1) Reflector,
2) Moderator, and 3) Curator.
Reflector.
The Reflector analyzes recent episode
logs together with the current handbook to identify
recurring success and failure patterns. Rather than
rewriting the handbook as unrestricted text, it pro-
poses structured delta operations that either add a
new behavior or modify an existing one. An ADD
operation is proposed when the observed pattern is
not adequately covered by the current handbook. A
MODIFY operation refines an existing behavior by
clarifying its instruction, application condition, or
permitted target fields. The Reflector does not di-
rectly delete behaviors, since behaviors associated
with consistently harmful outcomes are excluded
through the active-status criterion while remaining
stored for traceability.
Moderator.
The Moderator filters Reflector pro-
posals before they are committed to the handbook.
It performs (1) embedding-based deduplication
to reject proposals that are semantically too similar
to existing active behaviors. A proposed behavior

retained, since unrestricted rewriting of committed
behaviors would make the handbook fluctuate with
the noise of individual batches.
Curator.
The Curator deterministically com-
mits the proposals accepted by the Moderator.
New behaviors receive stable identifiers and zero-
initialized feedback metadata, while modified be-
haviors are updated in place, advancing their ver-
sion but retaining their accumulated metadata. The
Curator then constructs the next handbook Hn+1
and determines its active behavior pool using the
accumulated feedback statistics. The updated hand-
book is applied to the next batch of query-product
pairs, thereby repeating the rewriting, feedback col-
lection, and handbook evolution cycle.
3.4
Training and Inference
Starting from a seed handbook H1 containing
nine general GEO heuristics, GLOBE repeats the
deployment-and-evolution cycle for N training
batches. The handbook and its active behavior
pool remain fixed within each batch. Feedback col-
lected during the batch affects behavior status and
handbook contents only in the subsequent iteration,
ensuring that all episodes in the same batch are
generated under a consistent handbook version. Af-
ter the final training batch, the handbook is frozen.
At test time, the active behaviors from the frozen
handbook are supplied to the editor using the same
deployment procedure as during training. The ed-
itor selects and applies behaviors for each query-
product pair, but no feedback statistics or handbook
contents are updated during evaluation.

Experimental Setup
Dataset.
We evaluate GLOBE using the E-
GEO benchmark (Bagga et al., 2025), which
pairs real-world shopping queries from Reddit
(r/BuyItForLife) with structured Amazon product
listings. We categorize the products into six macro-
categories: Home & Kitchen, Fashion & Acces-
sories, Electronics, Tools & DIY, Sports & Out-
doors, and Health & Beauty. We use 1,000 training
queries to construct the behavior handbook and
a disjoint, category-balanced set of 900 queries
(150 per category) for evaluation. The handbook
remains frozen during testing.

Evaluation Metrics.
We evaluate system per-
formance using four metrics. Mean Rank Gain
(MRG) and Success Rate (SR) measure the av-
erage position improvement and the frequency of
successful optimizations, respectively. To evalu-
ate stability, we evaluate Downside Risk (DSR),
which applies a quadratic penalty to rank degrada-
tions to capture the severity of harmful rewrites.
Finally, Category Variance (CV) measures the
variance of MRG across the six categories, where
a lower score indicates generalized performance.

4.2
Effectiveness of the Behavior Handbook
To evaluate the effectiveness of our proposed hand-
book framework, we examine the contribution of its
core design components to the overall performance
of GLOBE. As shown in Table 1, the Static HB
already achieves a substantial improvement over
monolithic prompt optimization in both ranking
effectiveness and downside risk. Because this base-
line uses only the initial generic behaviors with-
out feedback-based updates, its improvement sug-
gests that decomposing optimization knowledge
into explicit operations, together with field scoping,
provides a stronger foundation than the evaluated
monolithic prompt baseline. This result further
shows that structured behavior representation con-
tributes substantially to GLOBE’s performance even
before feedback-driven evolution is introduced.
Feedback-based evolution further improves
MRG and SR by extending the handbook beyond
its initial generic heuristics. However, the unmod-
erated variant also produces a larger handbook
and higher category variance, suggesting that un-
restricted accumulation may introduce redundant
or narrowly specialized behaviors. The increase in
handbook capacity also means that its improvement
cannot be attributed entirely to feedback-based
learning. Evolution expands the available op-
timization knowledge, but accumulating more
behaviors does not necessarily produce a more
consistent handbook.
The full GLOBE addresses this limitation by
filtering semantically redundant and functionally
overlapping proposals before they are committed.
Despite retaining fewer behaviors than the unmod-
erated variant, it achieves stronger overall perfor-
mance together with lower DSR and CV. This pat-
tern suggests that the Moderator acts as handbook-
level regularization, improving the composition of
the accumulated knowledge rather than simply ex-
panding its size. Overall, moderated evolution
produces a more compact handbook with a bet-

4.3
Impact of Feedback-driven Refinement
To examine whether iterative handbook evolution
produces sustained improvements beyond the final
aggregate results, we trace ranking performance as
the number of training examples increases.
As shown in Figure 3, MRG improves rapidly
in the early stages of training, as feedback from
the initial batches reveals optimization patterns not
covered by the seed handbook, and then plateaus
as additional batches are processed. This trajec-
tory indicates that feedback-based evolution pro-
gressively enriches the handbook, but its ben-
efits exhibit diminishing returns once broadly
useful behaviors have been acquired. DSR also
declines overall, though not monotonically, indi-
cating that evolution gradually mitigates harmful
ranking outcomes. Together, these patterns sug-
gest that once frequently occurring optimization
patterns have been observed, further improvement
depends less on training-set size than on broader
coverage of query intents and product contexts
that provide genuinely new feedback.
Additionally, to test whether refinement im-
proves the specific behaviors it targets, rather than
merely coinciding with handbook growth, we com-
pare each modified behavior’s episode-level out-
comes before and after modification (Appendix C).
Among the nine under-performing behaviors that
were modified, eight showed improvements beyond
a matched mean-reversion control (sign test, p =
0.020), with an average adjusted increase of 5.3
percentage points in helpful rate. In contrast, modi-
fying behaviors that were already performing above
average yields no measurable gain. These results
suggest that MODIFY operations act as targeted

4.4
Cross-Category Generalizability
To examine whether the evolved handbook captures
transferable optimization knowledge rather than
category-specific heuristics, we conduct a leave-
one-category-out (LOCO) evaluation. Specifically,
for each macro-category, we evolve a separate hand-
book using only the training queries from the re-
maining five categories. Figure 4 compares the
MRG improvement over the seed handbook for the
LOCO models and the fully trained GLOBE model.
The result confirms that the benefits of feedback-
driven evolution are not strictly bound to domain-
specific exposure. The LOCO handbook consis-
tently retains a substantial portion of the aggregate
improvements seen in the fully trained model. Fur-
thermore, the LOCO approach even surpasses full
training in some categories. These category-level
differences suggest that training on all categories
can occasionally hurt a specific category through
cross-category interference, which we leave for
future analysis. Nevertheless, they show that in-
category exposure is not a strict prerequisite for
generating effective rewrites. Together with the
modest overall MRG decrease of 0.07, these re-
sults suggest that feedback-driven evolution ex-
tracts transferable optimization strategies that
remain effective without target-category expo-
sure, avoiding category-specific memorization.

5
Conclusion
We introduced GLOBE, a framework that main-
tains product-optimization knowledge as explicit,
field-scoped behaviors in an evolving handbook.
By converting ranking feedback into persistent and
reusable editing knowledge, GLOBE enables effec-
tive and interpretable optimization while transfer-
ring to unseen product categories. More broadly,
our findings suggest that evolving structured behav-
iors offers a practical path toward interpretable and
transferable agentic memory for generative search.
Limitations
A key limitation is that the editor, ranker, Reflec-
tor, and Moderator are all instantiated from the
same model (GPT-5.4). Because the ranker that pro-
vides the reward signal shares a model family with
the components optimized against it, the learned
behaviors may partly capture ranker-specific pref-
erences rather than ranker-agnostic optimization
knowledge. We probe this with a cross-ranker eval-
uation in Section 4.5; fully disentangling transfer-
able knowledge from ranker-specific artifacts re-
mains future work. In addition, credit assignment
over behaviors is approximate. Since each episode
yields a single rank change for the entire rewrite,
the episode’s directional outcome is attributed to all
applied behaviors rather than to each behavior’s in-
dividual contribution (Section 3.3). A useful behav-
ior can thus absorb harmful counts from co-applied
behaviors. These counts also rely on the editor’s
self-reported list of applied behaviors, which is not
verified against the actual edits; an audit of 50 train-
ing episodes judged by a separate LLM (Claude
Opus 5) found 88.3% of reported behaviors consis-
tent with the edits made and few applied behaviors
left unreported. Batch-level aggregation and the
probation period mitigate this attribution noise but
do not eliminate it. More precise attribution, such
as per-behavior counterfactual rewrites, would in-
cur substantially higher ranking costs per episode,
and we leave its efficient integration into the evolu-
tion loop to future work. Moreover, because modi-
fied behaviors inherit their accumulated feedback
counts, the active-status criterion reacts slowly to
a harmful MODIFY, and GLOBE currently has no
mechanism to revert such a revision. Beyond these
signal-level limitations, while establishing a rigor-
ous performance ceiling with high-end models ne-
cessitates substantial computational overhead dur-
ing the multi-agent learning phase, our inference
stage remains highly efficient; the compiled hand-
book acts as a static set of rules that applies verified
behaviors in a single pass without iterative opti-
mization loops. Furthermore, distilling these struc-
tured behaviors into smaller, open-source models
via fine-tuning represents a promising direction to
drastically reduce future deployment costs. Finally,
as real-world e-commerce search algorithms are
dynamic and continuously updated, accumulated
editing behaviors may experience temporal degra-
dation and require periodic re-evolution. Extending
our current text-only, field-scoped constraints to
multimodal settings also remains an open avenue
for future research.
Ethical Considerations
Factuality and Consumer Protection.
Meth-
ods for improving product visibility in generative
search may be used to unfairly influence rankings,
and rank improvement should not be interpreted
as evidence of superior product quality. To re-
duce misleading edits, GLOBE includes a factual-
grounding behavior that requires rewritten claims
to remain supported by the original listing. Never-
theless, language models may still introduce unsup-
ported implications or overstate product benefits.
In practical deployments, rewritten listings should
therefore be verified against the original listing and
monitored for deceptive or exaggerated claims.
Model Bias and Accessibility.
The ranking feed-
back used by GLOBE may reflect model-specific
preferences rather than general shopper prefer-
ences. We partially examine this risk through cross-
ranker evaluation, but it cannot be fully eliminated.
In addition, evolving the handbook with high-
capacity language models may favor organizations
with greater computational resources. Reusing a
frozen handbook and adapting it to smaller models
may help reduce this barrier.

Experimental Details
B.1
Dataset and Evaluation Setup
Every method optimizes the same target product
in the same competitive context, and is scored
against the same reference ranking. Each E-GEO
query (Bagga et al., 2025) comes with a candi-
date set of ten Amazon listings, each serialized
into the title, features, and description fields. We
sample one candidate uniformly at random as the
target product, store that index once, and reuse it
for GLOBE, its ablations, and all baselines; the pre-
rewrite ranking rM(q, P, pt) is likewise computed
once and cached. This shared setup is intended to
isolate differences arising from the rewriting strat-
egy.
We use 1,000 queries for handbook construction,
processed as ten batches of 100 pairs, and a dis-
joint set of 900 queries for evaluation. Category
labels are not provided by the benchmark at the
query level. We first use GPT-5.4-nano to construct
a mapping table from the unique Amazon main
category values in the dataset to the six macro-
categories. This mapping table is applied determin-
istically to every candidate product. Each query
is assigned the majority macro-category among
the candidates in its product set. The evaluation
set is built by sampling 150 queries per macro-
category. An episode is discarded when the edi-
tor returns no usable rewrite or when the ranker’s
output cannot be parsed into a complete permu-
tation of the ten candidates after all retries; such
failures are excluded rather than imputed, since
scoring them as zero gain would reward methods
that fail more often. This leaves 887 of the 900
episodes for GLOBE, with 146 to 149 episodes per
category, and comparable counts for the baselines.
GLOBE uses no validation set, as the handbook
is updated directly from training-batch feedback;
only the prompt-optimization baseline needs one,
selecting its final prompt on 100 held-out validation
queries evaluated after each epoch.

B.3
Implementation and Hyperparameters
All LLM components, Editor, Ranker, Reflector,
and the functional-deduplication stage of the Mod-
erator use the gpt-5.4-global Azure deployment
(which corresponds to the standard, publicly avail-
able GPT-5.4 model), with temperature set per role:
0.5 for the Editor, 0.3 for the Ranker, 0.7 for the
Reflector so that it explores diverse revisions, and
0.0 for the Moderator so that redundancy decisions
are near-deterministic. Calls are capped at 5,000
completion tokens, retried up to five times, and
issued with at most 30 concurrent requests. The ed-
itor receives the complete active behavior set as its
strategy pool rather than a retrieved subset, and the
handbook is frozen for the duration of a batch, so
all 100 episodes of a batch run under one handbook
version.
A behavior stays active while its score is non-
negative or while it is still within a probation period
of Nmin = 5 applications. Probation is needed be-
cause attribution is approximate: every behavior
reported as applied in an episode receives the same
directional feedback, so a useful behavior can ab-
sorb harmful counts from failures it did not cause.
For embedding deduplication, we set the cosine-
similarity threshold to τ = 0.9. This value bal-
ances diversity and compactness: lower values ag-
gressively filter nuanced behaviors, while higher
values allow semantic redundancy to persist.
B.4
Seed Handbook
Table 5 lists the complete seed handbook H1 from
which every GLOBE run starts. It contains nine be-
haviors adapted from the GEO strategies of Aggar-
wal et al. (2023): their best-performing document-
level strategies (Statistics Addition, Cite Sources,
Quotation Addition, Fluency Optimization, Easy-

rewritten as field-scoped editing operations over
structured product listings, plus two behaviors that
are required by the e-commerce setting but have
no counterpart in free-form GEO: B008, which
anchors the rewrite to the shopper’s query intent,
and B009, which constrains all assertions to ev-
idence present in the original listing. The same
nine behaviors constitute the Static HB baseline,
which deploys them without any feedback-based
update. All feedback counters h+, h−, h0 are zero-
initialized, so every seed behavior enters the first
batch inside the probation period.
C
Effect of MODIFY Operations on
Behavior-Level Outcomes
To examine whether MODIFY operations improve
the specific behaviors they target, rather than the
handbook improving only through the addition of
new behaviors, we compare each modified behav-
ior’s episode-level outcomes before and after its
modification using the training logs of the final
GLOBE run. For every behavior with at least one
deployed MODIFY, we split its applied episodes at
the modification boundary and compute the helpful
rate HR(b) = h+(b)/n(b) and mean rank gain on
each side (Table 6). Since overall performance also
improves over training (Figure 3), we estimate the
change expected from this shared trend and mean
reversion using non-modified behaviors over the
same windows, and subtract it from each observed
change.
The improvement concentrates on behaviors that
were under-performing when modified. Behaviors
that were under-performing when modified († in Ta-
ble 6) consistently improve beyond their matched
control (eight of nine; sign test p = 0.020), includ-
ing B012, whose repair is traced qualitatively in
Table 3. In contrast, modifying behaviors already
performing above average yields no consistent gain:
five of the six stay within 3.1 percentage points in
helpful rate, while B024 declines. B036 is the only
under-performing behavior that does not improve.
Both B024 and B036 were modified in the last


update before the final training batch, so their post-
modification outcomes rest on that batch alone, in
which the pooled helpful rate also fell; B024’s revi-
sion also dropped its list of example settings, and
in that batch the editor often applied it to queries
with no adjustment need. Per-behavior significance
is limited for behaviors with few post-modification
episodes (e.g., B013, B018, B020), so the evidence
rests primarily on the aggregate sign test rather
than on individual behaviors. While these compar-
isons are observational and inherit the approximate
per-behavior attribution (see Limitations), they in-
dicate that MODIFY operations are most effective
as targeted repairs for under-performing behaviors,
whereas revising behaviors that already perform
well offers no clear benefit.
D
Deactivated Behaviors
Table 7 lists the behaviors that the active-status
criterion excluded from the active pool during the
final GLOBE run. Of the 51 behaviors stored in
the final GLOBE handbook, four were deactivated,
leaving the 47 active behaviors reported in Table 1.
All four were added by the Reflector, none of the
nine seed behaviors was deactivated, and among
all behaviors applied at least five times, these four
have the lowest mean rank gains.
The behaviors fail in different ways. B014 is
mismatched with the setting: it places a price or
budget match up front, but E-GEO listings contain
no price field, so the edit can rarely be grounded in
the listing; it was applied only five times over seven
batches, and six successive MODIFY operations did
not make it useful. B023 and B032 are misapplied:
they describe reasonable edits, but the editor often
applied them to queries in which attachment or
style was secondary or absent, so placing that detail
up front displaced the shopper’s main need. B046
was added late and deactivated after a single batch,
in which it rarely changed the rank and was more
often harmful than helpful.
Although the criterion uses only ranking out-
comes, most of the behaviors it removes corre-
spond to identifiable failure modes. Removing
them keeps these behaviors out of the editor’s strat-
egy pool in later batches and at test time, without
manual inspection of the handbook. Because de-
activated behaviors remain stored, they also doc-
ument what did not work in this setting, such as
B014’s price-based edits on listings without prices.

Prompt for the Moderator (functional deduplication)
System message
You are a behavior handbook curator. Your job is to find NEW behaviors that are functionally redundant with existing ones.
Two behaviors are redundant if they trigger on the same query pattern AND produce the same type of edit, even if they use
different wording or slightly different examples.
User message
## Handbook ({n} behaviors)
[NEW] | [EXISTING] [{behavior id}] {behavior name}
instruction: {instruction}
target: {target fields}
... (one entry per projected behavior)
## Task
Find [NEW] behaviors that are **functionally redundant** with an [EXISTING] behavior.
Two behaviors are redundant when:
- They trigger on the **same type of query pattern** (e.g., both fire on "exclusion/negation" queries)
- They produce the **same type of edit** (e.g., both add a 1-sentence line near the top)
- Even if wording, examples, or formatting differ
### Examples of TRUE redundancy (flag these):
- "Add exclusion note when query says no/not" vs "Add not-wanted line when query has exclusions" →same trigger, same
edit
- "Add fit/compatibility micro-statement for size queries" vs "Add compatibility line for fit/use constraints" →same trigger,
same edit
- "Map durability skepticism to specific failure prevention spec" vs "Convert durability intent into failure prevention detail"
→same trigger, same edit
### NOT redundant (do NOT flag):
- Different trigger conditions (e.g., "budget queries" vs "size queries")
- Same trigger but meaningfully different edits (e.g., "add line in title" vs "add section in description")
- General vs specific (e.g., "add any constraint" vs "add currency-specific constraint") — keep both
- Same broad topic but different information added: "add use-case summary for vague queries" vs "add missing key spec for
vague queries" →one adds a GOAL, the other adds a SPEC — keep both
- Same trigger but different placement or scope: "add bullet block of top specs" vs "convert existing features into query-
relevant bullets" →one ADDS new content, the other RESTRUCTURES existing content — keep both
- Overlapping trigger with different specificity of action: "add accessories/consumables line" vs "add most important included
item" →one targets accessories specifically, the other is broader — keep both
- Same domain but additive: "add durability spec" vs "add protection/safety rationale" →durability is about material
longevity, safety is about harm prevention — keep both
**Rules**:
- [EXISTING]+[EXISTING] pairs must NEVER be flagged.
- Only flag pairs where the [NEW] behavior adds no unique capability.
- When flagging, always drop the [NEW] one and keep the [EXISTING] one.
- For [NEW]+[NEW] pairs, drop the one with the larger ID.
- **When in doubt, keep both.** It is better to have two similar behaviors than to lose a useful one.
- A broad/general behavior does NOT make a narrower/specific one redundant — the specific one may add unique value for
its target queries.
## Output Format
Return a JSON array (empty [] if no redundancy found):
[{"new_id": "NEW2", "existing_id": "B027", "reason": "Both add a fit/compatibility line for size-constraint queries"}]
Return ONLY the JSON array wrapped in “‘json . . . “‘.

is tagged as [EXISTING] or [NEW]. Existing behaviors can never be dropped by this stage.
