with Harness-Augmented Agents

Abstract
Humans efficiently process multimodal streams
spanning hours to years, building persistent,
fine-grained spatiotemporal memories that sup-
port reasoning about and predicting long-
horizon events. In contrast, existing bench-
marks focus on short- and medium-context
tasks, and most foundation models struggle
to preserve fine-grained information over long
horizons.
To evaluate and improve long-
horizon spatiotemporal reasoning, we intro-
duce the LongVideo dataset and the 4DMem-
oryBench. LongVideo comprises about 900
hours of video and over 5,000 manually re-
viewed questions covering visual, textual, and
spatiotemporal reasoning across diverse con-
text lengths. We further develop the 4DMem-
ory Harness, which integrates four comple-
mentary skills, 22+ specialized tool models,
and a model-specific retrieval-augmented gen-
eration system. Evaluating 13 leading mod-
els, we find that standalone foundation mod-
els struggle with long-horizon tasks, while the
4DMemory Harness improves all of them by
over 50%. Compared to previous benchmark,
the same models exhibit a performance drop of
more than 10% on 4DMemoryBench. We hope
4DMemory will facilitate the development of
spatiotemporal agents capable of addressing
complex, long-horizon tasks.

Introduction
Humans do not always know in advance which
aspects of an unfolding experience will matter to
a future goal. They form long-term memories as
the world evolves and, once a concrete goal arises,
selectively retrieve relevant experiences, integrate
them with new observations, and update their cur-
rent understanding (Tulving and Thomson, 1973;
Hupbach et al., 2007). This interaction between
persistent memory and goal-directed recall enables
reasoning across long temporal horizons. As multi-
modal large language models (MLLMs) advance

from static-image perception toward video under-
standing and multimodal reasoning (Maaz et al.,
2024; Li et al., 2024a; Gemini Team, 2024; Wang
et al., 2024a), developing an analogous capability
becomes increasingly important for artificial sys-
tems. Long-horizon 4D tasks require models to
preserve how entities, spatial relations, states, and
events evolve over time, so that observations made
hours earlier can inform later reasoning.
Although modern MLLMs perform strongly on
many vision-language tasks, they remain poorly
matched to long-horizon 4D reasoning. First, a
nominally long context window does not imply
an equally long effective video context, as dense
video generates long sequences of visual tokens
that impose substantial computational and memory
costs during visual encoding and attention. Practi-
cal systems therefore depend on sparse frame sam-
pling or token compression, which may discard
fine-grained evidence before the eventual task is
known (Wu et al., 2024; Fu et al., 2025). Second,
strong long-text reasoning does not by itself pro-
vide the multimodal alignment, temporal reasoning,
and spatial understanding needed to connect events
across changing scenes (Liu et al., 2024; Yang et al.,
2025). As a result, directly processing hours of
video with an MLLM remains unreliable for com-
plex 4D tasks whose evidence is distributed across
distant moments, scenes, and temporal scales.
Prior work has addressed these limitations
through complementary interventions at the sys-
tem, model, and method levels. At the system
level, agentic harnesses iteratively retrieve query-
relevant frames, maintain structured temporal and
object memories, and orchestrate multi-granular
search or specialist tools (Wang et al., 2024b; Fan
et al., 2024; Ma et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026). At
the model level, long-video MLLMs extend their
effective visual horizon through long-context train-
ing, compact per-frame tokenization, and explicit
temporal encoding (Chen et al., 2025; Li et al.,

At the method level,
adaptive spatiotemporal compression removes re-
dundant frames and visual tokens, sparse mem-
ory consolidates dense observations into long-term
representations, and query-adaptive hierarchical
selection allocates computation across temporal
granularities (Shen et al., 2025; Song et al., 2024;
Wang et al., 2025b). Together, these advances im-
prove access to long-range evidence, but their gains
are typically evaluated under different datasets, in-
terfaces, and access conditions, making it diffi-
cult to isolate improvements in the base model,
evidence acquisition, memory, and final reason-
ing. Moreover, these components are not readily
composable across systems, since methods such
as LongVU require access to the internal vision-
token pipeline, whereas LongVT learns its tool-
use protocol through model-specific post-training
of Qwen2.5-VL-7B rather than through a model-
agnostic plug-in interface (Shen et al., 2025; Yang
et al., 2026).
Moreover, expanding a model’s nominal context
window determines how much input it can accept,
but not how much distributed evidence it can use
effectively, as performance degrades with increas-
ing context length and task complexity even when
all relevant evidence is perfectly retrieved (Hsieh
et al., 2024; Du et al., 2025). External memory and
RAG reduce the raw context presented at once, but
shift the bottleneck to deciding what to store, com-
press, update, retrieve, and ultimately use; current
systems can lose visual fidelity during memory con-
struction and remain far below human performance
on long-term temporal and causal reasoning (Maha-
rana et al., 2024; Ren et al., 2026). Reusable skills
and tools provide procedural guidance and special-
ist capabilities, but their gains depend strongly on
the task and harness, while reliable execution re-
quires correct routing and parameter grounding and
deteriorates as tool catalogs, tool outputs, and inter-
action histories grow (Li et al., 2026c; Kate et al.,
2025; Shen et al., 2026). Consequently, as task
horizons and dependency depth grow by orders
of magnitude, errors accumulate across memory
construction, retrieval, skill selection, and reason-
ing, and current systems have yet to demonstrate
stable and reliable performance on complex multi-
modal tasks that demand human-like episodic re-
call, temporal integration, memory updating, and
coordinated action.

comprising over 1,000 standalone long videos
and two multi-season television series, with ap-

on LongVideo, we build LongVideo, which con-
tains more than 5,000 question-answer pairs and
combines multi-evidence questions on individual
videos with episode-, season-, and complete-series
queries across visual, textual, and spatiotemporal
reasoning. To support a broad range of LLMs and

2.2
Compositional QA construction
We turn the aligned records into questions that re-
quire combining evidence, rather than recalling
an isolated fact. For standalone videos, the gen-
erator receives time-ordered evidence cards con-
taining clip summaries, available ASR, and tempo-
ral bounds. Each candidate connects 2 to 4 clips
through an explicit operation such as comparison,
temporal ordering, state change, cross-modal bind-
ing, or distributed counting. A separate verifier
checks the answer and the joint necessity of the
evidence by removing each supporting clip in turn.
Only 3 to 5 accepted questions are retained per
video, yielding 4,388 QA pairs.
For serialized video, we first compute reference
answers with deterministic programs over validated
dialogue, ASR, and visual statistics at the requested
scope. A language model rewrites each program as
a self-contained question, and an independent veri-
fication call checks that the wording preserves the
program’s meaning and answer. This produces 826
questions covering episode, season, and complete-
series aggregation, comparison, ordering, and trend
analysis. Visual counts denote tracker identities
within a clip, summed across clips, rather than glob-
ally deduplicated people or objects.

2.4
4DMemory Harness
Complex tasks over long multimodal histories re-
quire both access to diverse evidence and selec-
tive recall of earlier observations. Although mod-
ern LLMs can compress context and invoke skills,
these capabilities do not remove the constraints im-
posed by native input support and finite context
windows. Repeated compression may also omit
details that become relevant to subsequent ques-
tions. We therefore introduce 4DMemory Har-
ness, which separates media processing, persis-
tent memory, and question-conditioned reasoning.
The harness exposes three processing skills—video
processing, visual grounding, and text statistics—
and stores question-independent, timestamped ob-
servations in persistent structured memory.
A
lightweight retrieval layer selects relevant records
and their temporal neighbors to provide bounded
evidence for reasoning. When the visual input path-
way does not carry audio, an internal transcription
module processes the available source audio and
incorporates timestamped speech evidence into the
corresponding memory records.
Video-processing skill.
The video-processing
skill prepares bound sources for model observation
through size inspection and temporal segmentation
under request constraints. It preserves source-time
references so that evidence from separate chunks
can be related along the original timeline.
Visual-grounding skill.
The visual-grounding
skill uses SAM 3.1 to provide tracking evidence
for supported TV scopes. It returns ordered person
and object counts within episodes and person-count
aggregates over longer scopes, supporting counting,
comparison, and temporal reasoning.
Text-statistics skill.
The text-statistics skill pro-
vides deterministic counts, speaker rankings, and
aggregates derived from TV dialogue and ASR an-
notations. A complementary episode-analysis tool
produces bounded semantic summaries and topic
information when richer textual evidence is needed.
All three skills expose typed operations, with
source configuration and execution policies con-
trolled by 4DMemory Harness.
Multimodal observation.
After media prepara-
tion, an observer converts temporal units into struc-
tured observations without access to the benchmark
question. It receives native video or timestamped
frames according to the configured input pathway.
When enabled, an internal transcription module
adds timestamped speech evidence from the source
audio.
Question-independent memory construction.
Observations are stored as persistent documents
with source identities, temporal bounds, and
structured multimodal fields. Their construction
excludes question-specific supervision, allowing
provenance-matched records to be reused across
questions and temporal scopes. Memory reuse fol-
lows the configuration of the actual observer, in-
cluding any separately configured observer used by
a text-only reasoner.

Experiments
Experimental setup.
We evaluate 13 API-served
models on all 5,214 questions in 4DMemoryBench
using 4DMemory Harness, without benchmark-
specific parameter updates.
Each system uses
Gemini 3.1 Pro as a separate media observer, en-
abling evaluated models to access evidence from
modalities they cannot process directly.
To re-
duce dependence on a single judge, we assess the
same model responses under two independent judg-
ing protocols: an Opus-5 judge and a majority-
vote panel comprising Kimi K3, GLM 5.3, and
Qwen3.8-Max. Tool invocation and execution are
assessed separately, and performance is summa-
rized through four complementary metrics. Invoca-
tion measures the fraction of questions with at least
one accepted tool call, while Ordered measures
the fraction of these questions answered correctly
with all required operations successfully executed
in the prescribed order. Order-relaxed removes the
execution-order constraint, while Overall measures
the resulting joint success rate over all benchmark
questions.
Skills/Tools is crucial for LLMs in complex tasks.
The overall results in Table 2 show that frequent

4
Conclusion
We introduced LongVideo and 4DMemoryBench
to evaluate long-horizon multimodal memory
across dozens of hours of video. The benchmark
comprises approximately 887 hours of video and
covers visual, textual, and spatiotemporal reason-
ing. Our 4DMemory Harness combines question-
independent persistent memory, bounded retrieval,
and specialized tools, with separate metrics for
tool invocation, execution order, and joint answer
success. Across 13 evaluated models, high invo-
cation rates coexist with substantial differences in
workflow compliance and final-answer correctness,
with the highest observed joint success reaching
77.79% under judging. Task-level analyses reveal
persistent difficulties in distributed counting and
multi-scope aggregation, while differences across
sources and temporal scopes expose complemen-
tary system strengths. These findings establish a
basis for studying how observation, memory access,
and tool-mediated computation jointly support reli-
able reasoning over extended multimodal histories.
8


Limitations
Our study does not include agent fine-tuning on our
data, leaving the potential benefits of such adapta-
tion unexplored. Consequently, the reported results
do not establish the effectiveness of these data as a
training resource for agents. Evaluation across ad-
ditional downstream tasks is also beyond the scope
of this work, and the broader applicability of our
approach remains to be established. Tasks with
different objectives and interaction settings may re-
quire further adaptation and dedicated evaluation.

S1
Related Work
Long-video benchmarks.
Long-video bench-
marks increasingly test evidence integration over
extended temporal spans (Zhu et al., 2026).
EgoSchema measures intrinsic evidence span
with temporal certificate sets, while MMBench-
Video uses free-form questions over multi-shot
videos (Mangalam et al., 2023; Fang et al., 2024).
LongVideoBench and Video-MME extend evalu-
ation to hour-long inputs and multiple modality
streams, while MLVU broadens video genres and
task formats (Wu et al., 2024; Fu et al., 2025;
Zhou et al., 2025). TempCompass and VSI-Bench
isolate fine-grained temporal reasoning and spa-
tial memory, respectively (Liu et al., 2024; Yang
et al., 2025). At longer scales, LVBench evaluates
multi-hour videos and InfiniBench covers long-
form movies and television episodes, highlight-
ing persistent difficulty in temporal grounding and
cross-event reasoning (Wang et al., 2025a; Ataal-
lah et al., 2025; Wen et al., 2026). EgoMonth ex-
tends this direction to month-level per-participant
continuity and cross-video egocentric QA over a
301-hour longitudinal corpus (Chen et al., 2026).
Agentic long-video understanding.
Agentic ap-
proaches treat long-video understanding as itera-
tive evidence acquisition through observation se-
lection, tool use, and reasoning-state updates. The
two VideoAgent systems exemplify query-driven
frame retrieval and structured temporal or object
memory, respectively (Wang et al., 2024b; Ataal-
lah et al., 2025; Jia et al., 2026c,a). Retrieval-
and planning-centered systems convert videos into
searchable evidence, zoom across space and time,
and control the scope and density of each observa-
tion (Ma et al., 2025; Ye et al., 2025; Zhang et al.,
2026; Li et al., 2026b). Memory-centric agents
organize episodic, semantic, visual, or hierarchical
representations for iterative retrieval and reason-
ing (Yeo et al., 2026; Yin et al., 2026). LongVT
and VideoThinker internalize multi-step tool use
through model-specific post-training or synthetic
tool-interaction trajectories (Yang et al., 2026; Li
et al., 2026a). Beyond video-specific systems, Skill
Retrieval Augmentation decomposes scalable skill
use into retrieval, incorporation, and end-task ex-
ecution, while AgenticSTS uses typed retrieval to
bound long-horizon decision contexts and isolate
memory layers (Li et al., 2026c; Cheng et al., 2026).
More generally, EnvHarness reshapes static agent
environments through Stage, Contract, and Chain
plug-ins while retaining their original verifiers, and
EnvRigger uses black-box policy rollouts to diag-
nose weaknesses and validate targeted customiza-
tions (Huang et al., 2026a). MemoHarness instead
adapts six harness control dimensions from accu-
mulated execution experience and reports selective
transfer across unseen task suites and base mod-
els (Huang et al., 2026b).
Multimodal long-term memory.
Long-term
memory research mainly evaluates sustained, multi-
session interactions (Zhao et al., 2026). LoCoMo
covers question answering, event summarization,
and multimodal dialogue generation, while Long-
MemEval tests extraction, cross-session and tem-
poral reasoning, knowledge updates, and absten-
tion (Maharana et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2025a).
MMRC evaluates memory abilities in real-world
multimodal conversations, whereas MemLens com-
pares long-context vision-language models with
memory-augmented agents (Xue et al., 2025; Ren
et al., 2026). Mem-Gallery assesses memory ex-
traction, reasoning, and knowledge management,
while M3-Agent builds entity-centric episodic and
semantic memories from continuous visual and au-
ditory observations (Bei et al., 2026; Long et al.,
2026; Jia et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025b; Jia et al.,
2026b). Mem2ActBench extends evaluation be-
yond recall to using stored information for tool se-
lection and parameter grounding (Shen et al., 2026).
MemoryArena couples memory formation with ac-
tion across interdependent sessions and exposes
failures missed by recall-oriented benchmarks (He
et al., 2026). Harness the Memory compares het-
erogeneous memory substrates under a common
evaluation harness and finds that their effective-
ness depends on task regime, retrieval breadth, and
history scale (Huang et al., 2026a).

Answer and skill assessment.
Answer correct-

constraints. We report Invocation as B/N, Ordered

all as (C + D)/N. An accepted invocation may
encounter a tool error; only successful calls sat-

once because it is identical across the two judging
protocols.

combined evidence summary to 64,000 characters.
S3.3
Inference budgets and execution
The preparation and reasoning loops allow four
and eight rounds, respectively, with an eight-call
tool budget for each loop and a 16,000-character
tool-output limit. The default API request uses tem-
perature 0, a 2,048-token output budget, and a 600-
second timeout. Model-specific request adapters
retain the required token and reasoning controls,
including an 8,192-token minimum output budget
for Muse-Spark-1.1. The evaluator selection seed
is 42; local visual and text tools use seed 0, with-
out assuming deterministic behavior from hosted
APIs. Current recovery runs use 8–48 item workers
and 16–32 in-flight evaluated requests per model,
with shared provider gates enforcing 60–300 re-
quests per minute. GPU-capable work is separately
limited to four candidate workers. Retries and re-
sumed runs are tracked by question identifier and
source/configuration provenance, and completed
model answers can be rescored without rerunning
inference.

Answer correctness and skill use are evaluated sep-
arately. Private reference answers are restricted to
the answer judge, while tool traces and provenance

valid decisions before applying a two-of-three ma-
jority rule. Missing or failed judge calls remain

votes, and these results are kept separate from the
Opus-only scores. Each run records the selected
question identifiers, resolved model and observer
routes, decoding and concurrency settings, code
and configuration hashes, cache policy, and termi-
nation status.

Arithmetic success does not generalize across

Table S4 localizes the

First-appearance reasoning and temporal ag-
gregation separate sharply.
GPT-5.6 solves all
64 first-appearance ordering and localization ques-

count comparisons (6.25%) and one of 17 trend-

ordering a small set of first occurrences is insuffi-

quence of counts. It does not establish whether the
failing stage is evidence retrieval, tool execution, or
final computation, because joint accuracy requires
all of these stages to succeed. The five-question
maximum/minimum visual subtype and other small
groups should likewise be interpreted at their stated
sample sizes rather than as stable rankings.

S4.4
Standalone-video spatiotemporal
reasoning
Distributed counting remains difficult across sys-
tems.
The six selected video subtypes in Table 3
reveal a common gap between event ordering and
distributed counting. GPT-5.6 scores 72.49% on
1,374 multi-event ordering questions but 44.35% on
372 distributed-counting questions, a 28.14-point
difference with both groups fully judged. Opus-4.8
also has fully judged distributed-counting results
and scores 45.97%; the highest observed score in
that column is Muse-1.1’s 47.58%, also with com-
plete judging. Every system with a reported three-
judge score performs worse on distributed counting
than on event ordering. This pattern is consistent
with difficulty accumulating evidence across sepa-
rated events, although aggregate scores alone can-
not distinguish omissions, duplicate counting, and
arithmetic errors.
Other subtypes reveal complementary strengths.
The highest observed trajectory-tracking score is
Qwen3.8-Max’s provisional 66.46%, while Muse-
1.1 has the highest observed state-change score
at a provisional 73.80%. Grok-4.6 and Muse-1.1
both score 78.80% on fully judged cross-modal
reasoning, and Grok-4.6 scores 81.58% on fully
judged cross-modal binding. These profiles support

evaluating evidence accumulation, temporal order,
trajectories, and modality binding separately, rather
than treating spatiotemporal reasoning as a single
uniform capability.
Judge choice and coverage bound the conclu-
sions.
The paired task-by-scope scores also ex-
pose sensitivity to the evaluation protocol. For
MiMo-2.5 on complete-series spatiotemporal ques-
tions, Opus-only and three-judge scores are 79.17%
and 70.83%, with complete judging under both
protocols. GPT-5.6 also has complete judging un-
der both protocols, but its standalone spatiotempo-
ral score changes from 69.20% to 67.96%. These
aggregate differences do not quantify inter-judge
agreement, which requires a separate comparison
of matched item-level votes. The results charac-
terize the evaluated reasoner–observer–tool sys-
tems under the stated metric; without matched
standalone-model runs or component ablations,
they do not measure a causal gain from the har-
ness or identify a unique failure mechanism.
S5
Discussion
S5.1
Limitations
Our findings should be interpreted within the bench-
mark’s current coverage and evaluation setting.
The serialized collection covers two sitcoms, and
the task distribution emphasizes spatiotemporal rea-
soning over video-derived evidence. Automated
annotation and model-based judging introduce un-
certainty, while performance depends on observa-
tion quality, retrieval budgets, and tool execution.
The reported scores therefore characterize the eval-
uated reasoner–observer–tool systems as a whole.
S5.2
Future Work
Future work will explore broader domains and con-
tinuous streams to evaluate memory updates, entity
persistence, and reasoning across temporal scales.
Matched evaluations with controlled observation
inputs and inference budgets can clarify the contri-
butions of memory representations, retrieval poli-
cies, and specialist tools. Promising directions in-
clude quantity-aware memory with explicit tempo-
ral provenance, uncertainty-guided retrieval, and
source-level human verification to strengthen eval-
uation reliability.
