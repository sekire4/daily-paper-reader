## STEER SEARCH : Interactive Search Reasoning with Mid-Response Steering 

Udit Thakur 1,2* Baohao Liao 1,2† Matteo Nulli 2 Stefan Vasilev 2

Seyyed Hadi Hashemi 2 Shahram Khadivi 2 Christof Monz 11Language Technology Lab, University of Amsterdam 2eBay 

Abstract 

Search-augmented language models increas-ingly serve as long-horizon research agents, yet most open training methods assume a fixed user goal throughout search. We study mid-response steering , where users redirect a model during ongoing reasoning or retrieval. We iden-tify answer-target anchoring , a failure mode in which fixed-query search agents retrieve evi-dence for the updated request but still answer an earlier question. We introduce S TEER SEARCH ,an RL framework that trains on interactive search trajectories with injected interventions to enable context-preserving target reassign-ment, and S TEER SEARCH -B ENCH , a bench-mark adapted from multi-hop QA datasets. We also investigate Contrastive Context Reward (CCR), an auxiliary signal for aligning post-intervention reasoning with the updated goal. Without CCR, S TEER SEARCH improves av-erage steering accuracy over Search-R1 from 11.1% to 31.3% on Qwen2.5-3B and from 36.6% to 44.6% on Qwen3-4B, while preserv-ing standard single-query performance. Gains also extend to larger Qwen2.5 models and nat-ural multi-turn conversations. 

1 Introduction 

Search-augmented language models are increas-ingly being deployed as long-horizon research agents rather than one-shot question answerers (OpenAI, 2025b; Anthropic, 2025b; Google, 2025; Zheng et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025a; Team et al., 2026). Systems such as Deep Research (OpenAI, 2025b) plan, search, and synthesize information over multiple steps, shifting search-augmented rea-soning toward extended interactive workflows. Re-cent agentic interfaces (Anthropic, 2025a; OpenAI, 2025a) allow users to interrupt long-running re-sponses and refine the request without restarting or losing progress. introduces a new requirement for search agents: they must continue from accumulated evidence while adapting to user instructions that arrive after reasoning or retrieval has already begun. However, most open search-reasoning training methods still assume a fixed-goal trajectory (Jin et al., 2025b; Chen et al., 2025; Zheng et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025a; Li et al., 2026; Wu et al., 2025b). Methods such as Search-R1 (Jin et al., 2025b) and ReSearch (Chen et al., 2025) train language mod-els to interleave reasoning with search, allowing retrieved evidence to influence subsequent gener-ations, but the user goal is typically fixed at the start of the rollout. Even when multiple searches are allowed, the model is optimized to answer the original question rather than to handle a new user turn that changes the operative target during gener-ation. This mismatch gives rise to a distinct failure mode for interactive research agents, which we call 

answer-target anchoring . When a model trained under a fixed-goal trajectory is placed in an inter-rupted interaction setting, it may retrieve evidence that is sufficient for the user’s updated request, but still produce an answer to an earlier question be-cause its reasoning state remains anchored to the original target. For example, after identifying the Harlem Globetrotters as the team Louis Dunbar played for, a user may interrupt with a dependent follow-up asking how many countries that team has played in. Answering the follow-up requires pre-serving the intermediate entity from the first phase, but also reassigning the answer target from “which team” to “how many countries?”. Standard search-reasoning RL provides little direct supervision for this kind of context-preserving goal shift. In this work, we introduce S TEER SEARCH , a re-inforcement learning framework for mid-response steering in search-augmented reasoning, as illus-trated in Figure 1. To support this behavior, S TEER SEARCH combines randomized in-tervention timing, intermediate-answer masking, and Contrastive Context Reward (CCR), a new technique encouraging the model’s reasoning to pivot toward newly injected user goals (Figure 2). To evaluate this setting, we construct STEER SEARCH -B ENCH , a mid-response steering benchmark adapted from state of the art multi-hop benchmarks (Yang et al., 2018; Ho et al., 2020; Trivedi et al., 2022; Press et al., 2023), where each original question is converted into a sequence of injected user interventions. Experiments on two instruction-tuned models show that S TEER SEARCH substantially improves mid-response steering on average ∼ 14 percentage points over multi-hop, while preserving single-query search reasoning performance. CCR further improves steering accuracy, with gains varying across model and reward-model configurations. We additionally demonstrate generalization across model scales, with S TEER SEARCH consistently outperforming Search-R1 on the 7B and 14B variants of Qwen2.5, as well as across model families on Gemma3-4B. Our contributions are as follows: • We formulate mid-response steering for search-augmented reasoning and identify answer-target anchoring , a failure mode where fixed-goal search agents retrieve evidence for an updated request but still answer an earlier question. • We propose S TEER SEARCH , an RL framework that trains models on interactive search trajecto-ries with injected user interventions, encouraging context-preserving target reassignment during generation. We also introduce CCR, an auxiliary reward for aligning post-intervention reasoning with the updated user goal. • We introduce S TEER SEARCH -B ENCH , a bench-mark for evaluating whether search agents can follow mid-response user interventions while reusing accumulated evidence. • We show that S TEER SEARCH improves aver-age accuracy on S TEER SEARCH -B ENCH by 8.0– 20.2 percentage points over Search-R1, while preserving performance on standard search-reasoning benchmarks. 

2 Related Work 

Search-augmented reasoning with reinforce-ment learning. Recent work trains language models to interleave reasoning with external search. Search-R1 (Jin et al., 2025b) and Re-Search (Chen et al., 2025) formulate retrieval as part of the model’s action space, allowing the policy to issue search queries, read returned evi-dence, and continue reasoning before producing an answer. Follow-up work shows that search-reasoning agents are sensitive to reward design, initialization, and retrieval environments (Jin et al., 2025a), while related RL-based approaches extend search reasoning to multimodal search and long-horizon web research (Wu et al., 2025b; Narayan et al., 2025; Zheng et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026; Liao et al., 2026b). However, these methods typ-ically optimize uninterrupted trajectories toward a fixed user goal, whereas S TEER SEARCH stud-ies search-reasoning after a new user turn arrives mid-response. 

Multi-hop reasoning and query decompo-sition. Multi-hop question answering bench-marks evaluate whether models can combine ev-idence across multiple facts or documents. Hot-potQA (Yang et al., 2018), 2WikiMultiHopQA (Ho et al., 2020), and MuSiQue (Trivedi et al., 2022) construct questions whose answers require linked reasoning steps, often with supporting evidence or structured reasoning paths. Prompting methods such as Self-Ask (Press et al., 2023) encourage models to decompose a question into intermediate sub-questions, and recent training approaches such as ParallelSearch (Zhao et al., 2025) learn to de-compose complex queries into sub-queries that can be searched independently. Unlike static query de-composition, S TEER SEARCH treats sub-questions as user interventions injected into an ongoing tra-jectory, requiring the model to incorporate the new goal without discarding prior context. 

Interactive and long-horizon research agents. 

Recent systems move search-augmented language models toward long-horizon research workflows. Deployed systems such as ChatGPT Deep Re-search, Gemini Deep Research, and Claude Re-search browse, inspect sources, and synthesize find-ings over extended interactions (OpenAI, 2025b; Google, 2025; Anthropic, 2025b). Web-agent benchmarks further evaluate models’ ability to nav-igate, gather information, and solve tasks across multiple steps (Liao et al., 2026a, 2025b; Mialon et al., 2024; Wei et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2025). Re-cent ChatGPT interfaces also expose mid-response course adjustment, where users can refine a request while a long-running response is still being pro-duced (OpenAI, 2026). Together, these systems and benchmarks moti-vate a training problem not captured by standard search-reasoning evaluations: agents must remain responsive to user steering after reasoning and re-trieval have already begun. 

3 Preliminaries 

3.1 Mid-Response Steering 

We study search-augmented reasoning where the user may intervene while the model is still rea-soning or retrieving. Unlike standard single-query search, where the answer target remains fixed throughout the trajectory, mid-response steering allows a new user goal to arrive after partial rea-soning and retrieval have already occurred. The model must therefore adapt to the latest goal while preserving useful information from the existing tra-jectory. 3.2 Answer-Target Anchoring 

A natural question is whether models trained only for single-query search can directly handle mid-response steering. We evaluate a Search-R1-style model (Jin et al., 2025b) on both the original multi-hop questions and their mid-response steering vari-ants. As shown in Figure 3, performance drops substantially when the same reasoning problem is presented through sequential user interventions, in-dicating that strong fixed-query search ability does not directly transfer to a changing answer target. We identify a common failure mode behind this degradation, which we call answer-target anchor-ing . As illustrated in Figure 4, the model may respond locally to a new user query by reasoning about it and retrieving the required evidence, yet still produce a final answer corresponding to an earlier goal. The failure is therefore distinct from retrieval error: the relevant evidence may already be present, while the model fails to update which user goal controls the final response. This phenomenon is related to long-context ro-bustness issues such as lost-in-the-middle (Liu et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024; Liao et al., 2025a), but differs in the source of the failure. Mid-response steering requires the model not only to use information from a growing context, but to reassign the operative answer target after a user interruption while preserving useful prior evidence. This motivates training directly on trajectories in which the target changes during generation. 

4 STEER SEARCH 

Motivated by answer-target anchoring (Section 3.2) in single-query search models under user interven-tion, we present S TEER SEARCH , a reinforcement learning framework for context-preserving target reassignment under mid-response steering. 

4.1 Overview 

STEER SEARCH trains the policy on the interactive trajectory formulation defined in Section 3.1, ex-posing it during training to the same type of target changes encountered when a user redirects an ongo-ing search. Its key departures from standard fixed-query search training are: (1) randomized phase budgets , which vary intervention timing; (2) inter-mediate answer removal , which prevents premature answers from anchoring subsequent reasoning; and (3) a Contrastive Context Reward (CCR), which provides direct supervision for post-intervention target shifts. A rollout begins with the initial user goal and alternates between model reasoning and retrieval. Before the terminal phase, the next user goal is in-jected after a randomized interaction budget while the accumulated reasoning, search queries, and re-trieved evidence remain available. If the model produces an answer in a non-final phase, the pre-mature answer is masked before continuing to the next intervention while useful preceding reason-ing and retrieval are retained. 5 Experiments 

Models and Baselines. Our primary exper-iments use Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct (Yang et al., 2025b) and Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 (Yang et al., 2025a). We additionally evaluate Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct, and Gemma3-4B-Instruct (Gemma-Team, 2025) in the text-only set-ting to study model generalization. Our primary baseline is Search-R1 (Jin et al., 2025b), which trains the same base models for fixed-query it-erative reasoning and retrieval but does not in-clude mid-response interventions. Search-R1 and STEER SEARCH use the same retriever, PPO objec-tive, reward format, and primary training budget, isolating the effect of intervention-based training. 

Training. Both methods are initialized from supervised fine-tuning on 4K GPT-5.4-generated HotpotQA trajectories, followed by PPO training. The primary experiments use 40K RL samples. Search-R1 is trained only on fixed-query trajec-tories, while S TEER SEARCH uses a mixture of fixed-query and multi-phase trajectories contain-ing injected sub-questions. The S TEER SEARCH 

trajectories are constructed from multi-hop ques-tions and expose the model to decomposition, hint steering, and disambiguation interventions. Unless otherwise stated, all primary results use the SFT-4K/PPO-40K configuration. Detailed training data composition, rollout configuration, and optimiza-tion settings are provided in Appendix A. 

Evaluation. We evaluate two complemen-tary settings. The Standard Benchmark measures single-query retrieval-augmented question answer-ing across seven datasets and tests whether steering training preserves ordinary search-reasoning ability. STEER SEARCH -B ENCH evaluates the target inter-active setting by injecting sub-questions during an ongoing search trajectory and scoring only the final answer. We report exact match (EM) as the pri-mary metric, following Jin et al. (2025b) while also reporting semantic evaluation using GPT-5.5 as a judge. We additionally evaluate S TEER SEARCH on natural multi-turn conversations from TopiOCQA Adlakha et al. (2022) to test generalization beyond synthetically generated interventions. Benchmark construction and evaluation details are provided in Appendix A.10.

6 Conclusion 

We introduced S TEER SEARCH , a reinforcement learning framework for mid-response steering in search-augmented reasoning. We showed that fixed-query search agents suffer from answer-target anchoring, where they retrieve evidence for a new user goal but still answer an earlier question. By training on interactive trajectories with injected user interventions, S TEER SEARCH learns context-preserving target reassignment, substantially im-proving performance on S TEER SEARCH -B ENCH 

while preserving standard search-reasoning ability. Limitations 

This work focuses on mid-response steering for text-based search-augmented reasoning. Our ex-periments use Wikipedia-style retrieval and multi-hop QA benchmarks adapted into steering trajec-tories, which provide controlled evaluation but do not cover every form of open-ended web research. Extending S TEER SEARCH to noisier web environ-ments, multimodal retrieval, and longer interactive sessions is a natural next step. STEER SEARCH improves the model’s ability to update its answer target after user interventions, but it does not solve all remaining reasoning errors. Some failures still require fine-grained role disam-biguation, exact comparison, or synthesis across partially retrieved evidence. These limitations are orthogonal to the mid-response steering setting and can be addressed with stronger retrieval, verifica-tion, or answer validation modules. 

Ethical Considerations 

This work studies how search-augmented language models respond to user interventions during on-going reasoning. The proposed training data and benchmark are derived from public QA datasets and Wikipedia-style retrieval, without collecting private user interactions. However, systems that support mid-response steering may be used in open-ended research settings where retrieved content can contain misinformation, bias, or sensitive informa-tion. Improving steering behavior therefore does not remove the need for source verification, cita-tion, and safety filtering in deployed systems. Because S TEER SEARCH trains models to fol-low newly injected user goals, care should also be taken when applying it to unsafe or policy-violating requests. Future deployments should combine mid-response steering with existing safeguards for tool use, retrieval access, and refusal behavior.

This chapter describes the models, data, training procedure, rollout environment, and evaluation benchmarks used in this work. The experimental design is built around a controlled comparison be-tween Search-R1 and S TEER SEARCH . Search-R1 trains search-augmented reasoning under a fixed user goal, while S TEER SEARCH trains the same base models to handle user interventions injected during an ongoing trajectory. Unless otherwise stated, both methods use the same retriever, base model family, PPO implementation, and evaluation protocol. 

A.1 Models and Training Configuration 

We conduct experiments on four instruction-tuned models. The primary experiments use Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct (Yang et al., 2025b) and Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 (Yang et al., 2025a). For brevity, we refer to Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 as Qwen3-4B-Instruct in the text; tables use the full model name. These two models are used for the main re-sults, CCR analysis, and data-scaling experiments. To study model scaling, we additionally train Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct. The scaling models are evaluated separately in Sec-tion C.3. Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 is used in non-thinking mode. Its built-in pre-reasoning behavior is disabled, and all reasoning is instead gener-ated inside explicit <reason> tags following the STEER SEARCH prompt format. Qwen2.5 models use the corresponding <think> format. All pri-mary experiments use the SFT-4k/PPO-40k train-ing budget. Smaller training budgets, SFT-2k/PPO-8k and SFT-4k/PPO-16k, are used only for the data-scaling ablation in Section C.5.

A.3 PPO Training 

Both Search-R1 and S TEER SEARCH are optimized with PPO (Schulman et al., 2017) using the verl 

framework (Sheng et al., 2025). PPO training uses a rollout batch size of 512, PPO mini-batch size of 256, and per-GPU micro-batch size of 8. The actor is optimized with AdamW at learning rate 1×10 −6

and warmup ratio 0.285. Rollouts are generated with vLLM using maximum response length 1,024 and temperature 1.0. Actor, critic, and reference models use FSDP2 with parameter and optimizer offloading. A KL penalty with coefficient 0.001 is applied against the frozen reference policy. Primary models are trained for 600 steps on 8 A100 GPUs and evaluated every 200 steps. For the 7B and 14B scaling experiments, the ac-tor learning rate is reduced to 5 × 10 −7 and RL training runs for 400 steps. These models are evalu-ated at step 400 because their reward curves plateau earlier than the 3B runs, indicating early conver-gence.

The key distinction between Search-R1 and S TEER SEARCH is not the base retriever or the PPO objective, but the interaction structure of the trajectories. Search-R1 receives fixed-query trajec-tories, while S TEER SEARCH receives a mixture of fixed-query examples and multi-phase trajectories with injected user interventions. 

STEER SEARCH sub-question generation. Sub-questions for multi-hop training samples are gener-ated from HotpotQA using GPT-5.4. We use three generation strategies. Decomposition produces se-quential sub-questions that narrow the problem through explicit constraints. Hint steering pro-duces softer directional interventions that redirect the search process without directly committing to an intermediate answer. Disambiguation focuses on resolving ambiguous entities or references be-fore factual retrieval. These strategies are designed to expose the model to different ways a user may steer an ongoing search trajectory. Generated chains are filtered by a committee of three GPT-5.4 judge instances. A chain is discarded and regenerated if any judge rejects it. Rejection criteria include answer leakage, bare-noun keyword queries, bundled multi-part questions, malformed utterances, or missing verb structure. This filtering step is important because mid-response steering evaluation is only meaningful if the injected sub-questions guide retrieval without directly revealing the final answer. 

SFT trajectories. SFT trajectories are gener-ated by running GPT-5.4 with the S TEER SEARCH 

prompt and the same E5-based dense retriever used during RL. A trajectory is retained only if its final answer passes exact match evaluation against the ground-truth answer. This ensures that the super-vised initialization is based on successful reasoning and search behavior rather than noisy demonstra-tions. 

RL data. The primary S TEER SEARCH RL pool contains 40,000 samples. Of these, 24,000 are multi-hop HotpotQA samples with generated sub-question chains, split equally across decomposition, hint steering, and disambiguation. The remaining 16,000 are fixed-query samples split equally be-tween HotpotQA and NQ. Mixing fixed-query and injected-trajectory examples prevents the model from becoming dependent on always receiving sub-question interventions, which is important because standard search evaluation contains no injections. For Search-R1 training, we use 40,000 fixed-query samples drawn equally from HotpotQA and NQ, following Jin et al. (2025b). This gives Search-R1 and S TEER SEARCH matched RL pool sizes while preserving the key difference in training for-mat: fixed-goal search for Search-R1 versus mixed fixed-goal and mid-response steering trajectories for S TEER SEARCH . Smaller RL pools of 8,000 and 16,000 samples are used for the data-scaling study in Section C.5. 

A.5 Rollout Configuration 

The S TEER SEARCH rollout loop constructs an interactive trajectory through a sequence of K

user-intervention phases, as described in Algo-rithm 1. Each phase corresponds to one user goal 

uk. The outer loop advances through the user goals 

u1, . . . , u K , while the inner loop allows the model to perform a bounded number of reasoning and search turns before the next intervention is injected. The number of phases K is determined by the sub-question chain length of each example and therefore varies across samples. To avoid teach-ing the model a fixed injection schedule, each non-final phase receives a randomly sampled search budget. For the initial and intermediate phases, 

Tk ∼ Unif {1, . . . , 5}; for the final phase, TK = 4 .This gives the model enough opportunity to retrieve evidence for the terminal goal while preventing un-bounded exploration. The same retrieval setup is used during training and evaluation. 

A.6 Search Engine Setup 

All search calls retrieve from the 2018 Wikipedia snapshot processed following Karpukhin et al. (2020). The corpus is indexed with 

intfloat/e5-base-v2 (Wang et al., 2024) using an exact flat index. We follow the corpus and top-k configuration of Jin et al. (2025b) unchanged across training and evaluation. For CCR similarity computation, the default ablation variants use the same e5-base-v2 encoder as a frozen text encoder; the (qwenemb) variants instead use 

Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B (Zhang et al., 2025). 

A.7 Per-Phase Search-Reasoning 

Within each phase, the model iterates between rea-soning, retrieval, and answering. The explicit tag structure separates reasoning from tool calls, makes search actions observable to the environment, and allows retrieved passages to be inserted determin-istically as part of the assistant turn rather than as model-generated text. At each inner-loop turn in phase k, the model first emits a reasoning block inside <think> or <reason> , depending on the model format. It then either issues a search query in 

<search> or produces an answer in <answer> . A search action invokes the retriever, and the returned passages are appended as an <information> block within the same assistant turn. The policy can there-fore perform multiple search-read cycles within a phase. Phase k terminates when the model emits an answer or exhausts its search budget Tk.

A.8 Mid-Response User Intervention 

When a non-final phase k < K terminates, the cur-rent assistant turn is closed, the next user turn uk+1 

is appended, and generation resumes from a new as-sistant turn. The full previous context is preserved, including reasoning, search queries, and retrieved passages. This design matches the target interac-tion setting: a user interrupts an ongoing search process, and the model must continue from the accumulated evidence rather than restarting from scratch. 

A.9 Intermediate Answer Removal and Final-Phase Generation 

Intermediate answer removal prevents a shortcut in the training environment. During a non-final phase, the model may emit an <answer> before the full intervention chain is complete. If this premature answer were left in the trajectory, the policy could learn to terminate early instead of continuing to later user goals. Therefore, for any phase k < K , the premature <think><answer> 

or <reason><answer> turn is removed from the policy-gradient loss by masking its token positions. Earlier reasoning and retrieval turns in the same phase are retained, preserving useful search behav-ior. After deleting the premature answer, the next intervention uk+1 is injected as if the phase had ended by budget exhaustion. This forces the roll-out to continue through the complete sub-question chain. If the model exhausts the final-phase bud-get TK without producing an <answer> tag, we run one additional generation pass with search dis-abled. This ensures that every trajectory yields a final prediction for reward computation.

A.11 Baselines 

The primary baseline is Search-R1 (Jin et al., 2025b). Search-R1 trains the model to answer a fixed user question through iterative reasoning and retrieval. It uses the same base model, re-triever, PPO framework, and reward format as STEER SEARCH , but does not include mid-response sub-question injection. This makes it the most di-rect comparison for testing whether interactive tra-jectory training improves steering beyond ordinary search-augmented reasoning. We also report the Vanilla model, which de-notes the original instruction-tuned checkpoint without SFT or RL training in our environment. On the Standard Benchmark, we include repre-sentative retrieval-augmented baselines reported by Jin et al. (2025b): direct inference, chain-of-thought prompting (CoT), interleaved retrieval and reasoning (IRCoT) (Trivedi et al., 2023), Search-o1 (Li et al., 2025), retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), supervised fine-tuning (SFT), DeepSeekR1-instruct (Guo et al., 2025), and rejec-tion sampling (Ahn et al., 2024). These methods contextualize performance on standard QA, while Search-R1 remains the controlled baseline for the mid-response steering setting.

D.1 Matched No-Injection Training Control 

The inference-time diagnostics in Table 3 keep the original Search-R1 and S TEER SEARCH train-ing procedures fixed. To directly test whether the main gains arise from exposure to decomposed multi-hop content rather than mid-response tar-get reassignment, we evaluate additional controls on S TEER SEARCH -B ENCH . For the concat-chain training control, we retrain Search-R1 using the same SFT initialization and 40K PPO pool as STEER SEARCH , but concatenate the decomposed sub-question chains upfront without mid-response injection. The base model, retriever, training bud-get, and final-answer reward are otherwise matched, while S TEER SEARCH retains its standard injected-trajectory training. The remaining controls evalu-ate upfront concatenation and the first sub-question without additional training. As shown in Table 16, matching Search-R1 to the decomposed training content substantially strengthens the baseline but does not close the gap on S TEER SEARCH -B ENCH . S TEER SEARCH 

retains advantages of 14.0 percentage points on Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct and 10.3 percentage points on Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507. The residual differ-ence therefore cannot be explained by decomposed multi-hop exposure alone and supports the contri-bution of presenting target changes as interventions within an active trajectory.

E.1 CCR Configuration Details 

CCR measures whether reasoning after an inter-vention becomes more aligned with the updated user goal than with the preceding context. The evaluated configurations differ in how this contrast is constructed and where the resulting auxiliary reward is applied. 

Context Variants. The individual variant com-pares consecutive user goals, 

pk+1 = uk+1 , nk = uk,

while the cumulative variant compares the user-goal history before and after the intervention, 

pk+1 = u1 ⊕ · · · ⊕ uk+1 , nk = u1 ⊕ · · · ⊕ uk,

where ⊕ denotes concatenation. Individual CCR therefore provides a local contrast between adja-cent goals, whereas cumulative CCR retains earlier user context that may be required to interpret later interventions. 

Reasoning Scope and Reward Assignment. 

The Last-think configuration computes CCR from the final reasoning block generated after each in-tervention. All-thinks instead scores every reason-ing block within that phase, providing denser post-intervention supervision. In the Terminal configura-tion, CCR is computed across the reasoning blocks associated with each intervention phase and the ac-cumulated phase rewards are assigned to the final answer token. Tables 19 and 20 use this shorthand for the evaluated configurations; these experiments are not intended as a complete Cartesian sweep over all possible scoring and assignment choices. 

Embedding Model. Unless otherwise spec-ified, CCR similarity is computed using the frozen intfloat/e5-base-v2 encoder (Wang et al., 2024). We additionally evaluate 

Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B (Zhang et al., 2025), a text embedding model built on Qwen3 founda-tion models and trained for diverse embedding and ranking tasks. Since our policy models generate the reasoning traces being evaluated, we hypoth-esize that representations from a Qwen-based en-coder may provide a useful alternative for measur-ing alignment between these traces and the evolv-ing user context. This is a motivation for the en-coder comparison, rather than an assumption that shared model-family membership guarantees bet-ter alignment. E5 is also a general-purpose em-bedding model trained on heterogeneous text pairs, including retrieval and natural-language inference data (Wang et al., 2024). The retriever remains unchanged in all experiments; Qwen3-Embedding is used only to compute the CCR similarity signal.
