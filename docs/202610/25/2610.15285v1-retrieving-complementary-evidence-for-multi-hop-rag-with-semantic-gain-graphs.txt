ABSTRACT 

Multi-hop retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) requires finding documents whose evidence is useful in combination. Query relevance and graph connectivity help locate related content, but neither directly measures the additional informa-tion one document contributes given another. We introduce SG 2RAG, a frame-work that represents this complementarity through semantic gain. Offline, a large language model assesses the relevant and nonredundant information contributed by one chunk given another within neighborhoods proposed by semantic simi-larity. These ordered assessments are aggregated into symmetric edge weights to construct a reusable Semantic Gain Graph. Online, controlled Personalized PageRank starts from query-relevant seeds and ranks complementary evidence beyond the initial retrieval set. Experiments on MuSiQue, 2Wiki, and HotpotQA with two language model backbones show that SG 2RAG achieves the best aver-age EM, F1, and Recall@10 among the evaluated single-retrieval methods. Under identical candidate support and propagation, semantic gain improves average F1 and Recall@10 over similarity weighting by 4.08 and 4.31 points. Analyses of supporting documents and improvements in three iterative retrieval frameworks further support its effectiveness as a reusable retrieval component. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) (Lewis et al., 2020) has become an important paradigm for mitigating hallucinations in Large Language Models (LLMs) and supporting knowledge-intensive applications (Song & Lee, 2026; Chu et al., 2026). By incorporating external evidence, RAG im-proves the groundedness and reliability of LLM outputs, making knowledge organization and re-trieval effectiveness critical to its reasoning capability (Li et al., 2025). Early studies improve re-trieval by preserving the semantic coherence of text snippets through heuristic chunking (Hostnik & Robnik- ˇ Sikonja, 2025; Zhao et al., 2025) and summarization (Ji et al., 2024; Hosking et al., 2024). However, these methods remain overly reliant on semantic similarity. In multi-hop reasoning (Wei et al., 2025), key evidence is distributed across documents, and a document with weak query similar-ity may become critical once another document is known. Because similarity-based retrievers score each document primarily against the query, they do not directly represent how documents complete one another. Iterative methods expand the context through multi-round retrieval-generation interac-tions (Shao et al., 2023; Yue et al., 2025), but introduce substantial inference overhead while leaving cross-document complementarity implicit. To better organize complex evidence structures, recent studies have introduced graph-based retrieval into RAG (Wang et al., 2026), moving from similarity-based retrieval toward topology-based evi-dence expansion. LightRAG (Guo et al., 2024), for example, constructs LLM-assisted entity-relation graphs and retrieves contexts through entity linking and graph traversal. This topology extends re-trieval beyond direct query matches, but connectivity does not reveal which neighboring documents contribute useful evidence. Methods such as HippoRAG2 (Jimenez Gutierrez et al., 2024; Guti´ errez et al., 2025) incorporate query-document relevance to constrain the traversal scope, yet still rely on entity-level relations organized by LLMs rather than explicitly assessing cross-document comple-mentarity. Existing retrievers therefore locate evidence through query relevance or expand it through graph connectivity, but neither makes cross-document complemen-tarity a first-class retrieval relation. To address this gap, we propose SG 2RAG , a Semantic Gain Graph based Retrieval-Augmented 

Generation framework. SG 2RAG makes cross-document complementarity a first-class retrieval re-lation, moving evidence discovery beyond independent query-document matching and topology-only expansion. It represents document chunks as nodes and estimates the relevant, novel, and significant evidence that one chunk contributes when another is available. These ordered assess-ments are aggregated into pair-level strengths that form a reusable Semantic Gain Graph. Similarity provides efficient candidate support and query entry points, while semantic gain defines the graph relation and routing preference. During retrieval, controlled Personalized PageRank (PPR) (Page et al., 1999) composes local gain-weighted connections into a multi-hop evidence ranking anchored to the query. Our contributions are threefold. (1) We formulate multi-hop evidence discovery around document-level conditional evidence contribution as an explicit retrieval objective rather than inde-pendent query relevance or topology alone. (2) We introduce the Semantic Gain Graph as a reusable complementarity index whose pair strengths are derived from ordered evidence-contribution assess-ments, and develop query-anchored controlled PPR to compose these relations into globally coordi-nated multi-hop evidence rankings. (3) Extensive experiments across two LLM backbones show that SG 2RAG achieves the best average performance, isolate semantic-gain weighting under fixed graph support and propagation, validate the score with gold supporting-document pairs, and demonstrate modularity in iterative retrieval frameworks. 

2 RELATED WORK 

2.1 RETRIEVAL -A UGMENTED GENERATION 

RAG grounds LLM generation in external knowledge, improving factuality and reducing halluci-nations in knowledge-intensive tasks (Lewis et al., 2020; Vidyarthi et al., 2026; Jiang et al., 2025). Retrieval advances refine knowledge units through fine-grained segmentation, as in MetaChunking (Zhao et al., 2024) and SAKIRAG (Tao et al., 2025), and semantic chunking (Moreno-Cediel et al., 2025), while dense embeddings efficiently match these units to queries (Lee et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025). Although effective for locally self-contained evidence, this flat, similarity-driven pipeline struggles with multi-hop reasoning, where supporting evidence is distributed across implicitly re-lated chunks and may have low query similarity (Wei et al., 2025). Iterative methods, including iterDRAG (Yue et al., 2025), RT-RAG (Shi et al., 2026), and iterRetGen (Shao et al., 2023), address this limitation through query rewriting, question decomposition, and repeated retrieval-generation interactions. The online stage uses query-relevant seeds to anchor controlled PPR over these complementarity relations. 2.2 GRAPH -BASED RAG Graph-based RAG addresses the limitations of flat retrieval by organizing knowledge into structured indexes. RAPTOR (Sarthi et al., 2024) builds a hierarchical tree through recursive clustering and summarization. GraphRAG (Edge et al., 2024) and LightRAG (Guo et al., 2024) construct entity-relation graphs for entity linking and graph expansion. Building on such structures, KG 2RAG (Zhu et al., 2025) expands semantically retrieved seeds under knowledge graph guidance and organizes the evidence into coherent paragraphs. Together, these methods capture associations beyond surface similarity. However, most graph-based retrievers remain governed by topology. Unconstrained expansion can introduce noisy nodes and retrieval drift. PathRAG (Chen et al., 2026) uses flow-based pruning to remove redundant information from retrieved relational paths. LinearRAG (Zhuang et al., 2025) and HippoRAG2 (Jimenez Gutierrez et al., 2024; Guti´ errez et al., 2025) restrict traversal to improve topic relevance, but still assume that topological neighbors are useful without explicitly estimating document complementarity. GraphReader (Li et al., 2024) and GeAR (Shen et al., 2025) improve control through LLM agents that direct graph traversal, at the cost of additional online inference and tighter coupling to agentic reasoning. SG 2RAG instead makes estimated evidence contribution part of a reusable document graph, rather than treating complementarity as an implicit consequence of topology or a query-time ranking signal. 

3 PRELIMINARIES 

Given a corpus D = {di}Ni=1 , conventional Graph-RAG methods transform unstructured documents into a relation graph Grel = ( Vrel , Erel ) through information extraction (Edge et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2024; Guti´ errez et al., 2025). The nodes represent knowledge units such as entities and phrases, while the edges encode extracted relations, shared mentions, or other associations. Given a query q, semantic matching identifies query-relevant seed nodes Vq ⊆ V rel , and graph propagation ranks additional nodes. Retrieval is therefore governed largely by topology induced from recurring mentions and extracted relations. In contrast, SG 2RAG represents each document chunk di as a node 

vi and assigns an ordered assessment wij to the useful and nonredundant evidence contributed by dj

when di is available. The ordered records are aggregated into a symmetric pair strength aij for graph traversal. Thus, assessment direction specifies how evidence contribution is measured, while pair strength determines how the graph is traversed. Query matching locates entry nodes, and controlled PPR (Page et al., 1999) ranks graph-reachable evidence over this reusable complementarity index. 34 METHODOLOGY 

This section presents SG 2RAG as a complementarity-indexed retrieval framework with offline Se-mantic Gain Graph construction and online query-anchored controlled PPR, as shown in Figure 2. 4.1 SEMANTIC GAIN GRAPH CONSTRUCTION 

Given a corpus, SG 2RAG first segments documents into chunks and represents each chunk as a graph node v ∈ V . Because user queries are unavailable during offline construction, SG 2RAG pre-computes reusable query-independent complementarity relations, separating corpus-level evidence completion from query-specific seed selection. To avoid exhaustive comparison among all chunk pairs, we adopt a coarse-to-fine graph construction strategy. Let E ∈ RN ×d denote the embedding matrix of all N chunks. By evaluating the top-n candidates of each node jointly in a single LLM re-quest, graph construction requires only N LLM requests in total. For each node vi, we first retrieve its top-n nearest neighbors according to cosine similarity: 

N (vi) = Top-n (cos( ei, E)) , (1) where N (vi) denotes the local semantic neighborhood of vi. Similarity sparsifies the pairs to be assessed, while semantic gain assigns their retrieval utility. Similarity therefore defines candidate support rather than final routing, allowing graph search to move beyond direct query similarity within that support. Inspired by conditional gain in set functions (Iyer et al., 2021) and novelty-aware selection (Car-bonell & Goldstein, 1998), we define semantic gain as an operational measure of conditional evi-dence contribution. For an ordered pair, it scores the relevant, novel, and significant information that 

vj adds when vi is available. Unlike query-conditioned reranking, this assessment defines a reusable document-to-document relation without reference to a query. An LLM-based scorer Msg interprets each baseline-candidate pair under a fixed rubric R and returns the bounded score record

5 EXPERIMENTS 

5.1 DATASETS 

We evaluate our method on the curated MuSiQue (Trivedi et al., 2022), HotpotQA (Yang et al., 2018), and 2WikiMultiHopQA (2Wiki) (Ho et al., 2020) datasets provided by HippoRAG2 (Guti´ errez et al., 2025). Each dataset contains 1000 queries together with supporting documents, distractors, and ground-truth answers for retrieval and QA evaluation. The document collections contain 11656 documents for MuSiQue, 9811 for HotpotQA, and 6119 for 2Wiki. MuSiQue is considered more challenging, while HotpotQA is relatively easier. 5.2 BASELINES 

We compare SG 2RAG with four groups of representative baselines. First, we evaluate pure LLMs without retrieval, including Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct (abbreviated as Qwen-72B ) (Yang et al., 2024) and Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct (abbreviated as Llama-70B ) (Grattafiori et al., 2024). Sec-ond, we consider standard dense-retrieval RAG methods using BGE-M3 (Chen et al., 2024), 

text-embedding-v3 1, and Qwen3-Embedding-8B (abbreviated as Qwen3-Emb ) (Zhang et al., 2025). We additionally construct a strong Qwen3-Emb w/ Reranker baseline to test whether query-conditioned LLM reranking can account for the gains of SG 2RAG. Third, we compare with structure-augmented retrieval methods, including LightRAG (Guo et al., 2024), PathRAG (Chen et al., 2026), KG 2RAG (Zhu et al., 2025), HippoRAG2 (Guti´ errez et al., 2025), and RAPTOR 

(Sarthi et al., 2024). HippoRAG2 uses F ilter by default, and KG 2RAG also enables its filtering module, implemented as a reranker. Finally, to evaluate the modularity of SG 2RAG, we compare it with representative iterative retrieval methods, including GeAR (Shen et al., 2025), iterDRAG (Yue et al., 2025), iterRetGen (Shao et al., 2023), and RT-RAG (Shi et al., 2026).

5.3 IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS 

We evaluate two unified LLM backbone settings using Qwen-72B and Llama-70B, with Qwen3-Emb as the shared embedding model. Within each setting, the same LLM is used for all applicable LLM-dependent stages, including graph construction, semantic-gain scoring, reranking or filtering, and answer generation. For the main comparison in Table 1, SG 2RAG and HippoRAG2 use their LLM-based F ilter to ensure a fair comparison under the same filtered-retrieval setting. KG 2RAG retains its built-in reranker, which is required by its original pipeline. We also report the unfiltered SG 2RAG variant to isolate the performance of semantic-gain-guided propagation. All subsequent ablation experiments disable F ilter so that changes can be attributed to graph weighting and prop-agation rather than additional LLM filtering. All methods report Exact Match (EM), F1, and Re-call@10. EM and F1 measure final QA performance, while Recall@10 measures the coverage of annotated supporting documents. 5.4 MAIN RESULTS 

Single Retrieval and Generation. Table 1 reports results under the Qwen-72B and Llama-70B backbone settings. Among the dense retrievers, Qwen3-Emb provides the strongest overall per-formance. Adding the matched LLM reranker improves its average EM and F1 by 2.33 and 2.17 points with Qwen-72B, while reducing Recall by 1.61 points. With Llama-70B, it yields only a 0.42-point EM gain, leaves F1 essentially unchanged, and reduces Recall by 1.30 points. This pat-tern exposes a structural limitation of reranking. It can only reorder documents already returned by dense retrieval and cannot reach missing support, which is particularly restrictive when multi-hop evidence has weak direct similarity to the query. SG 2RAG instead builds a reusable complemen-tarity index offline. Query similarity locates entry seeds, and controlled PPR follows semantic-gain connections to rank graph-reachable evidence beyond the initial dense list. Even without F ilter ,SG 2RAG outperforms Qwen3-Emb with the matched reranker by 2.07, 2.49, and 4.22 points in average EM, F1, and Recall with Qwen-72B, with corresponding gains of 2.47, 3.16, and 4.15 points under Llama-70B. Because no additional query-time filtering is used, this comparison dis-tinguishes complementarity-indexed graph retrieval from LLM reranking . Figure 3 compares alternative treatments of the same candidate pairs using the Qwen-72B results of SG 2RAG. The default Pair Sum aggregates the available ordered assessments into symmetric pair strengths. Pair Mean, la-beled Symmetric in the figure, averages those assessments instead and remains within 0.60 points of Pair Sum across all metrics. This small difference shows that retrieval is robust to how multiple assessments on a pair are ag-gregated. Directed instead interprets each or-dered assessment as a directed transition arc. Reversed flips those arcs while preserving their weights, and Random Swap randomizes their as-signment. These directed treatments produce larger reductions because Top-n proposal membership is not necessarily reciprocal. A missing reverse record represents an unobserved assessment, but directed PPR turns it into a hard one-way reachability constraint. The pair-level variants retain the observed conditional assessments in the connection strength while allowing traversal from ei-ther endpoint. The results therefore support the distinction between ordered evidence-contribution assessment and symmetric graph traversal. 5.7 VALIDITY OF SEMANTIC GAIN 

We evaluate semantic gain across all three benchmarks using gold supporting annotations only for post-hoc analysis. A G–G record links two documents annotated as supporting evidence for the same query, while G–D and D–D records are defined by the endpoint annotations. This analysis uses the original ordered assessment wij before pair aggregation, so Pair Sum, Pair Mean, and directional transition treatments do not change the score analyzed here. Supporting-document membership provides an empirical proxy for useful cross-document complementarity and is never used during 8graph construction. The upper panels of Figure 4 show the same G–G > G–D > D–D ordering across datasets. The respective mean scores are 0.44, 0.33, and 0.26 on MuSiQue, 0.63, 0.37, and 0.25 on 2Wiki, and 0.52, 0.37, and 0.33 on HotpotQA. All G–G differences from the comparison groups are statistically significant. This consistent hierarchy shows that the scorer assigns larger values to records whose endpoints contain more answer-supporting information. The lower panels treat G–G as positive and G–D as negative. Semantic gain obtains AUC values of 0.638, 0.759, and 0.701 on MuSiQue, 2Wiki, and HotpotQA, compared with 0.456, 0.449, and 0.548 for cosine similarity. Because both signals are evaluated on the same candidate records, the improvements of 0.182, 0.310, and 0.153 show that semantic gain encodes an evidence-contribution relation not captured by similarity alone . 6 CONCLUSION 

We presented SG 2RAG, which organizes retrieval around the additional evidence that document chunks contribute to one another. Its Semantic Gain Graph aggregates ordered assessments of ev-idence contribution into symmetric connection strengths, while controlled Personalized PageRank uses query-relevant seeds to guide retrieval across the graph. Results on three multi-hop question answering benchmarks with two language model backbones demonstrate improvements over the evaluated dense retrieval and graph-based baselines. Comparisons with identical candidate pairs and propagation isolate the contribution of semantic gain, while experiments with iterative retrieval demonstrate its usefulness beyond a single retrieval round. The current graph is constructed offline within similarity-based neighborhoods, so retrieval remains limited by the connections established before a query is available. Future work will explore adaptive candidate expansion and query-aware graph refinement to better capture evidence needs that emerge during retrieval. 9REFERENCES
