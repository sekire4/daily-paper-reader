## Abstract 

Language-model agents increasingly solve complex tasks through parallel reasoning and asynchronous information gathering. Con-trolled replay across two model families and three benchmarks shows that delivery timing redirects search decisions made before all results arrive, despite identical initial evidence. Acquisition dis-agreement reaches up to 24 percentage points above same-order repeat variation. This dependence persists after all initial evidence arrives and through a second search, turning temporary differ-ences in what agents have seen into lasting differences in what they acquire. An acquisition-regret bound with a matching con-struction shows that invariance at a fixed early arrival count can limit the use of evidence already received. We introduce Confluent Evidence Acquisition (CEA), which instead releases local reason-ing when each subquestion’s evidence is complete and allocates shared search independently of completion order. For a fixed plan and stable conditional response law, we prove joint invariance of decisions, acquisitions, and answers. With question-only plans across three benchmarks, CEA brings acquisition disagreement to within 1.0 percentage point of repeat variation and achieves accu-racy comparable to complete-input controls, improving over early commitment by up to 12.6 percentage points. Replacing CEA’s fixed allocation with first-ready allocation under contention adds 21.6 percentage points of acquisition disagreement, while local query-target disagreement remains near repeat variation. Timed comparisons show that CEA reaches decisions earlier and re-sponds faster than the same program waiting for all initial evi-dence.

Across three benchmarks and two model families, early commitment produces acquisition disagreement up to 24 percentage points above same-order repeat variation. In the two-search evaluation, early commitment improves accuracy but leaves acquisition more sensitive to timing. Average accuracy captures task success but cannot establish whether supporting evidence is acquired reproducibly, a distinction that matters when auditing an agent’s conclusions [37]. These findings motivate acquisition that is both useful and sta-ble. We formalize schedule invariance as an unchanged acquisition or answer distribution across delivery orders. Our acquisition-regret bound, with a matching construction, shows that enforcing invariance at a fixed early arrival count can restrict the use of informative evidence already received. A shared-budget coun-terexample shows that even invariant local proposals can yield different acquisitions when completion order determines which query executes. ## 2 Problem and Evaluation Setup 

We replay fixed evidence packets over a frozen corpus to isolate delivery-order effects on acquisition and task success from changes in retrieval content or service latency.

We dis-tinguish supplied evidence from evidence acquired through search and assess invariance for search decisions, retrieved document sets, and answers. 

An evidence acquisition task. Answering each question requires evidence from multiple documents. We withhold one supporting document from the supplied packet while keeping it in the retrieval corpus. The six-item packet contains the remaining support and re-trieved distractors. A dependent chain retains the bridge document but lacks an endpoint, whereas a comparison lacks support for one side. ## 3 How Scheduling Shapes Information Acquisition 

A search decision determines what additional evidence an agent acquires. We trace how schedule dependence enters acquisition, persists after input completion, and affects evidence quality, then characterize the cost of invariance at a fixed early arrival count. 

## 3.1 Scheduling Selects the Evidence for Search 

Delivery can affect a search decision through the evidence avail-able at commitment or the order in which it is processed. Figure 2 shows the primary analysis; Appendix Figure E1 reports the GPT replication. Batch 6/6 ( wait_all@6 ) presents all six documents in a fixed order, whereas Notes 6/6 ( native@6 ) accumulates notes in arrival order. Both show little excess retrieval disagreement when all six documents are available for the first decision. The pattern changes when decisions are made after only two ar-rivals (Notes 2/6, native@2 ). Retrieved-set disagreement exceeds same-order repeat variation by 11.6–23.8 pp across all eight model– task settings. Different delivery orders expose the decision to dif-ferent subsets of the same original packet. Because the retriever is deterministic and no retrieval feedback precedes this decision, the schedule effect enters through whether and what the agent chooses to search. To distinguish the selected evidence from its presentation, Batch 2/6 ( wait_all@2 ) presents the first two arrivals together in document-identifier order. Substantial sensitivity remains in ev-ery tested setting. On 2Wiki compositional questions, the primary evaluation yields 21.7 pp of excess disagreement, versus 21.6 pp with incremental processing. The same pattern appears in the GPT replication, with early incremental/batch contrasts of 12.7/12.5 pp on HotpotQA and 11.7/11.2 pp on MuSiQue (Appendix Table E3). Sorting fixes presentation but cannot make evidence still in flight available to the decision. Scheduling also changes access to useful support. Under early commitment, disagreement about whether the missing document is recovered exceeds the repeat baseline by 7.7 pp on bridge com-parison and 19.3 pp on compositional questions in the primary evaluation (Appendix E). These controls identify temporal evi-dence selection , in which timing shapes the evidence used to form a query and redirects corpus access despite a fixed packet and retrieval rule.

## 3.3 Acquisition Quality Beyond Final Accuracy 

We next examine what these acquisition differences mean for sup-port recovery and answer accuracy. Under the single-search bud-get, waiting reduces search use and increases recovery per search across all eight incremental settings (Table 2). Both agents also re-cover more support overall and answer more accurately on both 2Wiki tasks. In the primary bridge-comparison evaluation, recov-ery rises from 13.2% to 79.6%, alongside a 30.3 pp accuracy gain. Search productivity improves across tasks even though accuracy changes are much smaller on HotpotQA and MuSiQue. In the two-search evaluation, early commitment reaches 43.6% accuracy versus 40.9% for complete-input commitment, a paired gain of 2.7 pp [0.4, 5.1]. It uses 1.54 versus 1.13 searches per trajec-tory, with support recovery at 84.4% and 83.5%. Higher accuracy and frequent support recovery coexist with greater schedule sen-sitivity in the acquired evidence (Section 3.2). The complete-input controls clarify what a stable policy should preserve. On the primary bridge-comparison task, batch reasoning almost never searches and reaches 66.4% accuracy, versus 89.8% for incremental reasoning. Both have low sensitivity, but incremen-tal reasoning recovers more support (Appendix Table E1). Stabil-ity is compatible with productive retrieval; the reasoning program shapes how effectively the available evidence guides search. Different evidence paths can produce the same correct answer (Appendix B). The design objective is to preserve useful acquisition while making evidence selection insensitive to delivery timing.

## 4 Confluent Evidence Acquisition 

We introduce Confluent Evidence Acquisition (CEA), which coordi-nates reasoning and search through their evidence and resource dependencies while allowing independent work to proceed. 

## 4.1 Evidence and Resource Dependencies 

CEA organizes reasoning around the inputs assigned to each sub-question. A branch handles one subquestion, such as establishing a person’s birthplace in a comparison. The subquestion and its as-signed initial documents form a dependency domain , fixed before delivery. A branch begins reasoning once all documents in its domain have arrived, while other branches may still await their inputs. Changing delivery order changes when this branch is ready, but not which initial evidence it uses. CEA replaces commitment af-ter a fixed arrival count with completion of the decision’s inputs (Section 3.4). Those inputs may still leave facts unresolved, so the branch can request further retrieval. In an explicit task graph [20, 39], retrieval calls identify which evidence belongs to each subquestion. Our controlled experiments use annotated chains, with two domains for comparisons and one for dependent chains. Subquestions provide starting entities and relations without revealing annotated intermediates or answers.

## 4.3 Joint Invariance and Search Release 

We call joint schedule invariance of local decisions, executed ac-quisitions, and the final answer epistemic confluence . Let 𝑋 be a complete request, 𝑌 its response, and 𝐻 the preceding execution history. Assumption (A-K) states that ℒ(𝑌 ∣ 𝑋 , 𝐻 ) = 𝐾 (⋅ ∣ 𝑋 ) at fixed model settings, so the response distribution depends only on the request. With deterministic retrieval, these conditions yield the following guarantee. PRoposition 4.1 (Epistemic confluence of CEA). Under these conditions, CEA preserves the joint law of domain-indexed decisions, executed acquisitions, and the final answer across permutations of the original packet. Readiness and tool-release positions may change. 

To see why, couple corresponding logical calls with seeds shared across schedules and independent across calls. Each branch processes identical domain inputs in the same order, so induction gives matching private histories and proposals. Stable allocation selects the same requester. Deterministic retrieval, private feed-back, and canonical synthesis then preserve agreement through the final answer. Conditioning on a plan generated independently of delivery and averaging over plans preserves the guarantee. The same dependencies determine how early search can start. ## 5 Stable Acquisition with Asynchronous Progress 

CEA aims to retain the stability and answer quality of complete-input reasoning while allowing independent work to proceed. We test stability and search allocation, then evaluate question-only plans. Evidence recovery, matched reasoning controls, and timed execution assess useful search and efficiency (Figure 4). 

## 5.1 Stability from Retrieval to Answers 

We first compare CEA with early commitment on controlled bridge comparisons. Excess acquisition disagreement falls from 21.5 to 

−0.2 pp with DeepSeek V4 and from 24.4 to −0.7 pp in the matched GPT evaluation. Changing delivery order now produces about as much variation as repeating the same order. The corresponding answer contrasts are −0.6 and −0.3 pp. On dependent chains, pri-mary answer disagreement also falls from 12.1 to 1.0 pp (Appendix Table E1). Stable queries and stable acquisitions are distinct requirements. A branch may form the same request under every schedule, yet lose the opportunity to execute it when another branch finishes first. To test why stable query formation needs stable allocation, we keep local reasoning fixed and give the shared search slot to the first completed request (Table 3). With one supporting docu-ment withheld, only about 1% of trajectories have two requesters, so fixed priority and first-ready rarely choose between competing queries. With terminal support withheld from each branch on the same 100 questions, contention reaches 80.9–82.5%. Local query-target disagreement remains near repeat variation under both rules, yet first-ready adds 21.6 pp [18.3, 24.8] of acquisition disagreement, with similar accuracy. The winning request determines which new evidence enters the selected branch and, through its answer, the fi-nal synthesis.

## 5.3 Useful Evidence and Efficient Reasoning 

On the controlled two-domain task, withheld-support recovery measures whether stable acquisition remains useful (Section 3.3). CEA raises recovery from 79.6% to 86.4% with DeepSeek V4 and from 63.0% to 81.4% in the matched GPT evaluation, relative to complete-input native@6 . The gain comes mainly from more fre-quent productive searches. CEA’s corresponding accuracies are 90.5% versus 89.8% and 87.7% versus 76.4%, respectively (Appen-dix Tables E1 and E2). We next isolate the contribution of private histories. A matched 100-question GPT control makes six note-taking calls over canon-ically ordered evidence for the full question and passes the shared history to both subquestion decisions. CEA retains only each do-main’s evidence and notes. The reasoning-call structure, allocation, feedback, and synthesis are matched. Both use 9.9 calls per trajec-tory, but CEA uses 6,877 input tokens versus 11,505, a 40.2% re-duction, with accuracies of 87.3% and 86.0% (Figure 4b). Keeping histories local avoids repeatedly carrying other subquestions’ evi-dence and notes into later calls. By combining subquestion outputs only at final synthesis, CEA uses the same dependency boundaries to stabilize reasoning and limit the context carried through local calls. CEA can also coordinate different local reasoning programs. In-cremental note-taking raises primary accuracy from 67.2% with Batch to 90.5%; GPT Batch reaches 89.5% versus 87.3% with incre-mental reasoning (Appendix Table E2). GPT acquisition contrasts remain near repeat variation with CEA, CEA (Batch), and shared history. CEA’s coordination rules accommodate either program, al-lowing local reasoning to be chosen for answer quality while pre-serving stable acquisition. 

## 5.4 Progress through Local Completion 

We time the same CEA program under two release rules. Local com-pletion starts each domain’s reasoning once all its inputs arrive; the global barrier waits for all initial evidence. On the two-domain task, first subquestion decisions finish 8.7 s earlier with GPT (100 ques-tions) and 8.3 s earlier with DeepSeek V4 (20 questions). Across 800 matched GPT pairs, final-response time falls from 27.3 to 26.5 s, a paired 3.1% reduction [2.4, 3.8]. Accuracy is 88.3% with local com-pletion and 88.1% with the barrier. DeepSeek V4 saves 3.9% [1.9, 5.9] in response time (Figure 4c). Local reasoning can finish during delivery, but final synthesis waits for all branch outputs and selected retrieval feedback (Sec-tion 4.3). This explains why local decisions advance more than fi-nal responses. All three staggered arrival profiles show positive mean savings in both agents, while simultaneous delivery gives es-sentially none (Appendix Table E2). This pattern ties the speedup to overlap between reasoning and delivery. Appendix Table E3 re-ports when the shared search budget permits retrieval to begin. 

## 6 Related Work 

Evidence acquisition in search agents. Interactive search treats queries as evolving with acquired information [4]. Language-model search agents build on neural retrieval [18] and gener-ation conditioned on retrieved evidence [13, 22]. Adaptive re-trieval [2, 14, 15], browsing agents [23, 28], and learned tool-use policies [5, 16, 31] decide when and what to retrieve during reason-ing. Decomposition and interleaved reasoning guide subsequent queries [29, 36, 42]. GAIA, AgentBench, and BrowseComp eval-uate general-assistant tasks, interactive environments, and diffi-cult Web information seeking [25, 27, 38]. We complement these evaluations by examining evidence-path stability across delivery schedules. 

Asynchronous execution and timing sensitivity. ReWOO sepa-rates planning, tool execution, and synthesis through evidence dependencies [39]. LLMCompiler dispatches dependency-ready tasks concurrently [20], and RAG-R1 learns multi-query paral-lelism [34]. AsyncLM incorporates tool returns into ongoing gen-eration [8], while Speculative Interaction Agents reason during outstanding calls and revise pending work [11]. Demonstration order [26], evidence position [24], and irrelevant context [33] influence reasoning. Multi-turn studies identify retained assump-tions [21] and divergent information-seeking paths [12]. Concur-rent work on ChronosAttack studies timing attacks on authentic tool responses [37]. Its causal barrier buffers a decision’s obser-vations for canonical presentation, sharing the complete-input waiting principle of our global-barrier control. We trace how tim-ing affects subsequent corpus access. CEA coordinates local evi-dence dependencies and shared search allocation while allowing independent branches to progress. 

Confluence and dependency coordination. Concurrency theory characterizes when execution order can vary without changing results. Kahn networks establish determinism under a fixed com-munication discipline [17]; conflict-free replicated data types con-verge through structured updates and merges [32]. CALM links coordination-free consistency to monotonicity [1, 9], while invari-ant confluence characterizes coordination requirements for appli-cation invariants [3]. Recent work analyzes shared-state anomalies and concurrent external effects in multi-agent systems [19]. CEA

## 7 Conclusion 

Delivery timing shapes which evidence search agents acquire from identical initial inputs, with differences that persist through in-put completion and a second search. Timing influences both the evidence available when queries are formed and which proposed query receives a shared search slot. Acquisition stability captures the reproducibility of the evidence behind an answer, complement-ing task accuracy. Our acquisition-regret bound explains why invariance at a fixed early arrival count can restrict the use of informative evidence already received. CEA instead coordinates local evidence com-pletion and shared search allocation. For a fixed plan and stable conditional response law, it guarantees joint invariance of deci-sions, acquisitions, and answers while allowing independent work to proceed. With question-only plans, CEA retains acquisition sta-bility and answer quality comparable to complete-input controls. Matched evaluations show lower context cost with private his-tories and earlier decisions and responses with local completion. Coordinating each decision’s evidence and resource dependencies supports useful, reproducible acquisition without a global barrier while independent branches progress.

## A Measurement Protocol 

Tasks and retrieval. Questions come from the 2Wiki and MuSiQue development sets and HotpotQA’s distractor validation set. Corpus sizes are 398,354 (2Wiki), 509,315 (HotpotQA), and 117,534 (MuSiQue) paragraphs. Eligibility requires top-five re-trieval of withheld support using its original title. The agent never receives this query. Non-supporting documents retrieved with the question fill the remaining slots. Duplicate excerpts, truncated support, and answer-exposing chains are excluded; distractors are not answer-filtered. SQLite FTS5 BM25 ( 𝑘 1 = 1.2 , 𝑏 = 0.75 , equal title/body weights, ties broken by identifier) combines sorted unique Unicode alphanumeric tokens with OR, without stemming or stopword removal. A frozen tokenizer caps excerpts at 200 tokens; notes allow 256 output tokens and decisions/answers 512. 

Models and probes. DeepSeek-V4-Flash uses temperature 0, top-

𝑝 1, and non-thinking mode. GPT-5.6 Luna uses low reasoning effort without temperature/top-𝑝 settings. Unused early search opportunities expire, but delivery continues after answering. The completed-input probe uses the decision prompt without a budget notification. Target-eligible 𝑛 is 195, 200, 75 in Figure 2(a)–(c), and 98, 100 in Figure E1(a)–(b). 

Orders and estimation. We use eight orders (canonical, adjacent swaps, half-packet rotation, reversal), two repeats per order, and fixed identifiers and text. Equation 1 weights pairs equally within questions, then questions equally. Paired strategy intervals use 10,000 question-bootstrap resamples (seed root 20260914), retain-ing orders and repeats. Recovery per search uses cluster-ratio intervals. Missing acquisitions remain NULL; missing answers score zero. 

Search-budget extensions. Each extension has 100 questions, eight orders, two repeats, and two policies (3,200 trajectories). The two-search extension retains both opportunities (Section 2). The allocation experiment uses the fragmented profile below and varies only the allocation rule. First-ready selects the earliest completed search proposal. The contention extension retains the same bridge-comparison cohort and both roots, removes both ter-minal supports, and adds the question query’s four highest-ranked non-supports. 

Timed execution. Twenty DeepSeek and 100 GPT questions have two repeats per policy/profile. Profiles are simultaneous; priority-domain-first/last (canonical delivery over 0–4/10–20 seconds, sin-gletons at endpoints); and fragmented (0, 4, 8, 12, 16, 20 seconds). Elapsed time spans dispatch to response; resampling retains pro-files/repeats. Pseudorandom dispatch interleaves eight trajectories with at most sixteen concurrent requests. 

Persistence. Table 1 subtracts same-order from cross-order dis-tance over eligible pairs, jointly resampling numerators and de-nominators. Demand requires valid actions; direction requires two search requests. NULL actions remain unknown and are separately checked as a category. Eligible pairs, not questions, receive equal weight. 

Dependency-domain construction. Distractors are assigned by unique matches to branch entities in titles, then contents. Unas-signed items fill the smaller domain in evidence-identifier order, with ties broken by branch identifier. Partitions are fixed across schedules. 

Question-only plans. Planning precedes retrieval of six unique documents, three per domain in two-domain plans. Retrieval dedu-plicates the top-64 candidates, using the original question for fall-back. Retrieval calls determine document ownership. Plans and packets remain fixed across policies and schedules. Two-domain counts are 38, 5, and 8 for 2Wiki, HotpotQA, and MuSiQue, re-spectively; other plans form one chain. Schema failures use single-chain fallbacks (29, 11, and 45, respectively). All questions and pre-defined strata are retained. Each question adds one planning call, averaging 171 input and 147 output tokens.
