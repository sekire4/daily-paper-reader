## MEM REVISE : A Self-Revising Memory for Conversational Search 

Mohammed Ali Abdelrahman Abdallah Adam Jatowt 

University of Innsbruck, Austria 

{mohammed.ali, abdelrahman.abdallah, adam.jatowt}@uibk.ac.at 

Abstract 

In conversational search, users correct misun-derstandings, revise their requests and return to earlier topics, so part of the history no longer re-flects what they want. Existing benchmarks do not isolate such turns. Current search methods either re-read the history or keep a record of every turn, and none of them marks earlier con-tent as dropped. We introduce C ONV REVISE , a benchmark of 4,429 test questions in six tiers of returns and revisions over TopiOCQA, CORAL and OR-QuAC. We also propose M EM REVISE ,which combines a revisable memory graph, a 3B writer distilled from teacher memories, and a search guided by the memory. The writer reads each turn once and adds a node that records its topic, its facts and the operations it performs, such as adding, abandoning or reacti-vating a topic. Topic status follows from these operations, so a dropped topic keeps its facts and can be restored. With a frozen retriever, MEM REVISE reaches a mean MRR@10 of 0.701, against 0.589 for the best competitor, ConvSearch-R1-FT, and 0.617 for a writer dis-tilled the same way without the memory, so the memory and the steps built on it add 0.084. MEM REVISE is significantly better on Topi-OCQA and OR-QuAC, statistically tied with prompted 32B rewriters on CORAL, and never significantly behind the strongest competitor of any tier. 1

However, these approaches implicitly assume that conversational context accumulates as a purely additive process. In practice, search be-haviour is highly dynamic: users frequently correct system misunderstandings, as studied in spoken dia-logue (Hirschberg et al., 2001; Litman et al., 2006), revise their earlier requests, or return to previously explored topics. Consequently, after a correction or a revision, part of the established history no longer reflects what the user wants, and after a return, ear-lier context becomes relevant again. Since existing conversational search methods rely on full-history encoding or append-only memory, they lack an ex-plicit way to mark which topics have been dropped or restored. Figure 1 illustrates the challenge. The conversa-tion begins with Apollo 11 and the two astronauts who walked on the Moon. When the user asks, arXiv:2610.24672v1 [cs.IR] 17 Oct 2026 “where did he grow up?”, the system answers for Buzz Aldrin. The user replies, “no, i meant the sec-ond guy. . . wait, no, the first one. sorry”. This turn asks no explicit question, names no person, and im-mediately revises its own correction, yet it changes how the previous turn should be interpreted. The system must recover the open question, reject the earlier interpretation, and resolve “the first one” to Neil Armstrong. Later turns continue to shift the relevant context, from a follow-up about Arm-strong to a return to the mission and then a switch to Apollo 12. Existing methods do not explicitly represent such changes. Rewriters (Lin et al., 2020; Mo et al., 2023, 2024a; Zhu et al., 2025) are designed to re-solve missing context in the current query, but here the user provides only a correction and the intended query must be recovered from an earlier turn. Con-versational dense retrievers (Yu et al., 2021; Mao et al., 2024) encode the dialogue without marking which earlier context is no longer valid. Memory-based methods such as CMT-RAG (Zhou et al., 2026) preserve conversational history, but do not distinguish a rejected topic from one that remains relevant. We therefore propose M EM REVISE , a conversa-tional memory that later turns can revise. It reads each turn once and records its topic, associated facts, and operations such as adding, abandoning, or reactivating a topic. The status of each topic follows from these operations, allowing a dropped topic to retain its information and later become active again. A 3B writer, distilled from teacher-generated memories, updates the memory with one call per turn and produces the search query, which is combined with evidence from the memory during retrieval. In Figure 2, the writer drops Aldrin after the correction, makes Armstrong the current topic, and recovers the query “where did Neil Armstrong grow up?”. Evaluating this behaviour requires data that ex-isting benchmarks do not isolate. A screen for phrases such as “I meant” and “not that one” finds none among the 47,964 user turns in TopiOCQA’s training and validation conversations or the 6,480 user turns in CORAL’s evaluation conversations. Although this screen captures only explicit correc-tions, it suggests that such turns are poorly repre-sented in these datasets. We therefore introduce CONV REVISE , a benchmark of 4,429 test ques-tions over TopiOCQA, CORAL, and OR-QuAC. Its six tiers cover returns and revisions, ranging from naturally occurring returns in TopiOCQA to corrections written with language models. We compare M EM REVISE with seven methods: trained rewriters, a conversational dense retriever, and methods prompted on a 32B model. With a frozen retriever, M EM REVISE reaches a mean MRR@10 of 0.701. It significantly outperforms the strongest competitor on TopiOCQA (0.686 vs. 0.620) and OR-QuAC (0.783 vs. 0.594), and achieves the highest score on CORAL (0.634), al-though the difference is not statistically significant. 2 Related Work 

Conversational query rewriting and dense re-trieval. Conversational query rewriting turns the current question into a standalone query, with a rewriter trained on human rewrites or on retrieval feedback (Lin et al., 2020; Mo et al., 2023; Zhu et al., 2025) or with a prompted large language model (Mo et al., 2024a). Conversational dense retrieval instead encodes the current question and the earlier turns into a single query representation (Yu et al., 2021; Mao et al., 2024). Both designs read the full history at every turn and keep no state between turns. Whether an earlier request still holds must therefore be inferred again from the raw text each time, and a withdrawn request is never marked. 

Conversational memory. CMT-RAG (Zhou et al., 2026) is the closest work to ours. At each turn it decomposes the question into sub-questions and stores each as a trace with its retrieved pas-sages and a reader’s answer; later turns retrieve these traces. The record is append-only, and each trace depends on a generated answer. Long-term memories for chat agents store facts and update them as new information arrives (Chhikara et al., 2025; Rasmussen et al., 2025), and Context-Agent (Hu et al., 2026) organises the dialogue history into topic branches. Like CMT-RAG, they provide con-text for a generator. Ours serves conversational search and is evaluated on retrieval. It records the conversation’s topics and what each turn did to them, and writes the search query for the topic the user currently means, even after a correction or a return. Of the conversational search methods com-pared in Table 6 (Appendix A), only M EM REVISE 

reads each turn once, keeps a memory that later turns can revise and needs no generated answer. 

Benchmarks. TopiOCQA (Adlakha et al., 2022) contains topic switches and returns, CORAL (Cheng et al., 2025) samples topic shifts through Wikipedia sections, and OR-QuAC (Qu et al., 2020) keeps each conversation on one article. None of these papers, nor RECOR (Ali et al., 2026a), de-scribes turns in which the user corrects or revises an earlier request. 

Our contribution. Our work addresses the non-additive nature of conversational search. On the data side, we introduce C ONV REVISE to bench-mark the user corrections, revisions and returns that existing datasets do not isolate. Methodologi-cally, M EM REVISE departs from full-history and append-only models by processing each turn ex-actly once to maintain a revisable memory graph, actively isolating dropped context as user intents evolve. 

3 The C ONV REVISE Benchmark 

CONV REVISE evaluates whether conversational retrieval systems can maintain the correct retrieval target as user intent evolves. Each question appears as the final turn of a conversation and tests whether the system can resolve a return to an earlier topic, revise an existing interpretation, or continue from previously revised context. We build C ONV REVISE from TopiOCQA (Ad-lakha et al., 2022), CORAL (Cheng et al., 2025), and OR-QuAC (Qu et al., 2020). Each conversa-tional topic corresponds to a Wikipedia page. For TopiOCQA and OR-QuAC, the retrieval collection contains passages from that page, while CORAL uses passages from web pages cited by the cor-responding Wikipedia article. Tiers 

CONV REVISE defines six tiers (Table 1) that cap-ture two forms of conversational change: returning to earlier context and revising the current context. 

Return-1 resumes the topic discussed immediately before the current one, while Return-2+ returns to a topic at least two topics earlier. In both cases, the target is referred to indirectly through a pronoun or description rather than named explicitly, requiring the system to recover it from the dialogue history. The remaining tiers capture different forms of revision. Correction rejects one or more topics and redirects the conversation to the intended one. 

Follow-up is a short question whose interpretation depends on a preceding correction or corrective return. Self-repair occurs when the user introduces a correction and then revises it within the same turn (Schegloff et al., 1977). Value revision changes a topic-defining value, shifting the retrieval target to a closely related page with a nearly identical name. Construction 

CONV REVISE builds on conversations from Top-iOCQA, CORAL and OR-QuAC. The natural re-turns are original TopiOCQA turns; all other re-vision turns were generated with GPT-5.4-mini, refined with Claude Fable 5.1 and then verified individually. We construct C ONV REVISE in four main ways. 1. Natural returns use TopiOCQA turns that re-sume a topic discussed earlier in the same con-versation. 2. Built revisions start from two topics of the same type within a TopiOCQA conversation. We add a question that applies to both, initially answer it for the wrong topic, and then redirect the con-versation through a correction, self-repair, or correction followed by a short follow-up. Each example is paired with another that shares the same history but targets the alternative topic, preventing topic position from revealing the an-swer. 3. Spliced conversations take the opening of a source conversation, insert two intervening top-ics, and end with a return or correction. This cre-ates histories in which the intended topic must be recovered across competing context. 4. Value revisions extend a conversation to a closely related page whose title differs in a value, such as a number, year, or edition. The final turn changes that value and redirects the retrieval tar-get to the corresponding page. 

Size and Splits 

The test set contains 4,429 questions : 3,500 from 1,196 TopiOCQA conversations, 617 from 268 CORAL conversations, and 312 from 219 OR-QuAC conversations. TopiOCQA covers all six tiers, while CORAL and OR-QuAC cover correc-tions and returns. Of the TopiOCQA questions, 2,839 (81%) originate from conversations in its official training split, so models trained on Topi-OCQA may have prior exposure to these dialogue histories. The training set contains 1,790 questions from 769 conversations , including 839 from TopiOCQA and 951 from CORAL. No conversation overlaps with the test set, and OR-QuAC is not used for training. 

4 MEM REVISE 

MEM REVISE models a conversation as a revisable memory that is updated once per turn. Instead of repeatedly encoding the full dialogue history, it preserves the information needed for future turns in a structured memory and revises that state as the conversation develops (Figure 2). 

4.1 Task Formulation 

A conversation consists of turns (qt, a t) for t =1, . . . , n − 1, followed by a final question qn whose answer is unknown. Given a passage collection P,the task is to rank passages for qn.Conventional query rewriting derives a stan-dalone query from the full dialogue history. 4.4 Grounding Topics in the Corpus 

The writer can occasionally attach a turn to the wrong topic, especially when the conversation shifts to an entity introduced in the previous answer. For collections with page titles, M EM REVISE uses topic repair to ground the predicted topic against the corpus. After each history turn, it retrieves with the turn’s question and answer and separately with the node’s stored facts. If both searches identify the same page and that page conflicts with the writer’s topic, the node is reassigned to the retrieved page. CORAL has no page titles (Cheng et al., 2025), so its mem-ories retain the writer’s original topic assignments. 

4.5 Search 

At the final turn, M EM REVISE searches with the frozen Reason-ModernColBERT retriever (Khat-tab and Zaharia, 2020; Chaffin, 2025). Retrieval combines two complementary queries: the writer’s resolved query qs and a template query qt that re-tains the user’s wording while foregrounding the current topic. For passage p,

s(p) = z 0.67 S(qs, p ) + 0 .33 S(qt, p ), (2) where S(q, p ) is the late-interaction score, nor-malised by the query’s total token weight, and 

z(·) standardises scores over the collection. The writer query expresses the resolved information need, while the template provides a complemen-tary signal when the generated query drifts. When page titles are available, M EM REVISE 

also uses memory-derived ranking signals. A ti-tle prior increases the score of passages whose page title matches the final topic, while a facts vote favours pages supported by the facts stored for that topic. Final turns that only replace a number are handled by a small rule that updates the number in the topic and query before retrieval. Finally, revisions may distinguish between dif-ferent pages with the same surface name. In such cases, M EM REVISE uses the memory to identify the rejected page and adjusts the ranking against it while favouring same-name alternatives. This exclusion step follows EXCISE (Ali et al., 2026b) and leaves the underlying retriever frozen. 

5 Experimental Setup 

Competitors and Retrieval Architecture 

We compare M EM REVISE against seven competi-tors representing diverse conversational search approaches: trained query rewriters (T5QR, ConvGQR †, ConvSearch-R1), prompted query rewriters (LLM-Rewrite, CHIQ-AD), trace-based multi-turn memory (CMT-RAG ‡), and conversa-tional dense retrieval (ChatRetriever). All query-based methods, including MEM REVISE , use the same frozen Reason-ModernColBERT retriever over identical collections. MEM REVISE additionally incorpo-rates the memory-guided search steps detailed in §4.5, whereas ChatRetriever relies on its own conversational encoder. An Original Query 

baseline is included to provide a reference without context by searching only with the final turn. 

Training and Implementation Details 

To provide a comprehensive comparison, we eval-uate these competitors in their off-the-shelf con-figurations and, where applicable, fine-tune or train them on C ONV REVISE ’s training questions (T5QR-FT, ConvSearch-R1-FT, ChatRetriever-FT, and ConvGQR †). All prompted competitors— LLM-Rewrite, CHIQ-AD, and CMT-RAG ‡—run on Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct (Qwen Team, 2024). Methods marked with † or ‡ denote reimplementa-tions; complete configuration details, including spe-cific hyperparameter settings and deviations from the original systems, are provided in Appendix D.

This represents a clear reduction in errors compared to the compet-ing methods, whose lowest rates on these tiers are 7.0% and 13.5%. 

The Impact of Memory versus Model Distillation 

To determine whether these gains stem from the explicit memory graph or simply the strength of the distilled model, we trained a memory-free writer with the same 3B model and recipe, which gener-ates queries directly from the raw conversational text without memory or memory-guided search steps. This writer achieves a mean MRR@10 of 0.617, surpassing all competitors in Table 2 and confirming the effectiveness of the distillation recipe. Nevertheless, integrating the explicit mem-ory framework of M EM REVISE yields a further sig-nificant improvement of +0 .084 [+0 .069 , +0 .100] 

(Table 12 in Appendix E). This margin shows that while distillation provides a strong baseline, the memory and the search steps built on it are the pri-mary driver of the system’s leading performance.

Computational Efficiency and Context Dependency 

We also investigated whether the language model needs to process the entire raw dialogue history at each step to generate accurate memory states and queries. Modifying the writer to read the full raw conversation at every turn yields no performance benefit, resulting in a score difference of +0 .000 [−0.007 , +0 .008] . This confirms that our sequen-tial approach, which processes each turn exactly once to update a persistent memory graph, loses no accuracy. Additionally, we evaluated how the system han-dles simple queries that do not require previous con-text. These self-contained questions, for which the final question alone retrieves a gold passage in the top 10, make up 52% of the CORAL test questions, against 14% on TopiOCQA and 42% on OR-QuAC. MEM REVISE remains the top-performing method on these queries on every set (0.840 vs 0.767 on TopiOCQA, 0.826 vs 0.804 on CORAL, 0.894 vs 0.788 on OR-QuAC). This shows that maintaining a persistent memory graph does not degrade perfor-mance on simple and standalone searches. On the questions that need the conversation, M EM REVISE 

leads by +0 .044 on TopiOCQA and +0 .203 on OR-QuAC and ties on CORAL, whose passages carry no page titles for the memory-based search steps to use. 

Comparing Revisable Graphs with Trace Memory 

Compared to the closest competing paradigm of multi-turn retrieval augmented generation, M EM -REVISE demonstrates clear advantages in both ac-curacy and the number of model calls. The CMT-RAG ‡ competitor struggles with an MRR@10 of 0.410. This is consistent with its trace generator ig-noring system answers, even though the gold page of 48% of TopiOCQA test questions is named only in an earlier system answer. Furthermore, CMT-RAG ‡ lacks a mechanism to record withdrawn re-quests. Consistent with this, it ranks a passage of the rejected request above every gold passage for 25.6% of TopiOCQA corrections and 36.2% of self-repairs, against 5.1% and 7.0% for M EM -REVISE . Operationally, M EM REVISE resolves the dialogue state using a single inference pass through a 3B model per turn, whereas CMT-RAG ‡ requires approximately three calls to a 32B model per turn. Appendix F gives hard cases and an error analysis. 

7 Conclusion 

We introduced C ONV REVISE , a benchmark of re-turns and revisions, and M EM REVISE , a memory of topics, facts and operations that a 3B writer re-vises one turn at a time. M EM REVISE obtains the highest score on all three test sets and is never sig-nificantly behind the strongest competitor of any tier. Distillation alone already makes a 3B model a strong rewriter; the memory and the search steps built on it add a further 0.084 MRR@10. Together, they let a 3B model match or outperform prompted methods ten times its size on every test set. Fu-ture work can extend the memory to questions that combine several earlier topics. Limitations 

CONV REVISE covers revisions of a single request. Questions that combine several earlier topics, as in multi-hop questions, were not evaluated. All three corpora are in English and built on Wikipedia. Our TopiOCQA and OR-QuAC search collections are subsets of the full Wikipedia collections, built around the articles used by the benchmark conver-sations, so our scores cannot be compared with numbers published on the full collections.

This appendix extends §2 and follows the columns of Table 6. 

Conversational query rewriting. The standard approach to searching in a conversation is to rewrite the last question into a standalone query and sub-mit it to an ad hoc retriever. Trained rewriters learn this from human rewrites, as T5QR (Lin et al., 2020) does on CANARD (Elgohary et al., 2019). ConvGQR (Mo et al., 2023) appends a generated answer to the rewrite and, during training, pulls the rewriter’s representation of the conversation to-wards the retriever’s embedding of the gold passage. ConvSearch-R1 (Zhu et al., 2025) does not use hu-man rewrites. It first trains on its own rewrites that rank the gold passage first, and then optimises the rewriter by reinforcement learning with a re-ward derived from the rank of the gold passage. Prompted rewriters obtain the rewrite from a large language model. CHIQ (Mo et al., 2024a) first en-hances the history with an open language model. It disambiguates the new question, expands the last system response, generates a pseudo-response, keeps only the last turn after a topic switch and summarises the history, and it then rewrites the question from the enhanced history. All of these methods re-read the whole history at every turn and output a single query. None records that an earlier request was withdrawn, so a revision must be recognised anew, from the raw text, whenever a query is formed. 

Conversational dense retrieval. A second line removes the rewriting step and encodes the con-versation directly as the query. ConvDR (Yu et al., 2021) encodes the last question together with all earlier questions and trains this encoder to imitate the embedding an ad hoc retriever assigns to the human rewrite. ChatRetriever (Mao et al., 2024) adapts a large language model (Qwen-7B-Chat) for this purpose, combining contrastive learning with session-masked instruction tuning. HAConvDR (Mo et al., 2024b) judges an earlier turn useful when adding it to the current question improves re-trieval, and uses these judgments to train a retriever on a denoised history. These retrievers also read the earlier turns at every turn. Whether a topic is still wanted must be decided inside a single query vector, where the decision cannot be inspected. 

Memory for multi-turn RAG. CMT-RAG (Zhou et al., 2026) stores, for every turn, a trace of sub-questions with their dependencies on ear-lier turns, the passages retrieved for them and the reader’s answer, and recovers these traces in later turns. Its trace generator is a trained state-space model, and the method is evaluated on multi-hop question answering on its MuMu-QA benchmark. Our memory instead records the topics of the con-versation and what each turn did to them, while CMT-RAG keeps a growing record of what was asked. The exclusion step of our search follows EXCISE (Ali et al., 2026b), which identifies the ex-cluded topic outside the index and demotes match-ing candidates over a frozen index; exclusionary queries of this kind are studied in ExcluIR (Zhang et al., 2025). 

Memory for chat agents. MemGPT (Packer et al., 2023) moves information between the con-text window of a language model and external stor-age, so that an agent can continue a conversation over many sessions. MemoryBank (Zhong et al., 2024) stores memories of past conversations and lets them fade with time unless they are recalled. Mem0 (Chhikara et al., 2025) extracts facts from each exchange and decides for each one whether to add it, update or delete an existing memory, or change nothing. Zep (Rasmussen et al., 2025) stores facts as edges of a temporal knowledge graph and marks an edge as no longer valid when new information contradicts it. A-Mem (Xu et al., 2026) keeps memories as linked notes, and a new note can update the descriptions of related older ones. Context-Agent (Hu et al., 2026) organises a dia-logue into trees of topics and branches and builds the context of each response from the active branch and summaries of the others. These systems are evaluated through the responses of a generator, on benchmarks that test whether facts from long con-versations are recalled, as in LoCoMo (Maharana et al., 2024), and kept up to date as new informa-tion arrives, as in LongMemEval (Wu et al., 2024) and BEAM (Tavakoli et al., 2026). Sinha et al. (2026) build dialogues from TopiOCQA and MSC (Xu et al., 2022) and test whether language mod-els notice when the user moves to a new topic and leave the earlier context behind. Dialogue state tracking follows the values a user gives for a fixed set of slots in task-oriented dialogue, such as the area or price range of a restaurant, and a later turn can change a value (Budzianowski et al., 2018; Wu et al., 2019). Natural returns. A TopiOCQA turn with an an-swer is a return when its topic label differs from that of the previous turn and appeared earlier in the conversation. We keep only returns whose question refers to the target by a pronoun or a description. Its depth, the number of other topics discussed since the target, decides between Return-1 (depth one) and Return-2+. 

Built revisions. We select two topics of the same kind from one TopiOCQA conversation and trun-cate the conversation after the last turn about either of them. We then add a question that applies to both, answer it for the wrong topic, and append a correction, a self-repair, or a correction followed by a new question. Each item is built for both topics with the same history. The gold passage belongs to the intended topic and the stale passages to the other one. 

Written returns and value revisions. For 104 Return-2+ questions we append one turn to a Topi-OCQA conversation with at least four topics, and this turn returns to a middle topic by describing it. A value revision follows a turn whose page has a sibling that differs by a number, such as a year or an edition, with a short switch such as “sorry, i meant 2, not 1”. Its gold passage belongs to the sibling and its stale passage to the original page. 

Spliced conversations. A spliced item keeps the opening of a conversation about a topic E and ap-pends digressions about two further topics, each linked to E, confusable with it, or taken from an-other conversation. The final turn targets one of the three topics: it is either the source’s own next turn about E or a new question about an unseen passage of the target, written as a correction or as a return by pronoun or description. A return to E is Return-2+, a return to the first digression Return-1, and the passages of the other two topics are stale. On TopiOCQA and CORAL a correction describes the intended topic through an earlier fact; on OR-QuAC the digressions are the opening turns of two other OR-QuAC conversations, and the wordings start from templates. 

Gold passages and collections. Every gold pas-sage is present in its collection, no stale passage is gold, and no mined passage contains an answer from the same conversation. The CORAL col-lection is the official corpus (162,650 passages). Training. The writer is trained on 22,163 ex-amples from 9,916 turns of the TopiOCQA and CORAL training conversations: history turns, final turns, and copies of the history turns that change the memory, with their answer removed, so that the writer also learns to decide without an answer, as at the final turn. Most turns appear twice, once with the teacher memory and once with the memory of an auxiliary writer trained on the other half of the conversations. The competitors are trained on final questions only. We train LoRA adapters (rank 16, 

α = 32 , dropout 0.05) on the attention projections of Ministral-3-3B for three epochs, with a learning rate of 5 × 10 −5 chosen on development data and an effective batch size of 12; the checkpoint is cho-sen by the held-out loss on the fields written before the query. 

Memory-free writer. The memory-free writer of Table 12 uses the writer’s base model and LoRA settings, its learning rate, effective batch size and number of epochs, and three seeds, and its check-point is chosen by held-out loss. It reads the raw conversation, with answers cut at 600 characters as for every competitor, and is trained on the writer’s final-turn target queries, each turn once. It has no template part, no priors, no exclusion step and no topic repair, and its queries are scored with the same frozen retriever as every other method. 

Search rules. From the second turn on, topic re-pair runs two searches, one with the turn’s question and answer and one with the stored facts, and each picks the page with the most 1/rank votes among its top 20 passages, which is the page it identifies in §4.4. If the two agree, and the page differs from the node’s topic, is a name from the previous answer or an earlier topic, and does not appear in the turn’s own answer, that page becomes the node’s topic and is entered with an A DD or R EACTIVATE op-eration. This changes the topic of 4.5% of history turns on the development sets. The template starts with the current topic, its tokens weighted twice, and ends with the user’s final question. The title prior adds 1.5, in units of the standardised score, to the passages whose page title, ignoring any brack-eted qualifier, is the topic of the final node. For the facts vote, the facts of the earlier nodes about the final topic are searched, and the top 20 passages vote for their page with weight 1/rank. On Top-iOCQA each node is searched on its own, every earlier node is used if none is about the topic, and the winning page gains 1.0 within M EM REVISE ’s top 100 passages; on OR-QuAC the facts of the last three such nodes form one search, there is no vote without such a node, and the winning page gains 

1.0 on all its passages. The number rule applies to a final turn of at most 12 words, without a question word, that names exactly one new number, decade or Roman numeral. It replaces a number of the same kind in the topic of the final or the previous node, and the search then uses the writer’s query with the new name, plus the title prior where pages have titles. The exclusion step takes the page of the top passage retrieved for the facts of the last three nodes as the rejected page, and applies only if an-other page has the same title once a bracketed part is removed. The rejected page then loses 1.5 and the other pages with its name gain 1.5. Same-name pages that the title prior has already raised do not gain a further 1.5.

G Human Evaluation of C ONV REVISE 

To validate the quality of the CONV REVISE 

benchmark—specifically the built revisions and spliced conversations generated using language models—we conducted a human evaluation on a random sample of 100 final turns stratified across the test set’s six tiers. Three independent annota-tors evaluated the data across two dimensions: Dia-logue Naturalness (a 5-point Likert scale assessing the fluency of the conversational shift) and Gold Passage Correctness (a binary judgment on whether the designated gold passage accurately fulfills the user’s intent). The benchmarked turns achieved a mean Naturalness score of 4.65/5.0, demonstrating that the simulated corrections, self-repairs, and re-turns effectively mimic natural human discourse. Furthermore, annotators agreed with the designated gold passage in 95% of cases, with substantial inter-annotator agreement (Fleiss’ κ = 0 .78 ). Disagree-ments were resolved via majority vote, confirming that C ONV REVISE provides a highly reliable gold standard for evaluating evolving user intents in con-versational search. Return-1
