ABSTRACT 

Language-model agents often reason before they act, and the value of monitoring that reasoning depends on how it relates to their actions. We ask how an agent’s actions depend on its reasoning when the correct action depends on a changing state. In a small file-system task, background events announce which file’s lock has been toggled but not its new value. The agent therefore has to reconstruct the current locks from its history before it can move files safely. With Qwen3-8B and Qwen3.5-9B, we separate two roles of reasoning: generating it before each action, and retaining earlier reasoning in the prompt. Most of the reduction in lock-violating commands comes from reasoning written at the moment of action. Retaining earlier reasoning adds little to rule-following, although it helps one model complete the task. The benefit depends on what the reasoning says. Filler text, reasoning written for another history and an accurate table of the state do not reproduce it. When we give the agent reasoning written for a history that differs in one lock value, the agent acts on that reasoning even against its own history. Without reasoning, linear probes recover the state from the model’s activations about as well as a text classifier recovers it from the transcript. However, decoding accuracy does not predict which interventions change the agent’s actions. In this setting, the benefit of reasoning for rule-following comes mainly from generating it at each step. The state that can be decoded without reasoning is a limited guide to how the agent acts. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Language models are increasingly deployed as agents that reason before they act (Yao et al., 2023). Their written reasoning is also a potential source of information about their decisions, which motivates proposals to monitor agents through their chains of thought (Baker et al., 2025; Korbak et al., 2025). How useful the reasoning is for this purpose depends on the role it plays in the agent’s decisions. In theory, writing intermediate tokens extends the serial computation that a transformer can carry out before it answers (Merrill and Sabharwal, 2024; Li et al., 2024; Brown-Cohen et al., 2026). In practice, the role of reasoning in a given agent and task is an empirical question. A trace may carry out computation that the next action needs, store information for later turns, or describe a decision without causing it. Telling these roles apart requires interventions on when reasoning is generated, on what is retained, and on what the trace says. We study these roles in a task that requires keeping track of a changing state, a recurring requirement in sequential decision making. We built a small file-system environment in which files are locked or unlocked. Background events toggle the locks and announce which file changed, but not its new value. Safe moves depend on the current lock state, which is determined by the history and must be reconstructed from it. The full history of observations and executed commands stays in the prompt in every condition, so the evidence needed to reconstruct each lock state is always available. Reasoning can help such an agent in two ways. Generating reasoning before an action gives the model extra computation at the moment of decision. Retaining earlier reasoning in the prompt, which we call persistence , gives it notes that it could consult instead of working out the state again (Lanchantin et al., 2023). A question about the state and the choice of action also rely on different parts of the context (Section 7). Together, the answers show that reasoning written at the current step brings a substantial benefit, and that the agent’s next actions depend on this reasoning. The answers also show that the state decodable from the model without reasoning does not, by itself, characterize the interventions that change the agent’s actions. 

2 RELATED WORK 

Reasoning as computation and as memory. Chain-of-thought prompting (Wei et al., 2022) and interleaving reasoning with actions (Yao et al., 2023) improve multi-step problem solving, and theoretical work shows that intermediate tokens increase what a transformer can compute (Merrill and Sabharwal, 2024; Li et al., 2024). Filler or pause tokens can sometimes replace the content of the reasoning (Pfau et al., 2024; Goyal et al., 2024). Other work uses generated text as memory: Self-Notes interleave notes with the input (Lanchantin et al., 2023), and agent systems keep reflections or external memories across steps (Shinn et al., 2023; Park et al., 2023; Packer et al., 2023). We measure the two roles separately while the full event history remains available. 

Faithfulness and monitoring. Reasoning traces do not always reflect the computation behind an answer (Turpin et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2025), and answers can sometimes be decoded before the reasoning begins (Cox et al., 2026). The value of monitoring reasoning may therefore depend on how much computation must pass through it (Korbak et al., 2025; Brown-Cohen et al., 2026). Truncating the reasoning, inserting mistakes or replacing the reasoning with filler tests whether an answer depends on it (Lanham et al., 2023). Resampling methods measure the influence of individual reasoning steps (Bogdan et al., 2025; Macar et al., 2026). Our controls, swaps and edits apply this kind of test to an agent whose state and actions can be checked against the environment. 

State in activations. Board states can be decoded from sequence models trained on game moves (Li et al., 2023), and linearly so in Othello models (Nanda et al., 2023). Language models also track entity states that change through sequences of operations (Li et al., 2021; Kim and Schuster, 2023; Li et al., 2025), bind attributes to entities (Feng and Steinhardt, 2024), and implement entity tracking with identifiable circuits (Prakash et al., 2024). High probe accuracy does not show that a model uses the decoded information (Hewitt and Liang, 2019; Belinkov, 2022; Elazar et al., 2021). Activation patching tests which activations affect an output (Meng et al., 2022; Zhang and Nanda, 2024; Heimersheim and Nanda, 2024; Geva et al., 2023), and difference-of-means directions tend to have larger causal effects than logistic-regression directions (Marks and Tegmark, 2024). 3 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Environment and task. The file system has three log files (alpha.log , beta.log ,

gamma.log ) and a goal file ( report.txt ). Each file is either in /logs or in /archive ,and each is either locked or unlocked. At each turn the agent issues one command ( ls , cd , stat ,

lock , unlock , mv , rm or done ). The task is to archive every unlocked log file, never to touch a locked file, and to leave report.txt locked at the end. The first observation lists the lock value of every file. After the agent’s second, fourth and sixth commands, a background job toggles the lock of a random file and announces that it did so, but not the new value (Figure 1a). The lock and 

unlock commands reply only “ok”. As a result, the current lock state is always determined by the visible history, and a symbolic replay of the history recovers it exactly. The agent, however, has to combine events across turns to know the current state. An episode has at most eight turns, and the agent can end it with done from the seventh turn on. All runs use the same system prompt, task text and event schedule (Appendix A). 

Models and formats. We use two open-weight models of similar size from different generations: Qwen3-8B, a transformer with standard attention, and Qwen3.5-9B, which combines linear and full attention (Yang et al., 2025; Qwen Team, 2026a). Each model can either reason in a thought block or answer directly, within the same checkpoint. We can therefore switch reasoning on and off without changing the weights. We run both models in their chat format. To test whether the results depend on post-training, we also use Qwen3-8B-Base, the pretrained checkpoint before post-training. Because it has no chat template, we write its history as plain text and show it a two-turn example session with other files before the real episode. We run the post-trained Qwen3-8B with the same plain-text prompt, so that differences caused by the format can be separated from differences caused by post-training. 

Generation and persistence. In the reasoning mode, the model writes a thought block and then a command. In the direct mode, reasoning is disabled, the prompt ends with CMD: , and the model writes only the command. The persistence setting decides which part of each earlier reply is retained in later prompts. With full , the reasoning and the reply are retained. With body , the default of the chat templates, only the reply is retained. With cmd , only the executed command is retained. We name conditions by mode and persistence, for example think/cmd or direct/cmd . Let V (c) denote the violation rate of condition c (defined below). We measure the effect of generating reasoning and the effect of retaining it by 

∆gen = V (direct/cmd ) − V (think/cmd ), ∆ret = V (think/cmd ) − V (think/full ). (1) 3Preprint Neither condition in ∆gen retains earlier reasoning, and the two conditions in ∆ret both generate it. 

∆gen compares two interfaces, with and without a thought block, and does not hold the amount of computation fixed. We run 500 episodes per condition for Qwen3-8B and 300 for the other models, and sample at temperature 0.7. Episode i starts from the same initial state in every condition, so conditions can be compared episode by episode. 

Fixed histories. Rollouts compare agents that end up in different states. To study the effect of reasoning on the same input, we take 400 prompts from held-out think/body episodes (third turn or later). We complete each prompt with five versions of the thought block, which we call arms (Figure 1c). The first arm leaves the block empty ( no reasoning ), and the second contains the model’s own reasoning ( own reasoning ). The filler arm contains as many dots as the own reasoning has tokens. The other-history reasoning arm contains the reasoning that the model wrote for a prompt from another episode. The state table arm leaves the block empty and adds an accurate table of every file’s lock and location after it. The table is an oracle, because it also lists locations that the agent may not have seen. Outside the thought block and the table, the prompts are identical. In these experiments, and in those of Section 6, reasoning and commands are produced by greedy decoding, which always selects the most probable next token. Absolute rates are therefore not directly comparable with the sampled rollouts. 

Measurements. An eligible attempt is an mv or rm aimed at a file that is in /logs before the command, so that the command would succeed if the file were unlocked. A lock-violating command 

is an eligible attempt at a locked file. The violation rate of a condition is 

V = number of lock-violating commands number of eligible attempts . (2) An episode is a strict success if, at the end, report.txt is locked and no unlocked log file remains in /logs . In addition, the agent must never have issued a command that touches a locked file ( mv ,

rm or unlock ) or deleted a file. To ask the model about the state directly, we append the question “Is f currently locked? . . . answer with exactly one word” and the beginning of an answer, “ f is currently”. We then compute the state readout 

sf = log p(locked ) − log p(unlocked ) (3) for the next word. We evaluate sf by its AUROC against the true state. Commands from the same episode are not independent. All confidence intervals therefore resample whole episodes, and paired comparisons use episode-level tests (Appendix B). 

4 GENERATING REASONING MATTERS MORE THAN RETAINING IT 

For both chat models, the three reasoning conditions have similar violation rates, and all three are far below the direct condition (Figure 2a). For Qwen3-8B, the rate is 1.4–2.3% with reasoning and 22.8% without; for Qwen3.5-9B, it is 3.4–4.8% and 15.2%. In think/cmd , the reasoning of each turn is removed before the next turn, and the rate is still low. The benefit therefore does not require earlier reasoning. The benefit also does not come from avoiding the task, since reasoning agents complete the task more often (Figure 2c; Qwen3-8B: 76% against 44%). The effect of generating reasoning appears at every Qwen3 model size from 0.6B to 14B and in episodes that are twice as long (Appendix D). Figure 2b compares the two effects of Eq. (1). For Qwen3-8B, ∆gen is 20.9 percentage points, whereas ∆ret is 0.5 points and its interval includes zero. The one-sided 95% bootstrap upper bound on the ratio ∆ret /∆gen is 7.6%. A second Qwen3-8B run with a smaller token budget gives the same ordering and similar effect sizes (Appendix C). Qwen3.5-9B shows the same ordering, but the ratio is less certain (upper bound 44%). We do not conclude that retention has no effect on this measure. Retention does change how much new reasoning the model writes. With its earlier reasoning in context, Qwen3-8B writes less than half as much per turn, while its violation rate stays about the same (Appendix C.3).

Most of the gain comes from the goal file. report.txt can be unlocked at the start or by a toggle, and it has to be locked before the end. Qwen3.5-9B leaves it locked more often when its earlier reasoning is retained (Appendix C.4). We did not analyse what the retained reasoning says about the goal. The plain-text format shows a different pattern. For the base model, generating reasoning brings no clear reduction in violation rate, while retaining it brings a large one; the post-trained Qwen3-8B gains from both (Figure 2b). These differences follow the amount of reasoning that the models write. When the history shows only commands, both models mostly stop reasoning (Appendix C.3). In this format, the persistence contrast largely measures whether reasoning is generated at all.

Own reasoning. On identical prompts, the model’s own reasoning improves the state readout of both models and lowers the violation rate from 49% to 6% (Qwen3-8B) and from 40% to 4% (Qwen3.5-9B). Per 100 prompts, this removes about five lock-violating commands in each model (episode-level sign-flip test, p < 0.001 ). 

Filler and other-history reasoning. A long insertion could help regardless of what it says (Pfau et al., 2024; Lanham et al., 2023). Filler of the same length does not reproduce the reduction: the violation rate stays at the level of the no-reasoning arm, and the Qwen3-8B readout becomes slightly worse. Reasoning written for another history is fluent and relevant to the task, but it describes a different state. Other-history reasoning gives no benefit per attempt (for Qwen3-8B, 82% of attempts violate the rule), and it pushes the AUROC of the state readout towards chance (0.57 and 0.60). Section 6 tests more directly whether answers and actions follow the reasoning in context. 

An accurate state table does not replace reasoning. With the oracle state table placed right before the command, the AUROC of the state readout is almost perfect. The violation rate per attempt falls, from 49% to 32% for Qwen3-8B and from 40% to 16% for Qwen3.5-9B, but it remains well above the rate with the model’s own reasoning. In many of these violations, the model moves a file that the table lists as locked. The table also changes the policy: the model checks files less often and moves them more often (Appendix E), so the number of violations per prompt does not decrease. Providing the state explicitly therefore does not ensure a rule-following command. Because the table also changes the form of the prompt, this result does not show that working out the state plays no role in the benefit of reasoning. 

6 THE ACTION FOLLOWS THE REASONING 

To test how a single lock fact affects the next action, we build counterfactual pairs . The two prompts of a pair are copies of a held-out prompt that differ only in the initial lock value of one log file f . The file is in /logs , and its lock is never mentioned again. We call the two prompts the locked and the unlocked version. In the locked version, mv f violates the rule; in the unlocked version, it is allowed and helps complete the task. We generate the model’s own reasoning separately for each version. 

Reasoning strengthens the effect of the fact on the action. Without reasoning, the flipped fact already has some effect on the action: mv f is more probable in the unlocked version (Figure 16b, Appendix F). The effect is weak, however. The greedy command moves the locked file in 4 of 114 pairs (Qwen3-8B) and 9 of 104 pairs (Qwen3.5-9B), and moves the unlocked file in only 18 and 31. 6Preprint With reasoning, the agent moves the unlocked file more often (45 and 41 pairs), and we observe no move of the locked file. 

Swapped reasoning changes which files the agent moves. The two versions differ in one fact, and each has its own reasoning. We now complete each history with the reasoning written for the other version (Figure 3). Given reasoning written for the unlocked version, the agent moves the locked file in 43 of 114 pairs (Qwen3-8B) and 41 of 104 pairs (Qwen3.5-9B). These counts are close to those in the unlocked history with the same reasoning (45 and 41). When the reasoning is held fixed, changing the history changes whether the file is moved in at most two pairs, and changes the complete command in at most three. The history still shifts the log-probability of mv f by several units (Figure 3b), so the model does not ignore it. The reasoning, however, has the larger effect on both the action and the state readout (Figure 3c). 

Editing only the lock statements has a smaller effect. The swapped reasoning differs from the original in its statements about the lock, in its conclusions and plans, and in its wording. To separate the effect of the explicit lock statements from that of a full swap, we keep the model’s own reasoning and change only its explicit statements of f ’s lock value. For example, “beta.log: locked” becomes “beta.log: unlocked”. Conditionals, questions, goals and plans are left unchanged. As a control, we change the same words only in letter case (“locked” becomes “LOCKED”), which keeps their meaning. The edit moves the state readout towards the edited value. In the locked version, it also raises the log-probability of mv f by about a quarter of the effect of the full swap. Yet no edited locked-version trace leads to moving the file, and in the unlocked version whether the file is moved changes in only one pair (Figure 17, Appendix F). The edited traces still contain conclusions and plans derived from the original value (“don’t touch”, “no more unlocked log files”), some unedited statements about the state, and the original history. The result is consistent with the action relying on the retained conclusions or plans, but it does not tell which of these sources sustains the action. Editing a trace also produces text that the model would not have written itself (Macar et al., 2026). 

7 STATE DECODABILITY AND INTERVENTION EFFECTS ON ACTIONS 

If an agent acts without writing out its reasoning, one might hope to learn what it acts on from its answers about the state or from its activations. We first compare how accurately the state can be decoded with the effects of patching. We then compare a question about the state with the direct action on matched counterfactual prefixes. These experiments concern the direct action, with no reasoning generated, and do not identify the mechanism by which reasoning improves behavior. 

Probes and text classifiers. We first compare how accurately a linear classifier recovers the lock state from the residual stream and from the visible text. For Qwen3-8B, logistic-regression probes at the last prompt token reach a mean held-out balanced accuracy of 0.79, and a TF-IDF classifier on the visible text reaches 0.77. The difference is 1.6 percentage points, with a 95% interval from −0.4

to 3.7 (Appendix G), and both classifiers stay far below a symbolic replay of the history (1.00). The probes therefore show no clear advantage over a classifier that reads the transcript. 

Decoding accuracy does not predict patching effects. To locate where the model carries the fact, we append the state question to counterfactual pairs and run the model on the unlocked version. Yet this probe recovers at most 0.02, and its direction is nearly orthogonal to the counterfactual direction. A mass-mean probe decodes slightly worse but recovers most of the effect, in line with Marks and Tegmark (2024). The direction with the highest decoding accuracy thus has a much smaller patching effect than the mean-difference direction. A large recovered fraction shows that editing a direction is enough to shift the output, not that the model relies only on that direction (Makelov et al., 2024). 

The question and the action rely on different positions. A question about the state is itself an output and need not use the fact in the same way as the action. For each pair, we take one prefix, the history up to the moment the agent chooses its command, and append either the state question or CMD: . For the action, x in Eq. (4) is the log-probability of the command “ mv f ”. Because the model reads text from left to right, the activations of the prefix are identical in both cases. Figure 5 8Preprint shows that the two outputs depend on different positions. For the state readout, patching the question tokens has large effects in the middle layers, while patching the rest of the history has small effects. For the action, patching the rest of the history recovers up to 0.57 (Qwen3-8B, layer 20) and 0.39 (Qwen3.5-9B, layer 16), well above the values for the state readout (Appendix K). Asking the model about the state, or probing its activations, thus tells us that the fact is present, but not how it reaches the action. The two outputs also differ in their suffix and in form, so this result does not show that the model holds two separate representations of the lock state. 

8 DISCUSSION AND LIMITATIONS 

Generating versus retaining reasoning. Our first question was whether the benefit of reasoning comes from generating it or from retaining it. In both chat models, most of the reduction in lock-violating commands comes from the reasoning written at the current step. The reduction remains when every earlier thought block is removed, as long as the observations and executed commands stay in the prompt. Retention still has two measurable effects. It raises task completion for Qwen3.5-9B, mainly because this model more often finishes with the goal file locked, and it shortens the reasoning that both models write at each step. For agents built on the Qwen templates, discarding earlier reasoning therefore costs little in rule-following when the full history is available. 

Actions follow the reasoning in context. Our second question was whether the content of the reasoning matters, and whether the action follows it. Filler of the same length, reasoning written for another history and an accurate state table all fail to reproduce the benefit, so neither a longer input nor explicit state information is sufficient. In the counterfactual pairs, without reasoning, the flipped lock value has only a weak effect on the direct action. With reasoning written for the other version, the agent often moves a file that its own history shows to be locked. The agent’s next action therefore depends strongly on the reasoning in its context. This dependence is relevant to monitoring, although we did not test whether a monitor could detect rule violations from unmodified traces. The same dependence has a practical consequence: reasoning that is wrong, or that has been altered, can lead directly to a rule-violating action. 

What can be read without reasoning. Our third question was whether the state, read from the model without reasoning, explains the direct action. Linear probes recover the state about as well as a text classifier. However, the most accurate probe direction has a much smaller patching effect than the mean-difference direction. On identical prefixes, a question about the state and the choice of action also depend on different positions. An accurate readout of the state therefore does not show how the state affects the action, and it should be tested against the behavior it is meant to explain. 

Limitations. The environment is small, and the full event history always stays in the prompt; retained reasoning may matter more when earlier observations are removed or summarized. Both models come from the same developer, so transfer of results to other model families should be studied. The generation contrast compares two interfaces, with and without a thought block, and does not hold the amount of computation fixed. For this reason, we cannot say whether limited serial computation causes the errors of the direct policy. The swaps and edits test the effects of transplanted or modified traces, which may differ from the effects of reasoning generated naturally for the recipient history. In the counterfactual pairs, the flipped lock value is stated once and never changes. Therefore, the patching results indicate how a stated fact is used, not how toggled states are tracked. 

9 CONCLUSION 

In our task, generating reasoning at each step reduced lock violations far more than retaining earlier reasoning. The benefit depended on the content of the reasoning, and swapping reasoning between histories changed which files the agent moved. Without reasoning, the state could be decoded from the model, but decoding accuracy did not predict which interventions changed the agent’s answers or actions. Evaluating reasoning and internal representations by their effect on behavior gives a clearer picture than reading either alone. 9Preprint

B STATISTICAL PROCEDURES 

Commands within an episode are not independent. Every confidence interval is therefore a percentile interval from a bootstrap that resamples whole episodes (2,000 to 10,000 resamples). Rates such as violations per attempt are recomputed as a ratio of sums in each resample (Cameron et al., 2008). Conditions of a rollout run share episode seeds, so contrasts between conditions are computed episode by episode, and the bootstrap resamples seeds. To compare the effects of generating and of retaining reasoning, we compute the ratio of the two effects in each resample and report its 95th percentile as a one-sided 95% upper bound. A bound of 7.6% means that, with 95% confidence, the effect of retaining reasoning is at most 7.6% of the effect of generating it. We do not report the ratio when the generation effect is close to zero. Several fixed-history prompts and counterfactual pairs come from the same episode. For paired action comparisons, we take the per-prompt (or per-pair) difference between arms in whether a lock-violating command was issued, and report the mean difference with an episode-bootstrap interval. We test this difference with an episode-level sign-flip test. The test sums the differences within each episode and 14 Preprint compares the observed total with totals obtained by randomly flipping the sign of each episode’s sum (all sign combinations when at most 20 episodes have a non-zero sum, otherwise 2 × 10 5 random draws). This test assumes that, under the null hypothesis, the two arms are exchangeable within an episode. With few events, a percentile interval can exclude zero while the test does not reject, so we treat the intervals as the main summary. State readouts are evaluated by AUROC against the true state, pooling the four files; probe data are split by episode. We do not correct for multiple comparisons, and we describe secondary analyses as descriptive.

F COUNTERFACTUAL PAIRS , REASONING SWAP AND TARGETED EDIT 

Pairs. A pair is built from a held-out prompt (third turn or later) that contains the first-observation line for a file f exactly once. The file f must be in /logs , and nothing later in the history may mention its lock: no toggle, no stat , lock , unlock , mv or rm of f , and no line that names f

together with a lock word. This filter was written for experiments in which the state question follows the agent’s current reply, and it also looks at that reply and at the executed command. For the action experiments, it therefore leaves out prompts after which the original agent acted on f (without this, the pool would contain 765 instead of 648 candidates for Qwen3-8B and 207 instead of 150 for Qwen3.5-9B). This selects on the original agent’s next action. It does not affect comparisons within a pair, but it restricts the conclusions to the selected prompts. The matched patching experiment of Appendix K uses only information available before the action. We use log files as the main analysis, because for them moving the file is part of the task; the goal file is reported separately below. 

Readouts. For each version and arm we record the state readout sf , the log-probability of the command “ mv f ” after CMD: (summed over its tokens), and the greedy command. The own reasoning is generated greedily and separately for each version, with a budget of 3,000 tokens. We summarize each pair by two separations. The state-readout separation is sf (locked version ) −

sf (unlocked version ), which is positive when the answer tracks the fact. The action separation is the log-probability of mv f in the unlocked version minus that in the locked version, which is positive when the action tracks the fact. 

Effect of reasoning. Figure 16 shows both separations and the greedy moves for the log-file pairs. Reasoning increases both separations, and no move of the locked file is observed with reasoning (Qwen3-8B: 4 to 0 pairs, p = 0 .13 ; Qwen3.5-9B: 9 to 0, p = 0 .031 ; episode-level sign-flip tests). For Qwen3-8B, 78% and 75% of the reasoning traces ended within the budget in the locked and unlocked versions; on the 66 pairs in which both did, the action separation with reasoning is unchanged. For the goal file (46 pairs per model), Qwen3-8B never moves the file, and Qwen3.5-9B moves it in 2 (locked) and 5 (unlocked) pairs without reasoning and in none with reasoning. 

Reasoning swap. For every pair, we reuse the two saved reasoning traces and evaluate all four combinations of history and trace; only the command is generated again. The two combinations in which the history and the trace match reproduce the saved results exactly in every pair. Table 4 gives the log-file results. Target-move counts track the version for which the trace was written, and the history adds a smaller shift in the log-probability of mv f . On the goal file, where moving is not part of the task, no cell moves the file more than once. 

Targeted edit. The swap changes everything that the reasoning says. G PROBES AND TEXT CLASSIFIERS 

We compare the accuracy of linear classifiers trained on residual-stream activations with that of clas-sifiers that read the visible transcript. Probes are ℓ2-regularized logistic regressions on standardized residual-stream activations at the last prompt token before generation (Qwen3-8B, think/body ), one per lock variable and layer. We use 30% of the episodes as the test set and 20% of the rest to choose the layer. The text classifiers see exactly the text the model saw: a TF-IDF logistic regression, and a rule that returns the last lock value written for the file. A symbolic replay of the history recovers every lock state exactly. Table 5 and Figure 18 show that the probes perform about as well as the TF-IDF classifier (mean balanced accuracy 0.79 against 0.77; paired difference 1.6 points [ −0.4, 3.7]). Accuracy is nearly flat across layers, high right after a value is written (0.92–0.96) and much lower five turns later (0.55– 0.70), as for the text rule. For the location of files, which ls shows directly, TF-IDF is better than the probe. The activation probes thus show no clear accuracy advantage over TF-IDF in this evaluation, and both are less accurate than symbolic replay. The natural-prompt probes used in Section 7 are labelled with the state before the current command. K MATCHED PATCHING OF THE STATE READOUT AND THE ACTION 

The experiments of Appendices I and J differ in more than the output: the state question was asked after the agent’s current reply, the pairs were drawn separately, and the pair filter looked at the current reply. This experiment removes these differences. Candidates are held-out log-file pairs (third turn or later) in which f is in /logs before the command and its lock value is not mentioned again, judged only from observations up to the current turn and replies up to the previous turn. The shared prefix is the saved prompt with an empty thought block. We append CMD: (with “ mv f ” as the scored continuation) or the state question, as separate token sequences, so that the prefix tokens are identical; tokenizing the full text gives the same tokens for every pair. Pairs are kept if both outputs differ by at least one logit between the versions (120 of 132 screened pairs for Qwen3-8B, and 119 of all 147 candidates for Qwen3.5-9B). The layers were fixed in advance, and patching the source token at the last layer has no effect on either output. The results are shown in Figure 5. For the action, the rest of the history recovers a large share of the effect in the middle layers (Qwen3-8B: 0.41–0.57 at layers 16–20; Qwen3.5-9B: 0.36–0.39 at layers 14–16), while for the state readout it recovers at most 0.19 and 0.06. The paired difference between the two outputs is largest at layer 20 of Qwen3-8B (0.57 [0.50, 0.64]) and layer 16 of Qwen3.5-9B (0.40 [0.32, 0.47]). For the state readout, the question tokens carry most of the effect in these layers (up to 0.72 and 0.75). The difference between the two outputs found in the earlier experiments 27 Preprint therefore remains when the prefix, the pairs, the selection rule and the position groups are the same. The two outputs still differ in their suffix and in what is measured (one next-token comparison against a three-token command), and each group is patched on its own, so the effects need not add up. 

L STEERING 

To test whether a probe direction can change the model’s answer about one file without affecting the others, we add α u ∥h∥/√d to the final token of the state question. We do this at layers 16, 24 and 32, with α ∈ { 16 , 32 , 64 }, where u is a unit direction, h the residual activation and d its dimension (α = 64 equals the mean residual norm at this position). We use two probe directions (the probe trained at the agent prompt and one trained at the steered token), the probe directions of the other files, and 24 random directions. For each direction, we read out the answers about the target file and about the other files. Large perturbations push this readout towards “unlocked” whatever the direction, so we measure the shift towards “locked”. We fixed a criterion in advance: a direction counts as file-specific if it shifts the target answer more than all 24 random directions and all other-file directions, while shifting the other files’ answers by less than half as much. None of the 36 tested settings (two files, three layers, three strengths and two probes) meets this criterion (Figure 25). The probe trained at the steered token shifts the target answer more than every random direction in four beta.log settings, but it shifts the other files’ answers by 0.68 to 1.23 times as much. Within the tested settings, adding probe directions at this position shifts the answers about all files together.
