# PACE-Mem: Prefix-Aware Complementary Evidence for Multimodal Agents 

Shidu Ren, Rong-cheng Tu, Hang Zhou, and Xiao Luo 

Abstract —As multimodal agents accumulate experience, their long-term memories contain many similar observations of recur-ring scenes, actions, and entities. Answer quality depends on the evidence retrieved from these memories into the model’s context. However, query-relevant memories may contain overlapping information, and a fixed retrieval depth may return too little or too much evidence for a given question. To address these challenges, we propose PACE-Mem, Prefix-Aware Complementary Evidence, which constructs a complementary evidence sequence and determines its length from the same selection trajectory. For structured memories, query-guided pooling combines records within each clip to obtain relevance scores and representations. Using these relevance scores and representations, maximal marginal relevance (MMR) selection balances query relevance against overlap with previously selected evidence. From the resulting score trajectory, a prefix-length selection algorithm determines retrieval depth by comparing length-normalized adjacent declines, accounting for both score changes and the amount of evidence accumulated. PACE-Mem is a training-free, plug-in retrieval method for clip-based agent memory systems, leaving memory construction and answer generation unchanged. Across three long-video benchmarks and two agent frameworks, PACE-Mem improves question-answering accuracy by an average of 1.6% over Adaptive-k, the strongest baseline at comparable mean retrieval depths. Further analysis reveals that jointly adding and removing evidence is associated with larger net accuracy gains than either change alone. 

Index Terms —Long-term memory, memory retrieval, multi-modal agents, retrieval depth, video question answering. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

# MULTIMODAL agents use long-term memory to answer questions about visual events and speech encountered in past experiences [1], [2], [3]. As interactions accumulate, this memory preserves information beyond the agent’s current observation. Retrieval brings relevant experiences into the model’s context [4], [5], making the evidence available at answering time depend on which parts of the stored history are returned. Agent memory retrieval operates over an accumulated stream of experience, where relevant records can be strongly correlated.

This overlap within a shared history distinguishes memory re-trieval from search over heterogeneous document collections [6]. In multimodal memory, adjacent video intervals often depict the same scene, people, or ongoing action. An event may appear in both visual descriptions and speech transcripts, and structured memory may associate multiple episodic and semantic records with one interval. Ranking records independently by relevance can therefore return repeated content while overlooking a decisive detail. In the top example of Fig. 1, relevance-only retrieval returns similar pole-vault views but misses the view revealing contact with the bar. Evidence needs also vary by question: a short context may omit necessary facts, while a long one can make relevant information difficult to use [7]. The middle and bottom examples of Fig. 1 illustrate a missed check handoff and the inclusion of visually salient but unrelated artwork, respectively. These examples show why retrieval must determine both which records to return and how many. A longer context can preserve existing evidence while adding new clips, whereas changing its composition can introduce a missing fact and remove competing information without increasing its size.

Existing work addresses agent memory through memory-system design and retrieval-time evidence selection. xMemory builds a revisable hierarchy of memory components and groups, then retrieves through this hierarchy with uncertainty-guided evidence expansion [6]. A-MEM organizes memories as interconnected notes and updates their links and contextual attributes as new experiences arrive [8]. A complementary direction develops retrieval methods that control which evidence enters the answer context. Adaptive-RAG uses a question-complexity classifier to choose among no retrieval, single-step retrieval, and iterative retrieval [9]. Adaptive-k locates the largest gap in ranked query–document relevance scores and extends the resulting cutoff with a fixed buffer [10]. Redundancy-aware selection has also been combined with quantity control, using relative declines between consecutive maximal marginal relevance (MMR) scores or gaps between utilities that account for redundancy [11], [12]. Improved memory organization alone does not ensure complementary evidence or a suitable retrieval depth. Even structured memories can retain overlapping observations of the same scene from similar viewpoints. Fixed-depth retrieval cannot adapt to varying evidence needs, while question-based routing and initial relevance-gap cutoffs do not track overlap with subsequently selected evidence. Redundancy-aware selection accounts for this overlap, but adjacent score declines alone do not explicitly reflect the amount of evidence already selected. The remaining question is how to compare candidate stopping points that correspond to different prefix lengths. To address this problem, we propose PACE-Mem, Prefix-Aware Complementary Evidence , which determines evidence composition and retrieval depth from a shared selection trajec-tory. For structured memories, query-guided pooling combines related records within each clip to obtain relevance scores and representations for ranking and redundancy assessment. Flat memories use their existing clip representations. MMR [13] then constructs an evidence sequence by balancing query relevance against overlap with earlier selections. From the recorded scores, our prefix-length selection algorithm chooses the initial portion of this sequence to return. It compares adjacent score declines after length normalization, accounting for both local score changes and the amount of evidence accu-mulated. PACE-Mem is training-free and can be plugged into these memory interfaces without changing memory construction or answer generation. Evidence selection requires no additional foundation-model calls. We evaluate PACE-Mem with M3-Agent and a retrieval-based SiLVR configuration on M3Bench Robot, M3Bench Web, and VideoMME-Long. Average accuracy rises from 44.2% with Original M3-Agent to 52.2% with PACE-Mem, an 8.0% gain. Across the two frameworks and three benchmarks, PACE-Mem improves accuracy by an average of 1.6% over Adaptive-k, the strongest baseline at comparable mean retrieval depths. Further evidence-set analysis shows that similar retrieval depths can correspond to different evidence compositions. In SiLVR’s single-retrieval setting, questions for which PACE-Mem both adds and removes clips relative to Fixed-7 exhibit larger within-group net accuracy gains than those involving only additions or only removals. This finding highlights the importance of evidence composition: retrieval must determine not only how much evidence to return, but also which evidence to retain, introduce, and remove. Our contributions are as follows:  

> •

We formulate memory retrieval as the joint selection of evidence composition and retrieval depth, where the returned amount depends on the complementary evidence accumulated for each question.  

> •

We propose PACE-Mem, a training-free, plug-in method that combines complementary sequence construction with length-normalized prefix-length selection. For structured memories, query-guided pooling supplies relevance scores and representations for both candidate ranking and redun-dancy assessment.  

> •

We evaluate PACE-Mem in two agent frameworks across three long-video benchmarks, covering structured and flat memory interfaces. Paired evidence-set analysis relates membership changes to answer corrections and regres-sions, complementing aggregate accuracy and retrieval-depth comparisons. II. R ELATED WORK 

A. Agent Memory 

Agent memory systems organize, update, and retrieve accu-mulated experience to support later interactions [14]. Generative Agents and MemoryBank retain observations or conversational information, while Reflexion stores feedback to guide sub-sequent attempts [4], [5], [15]. MemGPT and MemoryOS manage growing histories across memory tiers [16], [17]. To update content, Mem0 reconciles extracted conversational facts with existing memories, while A-MEM links new notes to prior records and revises their contextual attributes [8], [18]. xMemory addresses correlated records through hierarchical organization and uncertainty-guided expansion [6]. For mul-timodal experience, M3-Agent links episodic and semantic records around entities and retrieves them during iterative reasoning [1]. SiLVR [2] provides video descriptions that form the flat evidence store in our retrieval-based configuration. Mem-ory organization alone does not determine which overlapping records to return for a particular question. PACE-Mem selects complementary evidence and its quantity over these structured and flat interfaces without changing memory construction. 

B. Long-Video Reasoning 

Long-video reasoning requires identifying and integrating relevant evidence across time under limited input capacity [19]. M3Bench, Video-MME, and related benchmarks evaluate reasoning over video content and long-term multimodal experi-ence [1], [3], [20]. Visual–language models provide alignment and video interaction capabilities [21], [22], [23], while MovieChat, Flash-VStream, and LongVU extend temporal coverage through memory or token compression [24], [25], [26]. Language-based approaches include concept-based video captioning [27], [28], and LLoVi reasons over descriptions of local intervals [29]. C. Adaptive Retrieval 

Adaptive retrieval controls evidence access in retrieval-augmented generation [39], [40], [41], [42], [43] using query characteristics, model feedback, or retrieval scores. Query and corpus features predict document counts [44], and Adaptive-RAG routes questions by complexity [9]. FLARE, Self-RAG, and CRAG use generation confidence, learned self-assessment, or evidence evaluation to guide retrieval [45], [46], [47]. Score-based control includes Adaptive-k’s relevance-gap cutoff with a buffer and EviRerank’s threshold relative to the top-scoring block [10], [48]. A cutoff derived from the initial relevance ranking does not reflect how a candidate’s novelty changes as evidence is selected. This motivates coupling quantity control to a redundancy-aware selection sequence. MMR penalizes overlap with earlier selections [13], and DF-RAG adjusts its geometric variant’s diversity weight through model-based planning and evaluation [49]. Extending redundancy-aware selection to quantity control, MMR-based microblog retrieval uses relative score declines between consecutive results as a stopping criterion [11]. AdaGReS selects evidence with positive redundancy-aware gains under a token budget [50], whereas AdaGATE sets capacity from the largest gap in utilities incorporating redundancy and information-gap coverage [12]. Relative score declines capture local changes but do not explicitly include the length of the preceding evidence prefix. PACE-Mem incorporates this length through a prefix-dependent power transformation of declines normalized by the initial score. This adjusts the comparison of candidate stopping positions according to the amount of evidence already selected. III. P REFIX -A WARE COMPLEMENTARY EVIDENCE 

A. Overview 

PACE-Mem couples complementary evidence selection with adaptive retrieval depth through three connected stages (Fig. 2). For structured memories, query-guided pooling groups related records within each clip and aggregates query-relevant groups into a relevance score and representation. MMR uses these outputs to construct an evidence sequence, balancing query relevance against overlap with previously selected clips. Prefix-length selection uses the same MMR score trajectory to determine how many clips to return by comparing length-normalized score declines. Records from the selected prefix are supplied chronologically to the answer model. Algorithm 1 summarizes the procedure.

Baselines. We compare PACE-Mem with fixed-depth re-trieval and two adaptive context controllers within each host framework, alongside Original M3-Agent. M3-Agent retrieves during ordinary memory searches under its existing control policy, whereas the SiLVR configuration retrieves once from flat caption and ASR memory. Original M3-Agent retains its released relevance threshold and chain-of-retrieval behavior. The other M3-Agent variants remove this threshold and backfill candidates to share a common candidate-admission policy. Fixed-k returns the highest-ranked clips by dense relevance. Fixed-4 in M3-Agent and Fixed-7 in SiLVR provide comparisons at similar mean retrieval depths, while Fixed-5 and Fixed-8 provide additional comparisons with larger returned contexts. Adaptive-k selects a relevance-gap boundary and appends a buffer [10]. Adaptive-RAG uses its released T5-large complexity classifier without retraining [9]; we map its three retrieval-strategy classes to context depths while retaining each host’s reasoning loop. The buffers and class mappings approximate PACE-Mem’s mean returned depth within each host and remain fixed across benchmarks. 

Metrics. We evaluate answer quality using accuracy and returned evidence quantity using Clips. Accuracy is determined by exact option matching on VideoMME-Long and GPT-4o correctness judgments on M3Bench [52]. All methods use the same judging prompt, containing the question, reference answer, and generated answer; method identities and retrieval budgets are omitted. Avg. Acc. is the unweighted mean accuracy across the three benchmarks, rounded to one decimal place. For the overall comparison, we select the single baseline with the highest mean accuracy across both frameworks and all three benchmarks among those at comparable mean retrieval depths. Overall gains are averaged across these six evaluations before rounding. Clips is the mean number of evidence clips returned per nonempty ordinary retrieval call, excluding M3-Agent’s entity lookups and other searches. It measures evidence quantity at the retrieval-call level, where record lengths and the number of calls per question can vary. Performance is stable across the tested relevance weights. 

We vary the MMR relevance weight λ from 0.70 to 0.91 on SiLVR VideoMME-Long while keeping the other retrieval settings fixed. Larger values emphasize query relevance, while smaller values give more weight to avoiding overlap with selected clips. In Fig. 4(a), accuracy varies nonmonotonically between 58.4% and 59.0%, and mean depth ranges from 7.17 to 7.25. The default λ = 0 .85 matches the highest accuracy at λ = 0 .79 . The narrow accuracy and depth ranges indicate that the observed performance is maintained across several relevance–overlap balances within this interval, rather than depending on a single sharply tuned weight. 

PACE-Mem varies retrieval depth across questions. 

Similar mean depths can conceal different evidence allocations, as shown in Fig. 4(b). PACE-Mem returns two to eight clips, with fewer than eight returned for 52.2% of Robot questions, 53.1% of Web questions, and 49.4% of VideoMME-Long questions. Eight is the most frequent depth and equals the configured cap Kmax . Prefixes of at most five clips account for 10.6%, 11.6%, and 7.3% of questions, respectively, so shorter contexts serve a minority while most questions receive six to eight clips. Adaptive-k mainly returns six to eight clips after its five-clip buffer, with six accounting for 47.0%–49.6% of questions. Adaptive-RAG returns seven clips for 804 of the 900 VideoMME-Long questions. Compared with these more concentrated allocations, PACE-Mem uses a broader range of depths while preserving longer contexts for most questions. Together with its higher average accuracy at similar mean depths (Table I), this distribution supports adapting the returned prefix to the question-specific evidence trajectory rather than applying a nearly uniform context length.

This case illustrates why evidence selection must preserve facts that distinguish the target event from similar events, rather than treating topical similarity alone as evidence redundancy. V. C ONCLUSION 

We presented PACE-Mem, a training-free, plug-in retrieval method that jointly determines which memories to return and how much evidence to provide. A shared score trajectory con-nects complementary evidence selection with query-dependent retrieval depth. Across three benchmarks and two agent frame-works, PACE-Mem improves answer accuracy by an average of 1.6% over Adaptive-k, the strongest baseline at comparable mean retrieval depths, without additional foundation-model calls for evidence selection. Results across structured and flat memories, together with fixed-depth ablations, support the importance of evidence composition alongside context length. Similar memories can nevertheless contain distinct event-specific facts required for an answer. Future work will explore preserving these distinctions during redundancy assessment and extending prefix selection to account for evidence accumulated across retrieval rounds. 10
