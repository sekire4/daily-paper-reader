Abstract 

Retrieval-augmented QA can fail even when the correct answer is in the retrieved evidence. This can happen when a competing answer sat-isfies some, but not all, of the question’s re-quirements and is mentioned frequently in the retrieved documents, which can make it ap-pear to be the best-supported answer. To test whether this retrieval skew actually affects the decision, we manipulate it directly: increasing the prevalence of passages that favor the com-peting answer makes the model more likely to select it. This points to a decision problem that candidate-level support alone cannot resolve. We therefore introduce Joint Constraint Ag-gregation (JCA), which compares candidates against the same question-derived requirements using the retrieved evidence. Across three eval-uation settings and three instruction-tuned back-bones, JCA improves overall accuracy, and its advantage grows when retrieval favors a com-peting answer. Together, these results suggest that when partial matches and retrieval promi-nence pull models toward a competing answer, comparing candidates against the question’s full requirements can make selection more reli-able. 

1 Introduction 

Retrieval-augmented QA (Lewis et al., 2020; Izac-ard and Grave, 2021) can fail even when the re-trieved evidence contains the correct answer. One difficult case arises when a competing answer is also plausible: it may be correct for a closely re-lated interpretation of the question, but not for the question as stated. A time period, version, role, ju-risdiction, or other qualifier may determine which otherwise-supported answer applies. We study this problem as qualifier-sensitive answer selec-tion : given retrieved evidence and a set of plausible candidates that includes the correct answer, select the candidate that answers the question as stated. Conditioning on the correct candidate being avail-able isolates selection from failures to retrieve or generate it. The difficulty is not simply whether each candi-date has supporting evidence. Evidence that sup-ports a candidate is not necessarily evidence that the candidate answers the question being asked. When several candidates have legitimate support, the key distinction may be whether that evidence establishes the particular requirement that makes one candidate applicable. This motivates compar-ing candidates against the same question-derived requirements rather than treating candidate-level support as sufficient for selection. Figure 1 illus-trates this setting and the structured comparison we introduce below. Retrieval can amplify this problem when it makes a plausible near-miss more salient than the applicable answer. In naturally retrieved evidence, baseline accuracy declines as passages mention the near-miss more often relative to the correct an-swer. We also manipulate this imbalance directly: holding the question, candidate set, and neutral ev-idence fixed, shifting relative support toward the near-miss increases near-miss selection. Together, these analyses show that relative retrieval support can affect the downstream choice among plausible answers. We introduce Joint Constraint Aggregation (JCA) 1 to make comparison under shared question requirements explicit. JCA represents the ques-tion requirements as a shared set of atomic con-straints, checks each candidate against those same constraints using the retrieved evidence, and jointly compares the resulting evidence-grounded reports. In this way, all candidates are evaluated under the same question-derived criteria. We evaluate JCA across three qualifier-sensitive settings spanning human-annotated near-misses,

2 Related Work 

Evidence support does not always determine ap-plicability. Verification methods make evidence support explicit. In QA, predictions can be recast as hypotheses and checked against retrieved con-text with natural language inference (Chen et al., 2021b), while factuality evaluation can decompose generated text into atomic claims and verify each against external evidence (Min et al., 2023). These approaches are closely related to JCA’s candidate-checking stage, which asks what the available evi-dence establishes about each candidate. Candidate reranking instead addresses selection among alternatives. Open-domain QA rerankers aggregate evidence across passages (Wang et al., 2018) or learn to distinguish close false positives within a candidate set (Iyer et al., 2021). In our set-ting, however, multiple candidates may each have legitimate supporting evidence. The remaining de-cision is which candidate’s evidence establishes the requirements imposed by the question. JCA makes those requirements shared across candidates before comparison. 

Decomposing a problem differs from compar-ing candidates. Prompting methods can break a complex problem into simpler subproblems that are solved in sequence, with each answer informing the next (Zhou et al., 2023), or delegate subtasks to prompts specialized for them (Khot et al., 2023). These methods decompose the procedure for pro-ducing an answer. JCA instead decomposes what the question requires and applies the same require-ments to every candidate, so that candidates with partial support can be compared on equal terms. Decomposition alone also does not explain JCA’s gains. Our controls that use the same extracted requirements but score candidates independently (Constrained NLI) or combine verdicts with a fixed rule (Constraint Tally) remain below full JCA in 2every setting (Table 1 and Appendix B). 

Retrieval composition can shift candidate selec-tion. Prior work shows that downstream QA de-pends on what retrieval surfaces. Retrievers can favor more prominent entities under ambiguous queries (Chen et al., 2021a), related but irrelevant passages can distract RAG models (Amiraz et al., 2025), and imperfect retrieval can introduce con-flicting evidence (Wang et al., 2025). Our setting complements these findings: evidence favoring a near-miss need not be irrelevant, erroneous, or con-tradictory, but may correctly support that candidate for a nearby interpretation. We study the down-stream choice when retrieval makes such a plausi-ble near-miss especially salient. 

Ambiguity and context create plausible alter-natives. Ambiguity-aware QA makes multiple valid interpretations of a question explicit. Am-bigQA pairs plausible answers with disambiguated question rewrites (Min et al., 2020), while ASQA produces answers spanning multiple valid readings (Stelmakh et al., 2022). AmbigDocs extends this ambiguity to retrieved documents. When docu-ments describe different entities that share a name, models often merge information across them (Lee et al., 2024). There, the question itself is ambigu-ous and a complete answer covers each entity. In our setting, the question specifies which reading applies, and evidence for the near-miss can be ac-curate while answering a different reading. These settings illustrate why a competing answer can be genuinely plausible rather than simply false: it may answer a related interpretation of the question. Answer validity can also depend on specified context. SituatedQA studies questions whose an-swers vary with temporal or geographic context (Zhang and Choi, 2021). Our evaluation draws on both forms of variation but focuses on the down-stream decision among plausible alternatives once the target interpretation or context is specified. 

3 Experimental Setup 

3.1 Task and Evaluation Design 

We study retrieval-augmented QA as candidate selection. Each instance contains a question, re-trieved passages, and a small set of plausible can-didate answers that includes the correct answer. The task is to select the candidate that answers the question as stated. We therefore evaluate selection conditional on candidate recall rather than failures to retrieve, extract, or generate the correct candi-date. We use three evaluation conditions that vary where the candidate alternatives come from and what determines which candidate applies. Am-bigQA uses human-annotated alternatives asso-ciated with nearby interpretations. AmbigQA-Extracted keeps the ambiguity setting but derives candidates from retrieved passages. SituatedQA-temporal instead tests applicability determined by explicit temporal context. 

AmbigQA. We begin with AmbigQA (Min et al., 2020), which provides disambiguated ques-tion–answer pairs for questions with multiple valid interpretations. We use its human-annotated alter-natives to form candidate sets in which a distractor can be a valid answer to a nearby interpretation even though it does not answer the target question. This condition therefore tests selection among plau-sible alternatives supplied by the dataset. 

AmbigQA-Extracted. To test the same type of selection when candidates come from re-trieval rather than human annotation, we construct AmbigQA-Extracted from AmbigQA training ques-tions. Qwen3-30B extracts plausible candidates from the retrieved passages without access to the gold answer. The resulting candidate sets are gen-erated once and fixed across all methods and evalu-ated backbones. After deduplication, we evaluate only instances in which the gold answer is recov-ered and at least two distinct candidate groups re-main. Appendix A.2 reports candidate-recovery coverage, and the code release documents the full extraction procedure. 

SituatedQA-temporal. We use the temporal sub-set of SituatedQA (Zhang and Choi, 2021) to test a different source of applicability. The same ques-tion can have different correct answers at different times. For each instance, the answer associated with the target temporal context is the gold candi-date, while answers that are correct for the same question under other temporal contexts serve as plausible distractors. This condition therefore tests whether the model selects the answer that applies to the time specified by the question. 

3.2 Evidence and Instance Construction 

For all three conditions, we retrieve the top-k = 20 

results for each question using the Brave Search 3API 2 and use their extracted page text as evidence. All methods begin from the same ordered top-20 passage pool. Passage text is truncated as needed to fit the context available to each backbone. Ap-pendix A.1 provides retrieval, mention-matching, and truncation details. 

Instance eligibility. To make selection errors in-terpretable, an instance must present a well-defined choice among alternatives that are actually surfaced by the evidence. For AmbigQA and SituatedQA-temporal, we therefore require the correct candi-date and at least one distractor to each be mentioned in at least 10% of the top-20 retrieved passages, us-ing alias-based whole-word matching. We do not apply this requirement to AmbigQA-Extracted be-cause its candidates are themselves extracted from the retrieved passages. We also exclude malformed instances or answer structures that do not support a well-defined single-candidate selection problem. Exact screening criteria and counts are provided in the code release. For a selection error to be meaningful, the gold candidate must also be uniquely applicable to the target question. Because the alternatives are de-liberately drawn from nearby valid interpretations or contexts, a nominal distractor can occasionally also be a defensible answer to the exact target ques-tion. To avoid counting such a choice as an error, we use GPT-5.5 (OpenAI, 2026) to classify each distractor as DISTINCT , CO -CORRECT , or UNSURE .We exclude an instance if any distractor is judged

4 Methods 

We denote a question by q, retrieved passages by 

P , and candidate answers by A = {a1, . . . , a n}.Evidence construction, filtering, and model setup are described in Section 3. 

4.1 Comparison Methods Set-level selection. Three baselines receive the question, retrieved passages, and candidate set to-gether and return one candidate in a single com-pletion. Direct selects an answer without an ex-plicit reasoning instruction. CoT (Wei et al., 2022) instead prompts the model to reason over the re-trieved evidence before selecting. Comparative Prompting (Compare) explicitly asks the model to compare the candidates before choosing among them. These approaches vary how reasoning and 4comparison are elicited, but do not explicitly repre-sent the question requirements as a shared con-straint set with intermediate candidate-level re-ports. 

Independent evidence scoring. NLI scores each candidate separately. For each candidate ai, we form the hypothesis “The answer to the question q

is ai” and score it against P . Following Chen and Eger (2023) and Steen et al. (2023), we compute 

score( ai) = P (ENTAIL ) − P (CONTRADICT ),

(1) where P (·) is a normalized label probability read from the model’s output distribution at the label position. The candidate with the highest score is selected. We additionally evaluate two cross-encoder rerankers and CoT-SC, a self-consistency variant of CoT (Wang et al., 2023). Their definitions and results are reported in Appendices F.2 and F.1. 

4.2 Joint Constraint Aggregation 

JCA makes comparison under shared question re-quirements explicit. It represents the question re-quirements as atomic constraints, checks each can-didate against the same constraints using the re-trieved evidence, and then compares the resulting candidate reports jointly (Figure 1). 

Step 1: Extract constraints. The model decom-poses q into a small set of m atomic constraints. Each constraint captures one requirement implied by the question, such as an answer type, role, time period, version, or scope. This step operates on the question alone, so the same constraint set is used for every candidate. 

Step 2: Check candidates. For each candidate 

ai, the model evaluates all m constraints against the retrieved passages P . For each constraint, it produces a verdict in {S ATISFIES , V IOLATES ,NOT _S TATED } and a brief evidence-grounded ra-tionale. Across candidates, this produces an n × m

collection of reports under the same question re-quirements. In the primary staged implementation, each candidate is checked in a separate model call. 

Step 3: Jointly aggregate. The aggregation model receives the question, candidate set, and all constraint-checking reports, including their ver-dicts and rationales. It selects the candidate that best satisfies the question requirements relative to the alternatives. Step 3 does not receive the re-trieved passages directly, so its decision is based on the information preserved in the candidate reports, together with the model’s own knowledge of the question and candidates. The main experiments use staged JCA, with one constraint-extraction call, one Step 2 call per can-didate, and one final aggregation call. These three operations define the JCA procedure, while their packaging into model calls is an implementation choice examined below. 

4.3 Controls and Variants Step 3 controls. To analyze the final aggrega-tion while holding the earlier stages fixed, Con-straint Tally and Verdict-only aggregation both reuse the staged JCA constraint checks. Con-straint Tally replaces Step 3 with a deterministic signed tally over the verdicts: S ATISFIES = +1 ,NOT _S TATED = 0 , and V IOLATES = −1. The candidate with the highest tally is selected, with 

1/k credit when the gold candidate is among k

tied winners. Verdict-only aggregation instead re-moves the rationales from the same Step 2 reports before the joint Step 3 decision, leaving only the constraint verdict labels. Comparing Constraint Tally with verdict-only aggregation varies the de-cision rule while holding the verdict labels fixed. Comparing verdict-only aggregation with full JCA varies rationale availability while holding the joint aggregation procedure fixed. 

Constraint-aware independent scoring. Con-strained NLI provides a different control. It uses JCA’s extracted question constraints but does not reuse JCA’s Step 2 reports. Instead, for each can-didate it jointly produces its own per-constraint judgments and an NLI label from the retrieved evidence, then applies the same entailment-minus-contradiction scoring rule as NLI. This retains inde-pendent candidate scoring while making the ques-tion constraints explicit. 

Call-structure variants. We also evaluate two variants that change how JCA’s operations are pack-aged into calls. Merged-check JCA retains the three-stage procedure but checks all candidates to-gether in a single Step 2 call rather than one call per candidate. OnePass JCA instead asks a sin-gle completion to extract the constraints, evaluate the candidates, and select the final answer. These variants distinguish batching within the checking stage from collapsing the explicit stage boundaries. 5Exact prompts and implementation details are pro-vided in the code release. 

5 Results 

We begin by establishing JCA’s gains on the full evaluation sets and hard slices, then examine how relative retrieval support affects selection among plausible answers. We next hold the candidate checks fixed to isolate what the final joint aggre-gation contributes. Finally, we examine how the procedure behaves under alternative call structures and with a reasoning model. 

5.1 Main Selection Results JCA improves selection beyond the targeted hard regime. We first ask whether JCA’s gains are confined to the frequency-skewed cases that mo-tivate our diagnostic analysis. They are not. With Llama4-Scout-17B, JCA achieves the highest accu-racy on all three full evaluation sets (Table 1), and the same holds for Qwen3-30B and Gemma3-27B (Appendix B). This establishes that the advantage extends to qualifier-sensitive candidate selection more broadly, rather than appearing only when re-trieval favors a near-miss. We next examine what changes in that more challenging regime. On the hard slice, a plausible near-miss is mentioned in more retrieved passages than the correct answer, making retrieval salience less aligned with the question. Here the separation between JCA and the primary baselines becomes larger. Against CoT, the strongest primary base-line with Llama4-Scout, JCA’s margins range from 4.0–11.3 points on the full sets and from 6.1–15.5 points on the hard slices. Thus, JCA’s advantage is present on the full evaluation, but is more pro-nounced when retrieval favors a competing answer. The comparisons in Table 1 are statistically signifi-cant except for JCA versus Constrained NLI on the SituatedQA hard slice, where JCA remains higher but the confidence interval includes zero. 

The hard-slice result holds across backbones. 

The Llama4 results leave open whether this pattern depends on a particular model family. Table 2 there-fore extends the hard-slice comparison to Qwen3-30B and Gemma3-27B. Across the three evaluation conditions and three instruction-tuned backbones, JCA achieves the highest hard-slice accuracy in all nine combinations. The strongest competing baseline varies across settings, but the JCA ranking remains unchanged.

5.3 What Does Joint Aggregation Add? 

JCA’s final stage compares the candidate reports produced by the shared constraint checks. We ask whether that joint comparison adds anything once the Step 2 reports already exist, and whether any additional benefit comes from the rationale text in those reports. To separate these effects, we com-pare three aggregation rules over the same Step 2 outputs: deterministic Constraint Tally, joint aggre-gation over the verdict labels alone, and full JCA with the rationales restored. Table 3 shows that joint comparison helps even without access to the rationales. Replacing Con-straint Tally with verdict-only aggregation im-proves full-set accuracy on all three evaluation con-ditions, by 3.8 points on both AmbigQA conditions and 9.3 points on SituatedQA. Restoring the ratio-nales provides an additional 3–4 points on the two AmbigQA conditions, while the 1.3-point increase on SituatedQA is not statistically significant. The Step 3 gain therefore cannot be explained only by rationale content. Joint comparison adds value be-yond a fixed decision rule over the same verdicts (although the Step 3 model can also draw on its own knowledge), while rationales can preserve ad-ditional information beyond the verdict labels. The benefit is concentrated in cases where the verdicts do not already settle the choice. When the constraint checks decisively favor the gold candi-date, full JCA is slightly below Constraint Tally (−0.5 to −3.2 points). In contrast, when multiple candidates are tied under the verdicts, the total gain from Step 3 exceeds 30 points in all three condi-tions. Large gains also occur when the verdicts rank a distractor above the gold answer. These cases are precisely where a fixed tally leaves the final decision unresolved or points in the wrong direction. Rationale content can also matter when verdict labels do not distinguish the candidates. 5.4 Design and Inference Trade-offs 

We next vary how JCA’s three operations are pack-aged into calls and how the procedure interacts with Qwen3’s reasoning mode. 

Batching candidate checks differs from collaps-ing the procedure. OnePass JCA performs con-straint extraction, candidate checking, and final selection within one completion. In the tested instruction-model settings, this substantially re-duces accuracy: across Qwen3-30B and Llama4-Scout-17B, OnePass trails staged JCA by approxi-mately 4–10 points on the full evaluation sets and 10–18 points on the hard slices, with all differences statistically significant. By contrast, Merged-check JCA retains the three operations but evaluates all candidates to-gether in Step 2. Pooled over the 2,018 full-set instances within each backbone, Merged-check is non-inferior to staged JCA at the prespecified 2.5-point margin for Qwen3-30B, Gemma3-27B, and Llama4-Scout-17B, with accuracy differences of +0 .74 , +0 .05 , and +0 .25 points, respectively. Thus, separate per-candidate Step 2 calls are not required to preserve pooled accuracy, whereas col-lapsing the full procedure into one completion de-grades accuracy in the tested instruction settings. 

Merged and staged JCA occupy different infer-ence operating points. In a dedicated Qwen3-30B measurement pass, Merged-check reduces the measured candidate-checking and answer-stage in-put by 55% on AmbigQA and 49% on SituatedQA relative to staged JCA, while also using fewer re-quests. Staged JCA, however, exposes independent candidate checks that can be dispatched concur-rently. Under our derived candidate-parallel ac-counting, it has lower latency on both measured datasets. Thus, batching reduces repeated input and request count, while separate checks can favor parallel latency. Full measurements and accounting details are reported in Appendix E.4. 

The staged-JCA advantage is much smaller with reasoning enabled. Without Qwen3’s reasoning mode, staged JCA exceeds CoT by 7.1–20.6 points across the three hard slices. With reasoning en-abled for both methods, the differences shrink to 8+1 .0, +1 .0, and +2 .9 points (Table 4), and none is statistically significant. We therefore do not es-tablish an additional accuracy benefit for staged JCA in this Qwen3 reasoning setting. At the pre-specified 2.5-point margin, staged JCA with rea-soning is non-inferior to CoT with reasoning on AmbigQA and SituatedQA, while the AmbigQA-Extracted comparison is too imprecise to certify (Appendix E.3). Overall, the results separate the structured pro-cedure from its most expensive implementation: collapsing all three operations hurts in the tested instruction settings, whereas batching candidate checks preserves pooled accuracy while substan-tially reducing repeated input. 

6 Conclusion 

A candidate can be well supported by retrieved evi-dence and still be the wrong answer to the question being asked. Our experiments show that this dis-tinction matters when several plausible answers are supported: retrieval can make a near-miss more salient, while explicitly comparing candidates un-der the same question requirements substantially improves selection in the tested instruction settings. The relevant question is therefore not only whether evidence supports an answer, but whether it estab-lishes that the answer applies to the question at hand. Across the component and design analyses, a consistent picture emerges. The useful abstraction is comparison under shared question requirements, rather than one separate call per candidate. Col-lapsing all three stages into a single completion, however, substantially hurts accuracy. Joint com-parison adds value beyond fixed aggregation, while candidate checks can be packaged differently with-out giving up the structured decision process. JCA therefore provides one concrete way to preserve the distinction between evidence that supports an an-swer and evidence that establishes that the answer applies to the question at hand. 

Limitations 

Our experiments isolate answer selection condi-tional on candidate recall. They therefore do not measure whether an end-to-end RAG system re-trieves or generates the correct candidate in the first place. This distinction is substantial in AmbigQA-Extracted: the extraction pipeline recovers the gold answer for 68.6% of questions that produce candi-dates, and candidate recovery therefore remains a substantial upstream bottleneck. JCA improves the subsequent selection decision but does not address this recovery bottleneck. Our evaluation also prioritizes clean, distinguish-able near-misses. The LLM-assisted co-correct fil-ter removes some valid cases along with ambiguous ones, and the hard slice is defined by alias-based mention frequency rather than semantic support. We therefore treat it as a diagnostic slice, not an es-timate of how often such failures occur in deployed systems. Its membership can also change when the mention gap is recomputed over only the evidence visible within a model’s context window. Finally, the accuracy benefit of explicit JCA structure depends on the model regime. Our main gains are obtained with instruction-tuned models. With Qwen3-30B’s reasoning variant, staged JCA remains 1–3 points higher than CoT-thinking on the three hard slices, but none of these differences is statistically significant. Whether explicit JCA provides additional accuracy benefits for other rea-soning models or substantially larger candidate sets remains open. Staged JCA also costs more than single-call base-lines: it issues one call per candidate plus extraction and aggregation calls, although merged candidate checking substantially reduces repeated input (Ap-pendix E.4). Our evidence comes from a snapshot of live web search. We release the retrieved URLs, titles, snippets, and per-candidate mention statis-tics, but not the page text, and pages fetched later may differ from the originals.

A Experimental and Evaluation Details 

A.1 Retrieval and Mention Matching 

For each question, we retrieve the top-k=20 results from the Brave Search API and use the extracted page text as evidence. All methods begin from the same ordered passage pool. Retrieved text is lim-ited to the smaller of a 48k-token evidence cap and the context space available to the backbone. The 48k cap is operative for Qwen3-30B and Gemma3-27B. For Llama4-Scout-17B, the effective evidence budget is approximately 31.5k tokens, with small method-dependent variation from prompt and out-put budgets. Mention frequency is computed from the fixed top-20 retrieval before model-specific truncation. We use alias-based whole-word matching, count-ing a passage once for a candidate if any of its aliases appears. The mention rate is the fraction of retrieved passages containing the candidate. Full re-trieval, matching, and truncation implementations are provided in the code release. 

A.2 AmbigQA-Extracted Candidate Recovery 

For AmbigQA-Extracted, we attempted candidate extraction on 2,000 AmbigQA training questions using Qwen3-30B without access to the gold an-swer. At least one candidate was extracted for 1,964 questions. Among these, the gold answer was recoverable after extraction and deduplication for 1,347 questions (68.6%). Selection evaluation is then restricted to instances where the gold an-swer is available and at least two distinct candidate groups remain, followed by the evaluation filters described in Section 3. The final evaluation set contains 548 instances. Full extraction and dedupli-cation procedures are provided in the code release.

C Controlled Retrieval-Support Intervention 

To test whether relative retrieval support itself can change answer selection, we construct controlled evidence sets from each question’s own top-20 re-trieval and evaluate Qwen3-30B on them. For each instance, we hold the question, candidate set, and neutral evidence fixed while varying the ratio of passages supporting the gold answer to passages supporting a near-miss. AmbigQA and AmbigQA-Extracted use gold:near-miss support ratios {4:1 , 2:1 , 1:1 , 1:2 , 1:4 }. The primary Situ-atedQA analysis uses {2:1 , 1:1 , 1:2 } because its more extreme cells did not meet the minimum distinct-instance threshold for the primary panel. The primary outcome is whether the manipulated near-miss is selected. Table 8 reports near-miss selection at the endpoints of each support range. Near-miss selection increases between the inter-vention endpoints in all 15 dataset–method con-ditions, by 1.4–9.7 ×. The fitted analysis gives the same directional result. We define x =log( gold support /near-miss support ) and y = 1

when the near-miss is selected, so a negative co-efficient indicates greater near-miss selection as relative support shifts toward the near-miss. The fitted coefficient is negative in all 15 conditions, although only 13 of 15 are individually significant. The endpoint result should not be read as strict monotonicity over every intermediate support level: near-miss selection is stepwise monotonic in 9 of 15 conditions. The intervention fixes the neutral evidence, not the total amount of support evidence. Mean real-ized input volume varies by about 1.77 × across support conditions. A sensitivity analysis there-fore refits the model with the designed support-slot count, Scell , as an additional covariate. The fitted retrieval-support coefficient remains within its original bootstrap interval in all 15 conditions. This check supports the interpretation that the di-rectional effect is not explained by the associated change in support volume. Finally, the intervention tests whether chang-ing relative retrieval support affects selection, not whether one method is causally more robust than another. Of 24 paired between-method slope com-parisons, 20 are not statistically significant. We therefore draw no causal ranking of the methods from their fitted slopes.

F.2 Cross-Encoder Rerankers 

We also evaluate two backbone-independent cross-encoder rerankers: cross-encoder/ ms-marco-MiniLM-L-6-v2 (Wang et al., 2020; Bajaj et al., 2018) and 

BAAI/bge-reranker-large (Xiao et al., 2024). For each question–candidate pair, the reranker scores each retrieved passage separately. We aggregate passage-level scores for a candidate using either their maximum or their mean, then select the candidate with the highest aggregate score. The rerankers are used off-the-shelf without task-specific fine-tuning. Table 14 reports both aggregation variants. Maximum aggregation outperforms mean ag-gregation for both rerankers in every dataset and slice. BGE-large improves hard-slice accuracy over MiniLM by 8.5, 10.4, and 17.2 points on AmbigQA, AmbigQA-Extracted, and SituatedQA, respectively. It nevertheless remains 51.0, 39.6, and 35.3 points below the primary Llama4-Scout-17B staged-JCA results on those same hard slices. Its full-set accuracy does not consistently improve over MiniLM.
