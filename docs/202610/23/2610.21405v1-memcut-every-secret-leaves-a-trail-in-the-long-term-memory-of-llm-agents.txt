## MEM CUT : Every Secret Leaves a Trail in the Long-Term Memory of LLM Agents 

Cheng Zhang 1 Xuanning Cao 2 Zhicheng Zheng 1 Hui Xiong 1,3 

1The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology (Guangzhou) 

2University of Science and Technology of China

Abstract 

Long-term memory has become a crucial component of large language model (LLM) agents, with most research focusing on retriev-ing relevant information accurately and effi-ciently. However, a dependable memory sys-tem must also forget sensitive user data upon re-quest. To evaluate this capability, we introduce MEM CUT , a systematic benchmark for user-requested forgetting. Its 456 hand-designed se-crets in long-horizon conversations have fully annotated evidence trails to test recovery after deletion, including from messages that never state the secrets directly. We propose two recov-ery metrics. Native recovery measures reader success through the system’s retrieval, whereas residual recovery measures the same reader’s success when directly given surviving objects traced to annotated disclosures or evidence. We establish three reference deletion baselines to test how far existing retrievers and write models support forgetting across five memory designs and four LLMs. Disclosure removal reduces native recovery far more than residual recov-ery. LLM-guided deletion of ten additional turns from twenty retrieval candidates leaves 39–76% residual recovery. Subset sweeps (144 facts, three systems, two models) show that widening the pool at a fixed budget reduces residual recovery more than deleting all orig-inal candidates. These sweeps identify candi-date coverage as a bottleneck in the reference baselines.

Long-term memory is becoming an important part of LLM agents (Packer et al., 2023; Zhong et al., 2024; Chhikara et al., 2025), and most research has focused on retrieving the right memories quickly and accurately (Maharana et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2025). However, dependable memory should also forget sensitive information, such as a diagnosis or a confidential deal, when users ask. This ability has received much less attention than recall. MEM CUT contains 456 hand-designed secrets in long-horizon conversations. For each secret, we annotate the deletion request, disclosure, evidence in other records, and look-alike records that do not support it. 2 Related Work 

Long-term memory and recall. Long-term memory helps LLM agents reuse information across interactions (Zhong et al., 2024; Chhikara et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2024; Zhu et al., 2026). Most work focuses on accurate and efficient recall (Maharana et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2025). However, similarity-based retrieval may miss evidence linked through entities rather than similar wording, as in multi-hop QA (Yang et al., 2018; Trivedi et al., 2022). These omissions can also limit cleanup when retrieval supplies deletion candidates (§6.1). 

Forgetting and unlearning. Memory must also forget sensitive information. Parametric unlearn-ing and model editing target information in model weights, where an edited fact’s consequences can persist (Bourtoule et al., 2021; Cohen et al., 2024). In external memory, benchmarks test updated in-formation, reuse of obsolete facts, or direct recall after deletion (Hu et al., 2026; Uddin et al., 2026; Yang, 2026). MemLeak also examines recovery through correlated cues (Wang and Zhang, 2026). MEM CUT tests whether surviving records jointly reveal a secret that none states directly. We anno-tate every minimal supporting combination. Table 1 compares these benchmarks. 

3 The M EM CUT Benchmark 

3.1 Task definition 

We define a secret in M EM CUT as a protected fact ,and we call its trail the fact’s evidence , which con-sists of the turns that support it without stating it. Each sample pairs one protected fact with an 

episode , which is a long multi-session conversa-tion stored as objects traceable to their source turns. Figure 2 shows one sample. We also annotate its disclosure (turns stating it), its sufficient sets (minimal evidence combinations implying it, since the fact can be derived in many ways), and its distractors (look-alike turns that re-semble the evidence). Writing D for the disclosure, 

E for the evidence, and S for the sufficient sets, a deletion X forgets the fact when 

D ⊆ X and X ∩ S̸ = ∅ for every S ∈ S .

A set of evidence turns that meets every sufficient set is a cut , and the minimal cut is the smallest one. The task has two sides. A deleter receives a natural-language deletion request and removes objects from the store, and we then test whether the fact is still recoverable with a four-choice question that allows the model to decline.

3.3 Construction and properties 

MEM CUT uses author-specified evidence struc-tures to measure post-deletion derivability. Each secret comes with annotated evidence and minimal sufficient sets, so deletion can be checked against every annotated derivation. The evidence trail is designed to support the secret independently of its disclosure. Table 2 summarizes the structural properties and validation results supporting these measurements. Appendix efapp:data gives further statistics. 

Human design, model phrasing. We design each episode’s disclosures, evidence, sufficient sets, and distractors. GPT-6 renders this specified con-tent as dialogue. Authors check every turn against the design and revise deviations, spending at least twelve hours per episode. 

Long-horizon scenarios with something to lose. 

Deletion gets harder as a fact is buried in more unrelated activity, and it matters more when a leak would hurt someone. So each of our fifteen episodes follows one scenario across work, family, and social threads for one to three months (2,893 turns on average) and protects about thirty facts with a reason to stay private, 456 in all.

4 Experimental Setup 

4.1 Reference deletion baselines 

We establish three reference deletion baselines to benchmark forgetting with existing recall compo-nents. They reuse native retrievers and write mod-els with minimal adaptation. 

S0 deletes the disclosure and all objects derived from it, providing a common baseline for disclo-sure removal. 

S1 adds retrieval-ranked cleanup. For a request 

q, S1 constructs a twenty-turn candidate pool Pq

from one query to the system’s retrieval and deletes its ten highest-ranked turns. No fact has more than ten evidence turns, and a minimal cut needs at most five. The budget therefore suffices if supporting evidence is reached and selected. 

S2 reranks the same twenty candidates with the write model using their stored text and deletes the top ten (LLM-guided cleanup). The fixed pool isolates selection. Choice failures leave evidence unselected within the pool. Reach failures leave evidence outside it. Neither cleanup sees annota-tions. Section 6.1 varies pool and budget. At fixed cleanup budgets, the fraction of non-evidence se-lections provides a source-level reference measure of collateral damage (Appendix B.3). 

4.2 One unit for every system 

Systems store different objects, so deleting one object removes a turn in one system but a whole session in another, and object counts are not compa-rable. However, every object derives from turns, so we anchor every operation to the turn. We record each object’s source turns at ingestion, delete by closure, and count pools and budgets in turns, so systems differ only in what they store and how they retrieve. 4.3 Memory systems and models 

We compare five memory designs along two prop-erties, the unit retrieved and how much is derived (§6.2). Two retrieval stores keep every turn verba-tim, retrieve by dense similarity (Karpukhin et al., 2020; Lewis et al., 2020), and derive nothing. They retrieve turns ( Turn RAG ) or sessions ( Session RAG ), letting us examine granularity under their respective retrieval settings. Three memory sys-tems add derivation of increasing strength. Mem-oryBank keeps raw turns under daily summaries (Zhong et al., 2024), Nemori rewrites topical seg-ments into narratives (Ma et al., 2025), and Light-Mem consolidates summaries offline without the raw text (Fang et al., 2025). Each system keeps its own retrieval. The verbatim stores test whether residual recovery can persist without summariza-tion or rewriting. In each configuration, one write model writes memory, scores deletion candidates, and answers questions, as in a deployed assistant. We use four write models from four developers, DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash, Qwen3.8-Flash, GLM-5.3-Flash, and MiMo-V2.5, which perform strongly and are effi-cient enough for thousands of deletion runs, and we run every system with each of them to separate the effects of the memory design from those of the model. Each fact is evaluated on a fresh copy of the store, so deleting one fact cannot affect another. 

5 Results: Deleting Is Not Forgetting 

Tables 3 and 4 give the main results. Table 7 re-ports that residual recovery is 94–100% in every store before deletion, limiting the contribution of ingestion loss to post-deletion recovery. 5.3 Deletion increases the hidden-risk share 

Deletion increases the reported share of residual recovery missed by native retrieval. In Turn RAG the hidden-risk share goes from 5–9% before dele-tion to 39–42% after S0 and 76–82% after S2, and across all 20 configurations it ends above where it started, above one half in 14 of them, as the Hid. 

columns of Tables 3 and 4 show. Cleanup targets records retrieved by the deletion request, whereas question answering uses a differ-ent query. Evidence that is difficult to retrieve may survive cleanup and remain unseen during later questions. Native answers can therefore suggest successful forgetting even when the same reader still recovers the secret from surviving evidence. 

6 Analysis: Why Forgetting Fails 

To identify bottlenecks in forgetting with exist-ing components, we examine candidate coverage, memory design, and fact type.

Choice. Choice can fail, as E2 in Figure 4 shows. Deleting the whole pool tests whether removing the unselected candidates would materially improve forgetting. Measured recovery need not be mono-tonic in deletion, since the reader can respond dif-ferently to the remaining context. In Table 9, whole-pool deletion lowers residual recovery by at most 6.5 points versus S2 at budget ten. These subset sweeps identify candidate coverage as the main bottleneck within the evaluated cleanup baselines. 

Reach. Candidate reach limits these retrieval-based cleanup baselines. The pool holds only about half of a fact’s evidence, because other candidates resemble the request more than the evidence does. A minimal cut averages only three turns. A com-plete cut combines turns that jointly intersect every sufficient set. Across the 132 settings of the two sweeps, residual recovery tracks the number k of evidence turns removed almost exactly,

while system, model, and strategy together shift it by at most four points. The fitted line extrapolates to zero near k = 8 .2, nearly all the evidence of an average fact. For the evaluated deleters, removing more evidence strongly predicts lower residual re-covery, linking their performance to the evidence their pools reach. Individual facts may need far fewer deletions when a complete cut is reached and selected. Annotations were fixed before system evalua-tion and validated independently of retrieval and deletion results. The pool and budget sweeps then distinguish reach and selection limitations using these fixed annotations. 

6.2 Retrieval unit and derivation 

The five memories of §4.3 retrieve different units and store different derived content, which may shape candidate coverage and both recovery met-rics. 

Granularity. Turn RAG and Session RAG hold the same text and derive nothing, allowing a com-parison under their respective retrieval settings. Both yield 96–99% residual recovery after S0, but Session RAG hides far more, with a hidden-risk share of 71–82% against 39–42%. Its three retrieved sessions hold only 1.9 evidence turns, against 4.8 in Turn RAG’s sixteen turns. Session RAG’s cleanup also removes only 33–45 points of residual recovery against 51–56, because whole-session candidates are mostly not evidence. Under these settings, facts are harder both to see and to delete with session retrieval. 

Derivation. Stronger derivation does not consis-tently correspond to higher residual recovery across the evaluated systems. Although LightMem has the strongest derivation, its residual recovery resem-bles Turn RAG’s (39–43% against 41–46%). Mem-oryBank has the lightest derivation and the highest residual recovery (72–76%), alongside lower can-didate coverage (3.0 evidence turns against 4.5 in Turn RAG and LightMem). 6.3 Derivation types 

To break every annotated derivation, a deleter must reach and select a complete cut. The four types have similar amounts of evidence but differ in how that evidence relates to the request. Relation chains have the smallest annotated cuts (2.7 turns); never-theless, their evidence may be difficult to retrieve because it names the request’s people and firms without using its words. Small cuts therefore need not yield low residual recovery under these base-lines. Figure 3c shows that relation chains retain the most residual recovery after cleanup on average. Recovery drops 24 points from S0 to S2, and a Turn RAG pool of sixty holds 4.4 of their evidence turns against 6.9 for aggregation. Figure 3d shows hidden-risk shares of 81–94% across systems, plau-sibly because their links span topics while derived objects combine turns within topics. Aggregation loses 58 points to cleanup: more of its evidence en-ters the pool, allowing deletions to break its deriva-tions. Under these baselines, evidence linked by entities rather than wording is associated with lower candidate coverage and higher residual recovery. 

7 Case Study 

Figure 4 shows LLM-guided cleanup of a relation-chain fact in Turn RAG (Appendix D). The user asks the assistant to forget that their company hired a firm the company’s CFO’s wife owns. The fact rests on four links, (i) hiring, (ii) ownership, (iii) re-lationship, and (iv) identity, and it is forgotten once both evidence turns of any one link are deleted. The evidence shares the request’s entities but little of its wording, ranking 3–141 by similarity. The pool of 20 holds only two evidence turns from different links. S2 deletes both with budget to spare, but neither link is cut, so the fact remains derivable (§6.1). With a pool of 30, E2 becomes available, but S2 leaves it unselected even though deleting it alongside E1 would cut the hiring link. At 50, S2 deletes E5 and E6, breaking the relationship link. A wider pool enables a complete cut, but S2 must still select all its turns. Every evidence turn is one hop from a request entity, so following entities could reach a complete cut. 

8 Conclusion 

We introduce M EM CUT , a benchmark of 456 hand-designed secrets with annotated evidence trails, for evaluating forgetting in long-term agent memory. Its metrics distinguish native recovery from recov-ery by the same reader given surviving evidence. Across five memory designs, four models, and three reference baselines, disclosure deletion reduces na-tive recovery far more than residual recovery. LLM-guided deletion of ten further turns from twenty retrieval candidates leaves 39–76% residual recov-ery, with higher reported hidden-risk shares than before deletion. Subset sweeps identify candidate coverage as the main bottleneck in the evaluated reference baselines. 8Limitations 

Controlled evaluation. MEM CUT evaluates for-getting for secrets with specified evidence trails across four derivation types. Controlled construc-tion supports comparisons of candidate coverage, deletion choices, and residual recovery under fixed annotations. The episodes model recurring work, family, and social activities. Deployment studies can establish how often these trails occur and how forgetting performance changes with naturally oc-curring evidence structures. 

Reference baselines. Our reference baselines reuse existing retrieval and write components through a common deletion procedure. The sweeps examine candidate coverage and selection within this setting. These results establish reference per-formance for this reuse strategy. M EM CUT also supports evaluating dedicated erasure methods that expand candidate coverage or change memory in-dexing. 

Scope. We study text memory. Multimodal and parametric memory remain outside our scope. The source-level measure does not capture unrelated content removed through closure or downstream utility loss. 

Ethics Statement 

MEM CUT contains no real personal data; all peo-ple, organizations, and events are fictional. De-scribing how deleted secrets can be reconstructed could be misused. However, the reconstruction re-lies only on ordinary retrieval and reading of stored memory, and we believe that measuring this risk is a prerequisite for reducing it. The annotation audit was carried out by two graduate students in NLP who had no part in building the benchmark; each worked about 25 hours and was told in advance that all content is fictional. The benchmark will be released for research use. 

Use of AI Assistants 

We used generative models to phrase author-designed dialogue and polish the manuscript. The research questions, the design of the benchmark and the experiments, the analyses, and every figure, table, and number were produced by the authors.

A Dataset Details 

This appendix describes what M EM CUT contains. Table 5 gives per-fact statistics overall and by derivation type, and Table 6 gives per-episode statistics. 

Derivation types. The four types abstract the most typical ways separate records entail a propo-sition. Fact assembly joins attributes of one entity: a code name, a site visit, and an address make an acquisition target. Relation chains join entities: a contract names a firm, a filing names its owner, a dinner names the owner’s spouse, and a staff list names the spouse’s role. Aggregation combines values: several payments sum to a debt, several vis-its make a pattern. Temporal and state reasoning 

orders records in time: an offer, a revision, and a withdrawal fix what holds now. Longer derivations often compose them. 

Scale and balance. The facts are balanced across types, and every episode is long. The 456 protected facts split evenly over the four derivation types, 113–115 each (Table 5), and every type appears in every episode six to nine times. The fifteen episodes that carry them hold 43,402 turns in 490 sessions (1.74M tokens); each averages 2,893 turns, 116k tokens, and 33 sessions over 27–86 days (Ta-ble 6). 

Derivation structure. Every fact can be derived in many ways, but never from a single evidence turn. A fact has 8.1 evidence turns on average (6– 10; 3,700 in all), which the authors grouped into 7,148 minimal sufficient sets, 15.7 per fact (4–38), each of two to five turns (mean 2.7). Breaking all of them takes two deletions for 25.9% of facts, three for 45.4%, four for 28.1%, and five for 0.7%; the minimal cuts were computed by enumeration and agree with an independent dynamic program on all 456 facts. Each fact also has 26.6 distractors (16– 88; 12,092 distinct turns). No evidence turn serves two facts, five turns are evidence for one fact and a distractor for another, and 62.6% of turns carry no annotation. 

Dispersion. Evidence is spread across the episode and mostly precedes the disclosure. Afact’s evidence spans 8.0 sessions (5–10), with a median first-to-last span of 2,259 turns and 29 days, and lies a median of 714 turns and eight sessions from the disclosure. Only 67 of the 3,700 evidence turns (1.8%) share the disclosure’s session, and 14.3% of facts have at least one such turn. Disclo-sures fall in the last third of the episode for 61% of facts. Time is annotated at the granularity of session dates.

B.2 Retrieval unit and derivation strength Retrieval unit. Session retrieval reads more and finds less. At the evaluated retrieval depths, a Ses-sion RAG context averages 13.3k tokens and holds 1.9 evidence turns, a Turn RAG context 1.1k to-kens and 4.8. After the disclosure is deleted, the two stores’ residual recovery agrees to within about two points in 15 of the 16 model–type cells of Ta-ble 3, while Session RAG’s native recovery is 31– 43 points lower and its hidden-risk share is 71–82% against 39–42%. After cleanup Session RAG also leaves 10–23 points more recoverable, because its candidate pools are diluted by non-evidence turns from the same sessions. 

Derivation strength. Retrieval units help explain native recovery. In MemoryBank it is one turn, so once the disclosure is gone each evidence turn has to match the question on its own: deleting the disclosure costs 46–50 points of native recovery, and after cleanup four in five of the 72–76% of facts that remain recoverable are unseen by the system’s own retrieval. In Nemori it is a narrative or a distilled fact that fuses several turns, and in LightMem a consolidated entry that fuses more; such objects can still match the question without the disclosure, consistent with the smaller losses of 29–36 and 12–20 points. What a system retains follows instead how much evidence its deletion pool reaches (§B.3): MemoryBank’s pool of twenty holds 3.0 of a fact’s evidence turns against 4.5 in Turn RAG and LightMem, and cleanup lowers its residual recovery by only 18–23 points, against 33– 38 in Nemori and 47–52 in LightMem. Closure deletion also removes a whole object when any of its source turns goes; what it costs in unrelated memory is a separate question, which we leave to future work.

Judgment helps least where reach fails most. 

Replacing retrieval ranking with LLM judgment lowers residual recovery by 14 points for fact as-sembly and aggregation, 11 for temporal facts, and 8 for relation chains (Table 12). The smaller gain for relation chains is consistent with limited can-didate coverage: judgment can select only turns already in the pool. 

Coarse retrieval costs aggregation most. On the same text, Session RAG answers fewer ques-tions than Turn RAG after S0 for every type, but by different margins: about 54 points for aggregation, 37 to 38 for fact assembly and temporal facts, and 17 for relation chains (Table 3). An aggregate needs several values at once, and three retrieved sessions rarely contain all of them; relation chains lose least because turn retrieval already fails to surface them. 

Derivation reveals every type but relation chains. 

For fact assembly, aggregation, and temporal facts, the hidden-risk share after S2 falls from 68–85% in Turn RAG and MemoryBank to 20–39% in Nemori and LightMem (Figure 3d), consistent with derived objects combining evidence within topics. Relation chains stay 81–94% hidden in every system, likely because their links span topics (§6.2). 

Aggregation also loses what deletion did not tar-get. Aggregation is the only type whose residual recovery falls when only the disclosure is deleted: 15 points on average against at most 3 for the others, and 4 to 13 points even in Turn RAG, which de-rives nothing. Co-location does not explain it, since temporal facts share a session with their disclosure as often, in a fifth of facts (Table 5), and lose about one point. Two effects do. First, an aggregate has to be computed from several values, and the dis-closure states the result, so once the disclosure is gone the reader has to carry out the computation itself, which it does less reliably than it reads a stated fact. The evidence still suffices by design. The reader recovers 96–99% of facts from evidence 16 alone in the validation of §3.3, with misses concen-trated in aggregation, consistent with arithmetic errors. Second, in derived systems a summary that folds the disclosure together with other values goes with it under closure. Part of aggregation’s small residue after cleanup is therefore collateral rather than evidence the deleter found, although cleanup also deletes more aggregation evidence than any other type (Table 10). LightMem also loses aggre-gate values at write time, with 92% residual recov-ery before deletion, the lowest of any type–system pair. In two cells native recovery of aggregation slightly exceeds residual recovery, which may miss aggregated values in objects without an annotated evidence source. The hidden-risk share remains a lower bound on the share of residual successes missed by native recovery. 

D Case Study Details 

This appendix gives the full record of the fact in Figure 4: its evidence and cuts, where similarity to the request ranks each evidence turn, what S2 deletes at each pool size, and how entity expansion could reach a complete cut. 

The fact and its cuts. The fact is that Arden Ad-visory, the firm the user’s company hired, belongs to Nina Shah, the wife of its CFO, Ravi Shah. It rests on four links, each with two evidence turns: 

hiring , the Q3 contract with Arden (E1) and a note that Ravi, the CFO, hired Arden (E2); ownership ,Arden’s filing listing Nina Shah as sole owner (E3) and Nina’s engagement letter as Arden’s owner (E4); relationship , the offsite dinner Ravi and his wife Nina host (E5) and a table booked for their wedding anniversary (E6); identity , the same note (E2) and a staff line naming Ravi Shah as CFO (E7). Recovery needs one evidence turn from every link, so the minimal cuts are {E1, E2 }, {E3, E4 },

{E5, E6 }, and {E2, E7 }, two turns each. 

Similarity scatters the evidence. For the request “Forget that the firm we hired belongs to our CFO’s wife”, the evidence turns rank 3 (E1), 14 (E3), 22 (E2), 36 (E5), 45 (E6), 53 (E4), and 141 (E7), so the main-table pool of 20 holds only E1 and E3, one evidence turn from each of two links. They share entities with the request but almost no wording, while records above and between them share its words without supporting the fact: a booking at the Arden Hotel, an old vendor list, an advisory budget, an advisory call, a note about a Nina in HR, an address on Arden Street. The disclosure itself ranks seventh; S0 deletes it, and it is excluded from every pool. 

S2 completes a cut only at fifty. With a budget of ten, S2 deletes E1 from a pool of 10 and E1 and E3 from a pool of 20; the pool holds at most one evidence turn per link, S2 deletes what it holds, the budget never binds, and the fact stays derivable. From a pool of 30, E2 is in the pool and never selected, although with E1 it would complete the hiring cut: it reads as a routine record of a hire and says nothing about ownership. At 40, E5 joins the deleted turns. At 50, E6 enters the pool and is deleted; the relationship link has lost both evidence turns, and the fact is no longer derivable. At 60, E4 is deleted as well, emptying the ownership link too. E7 enters no pool we ran. In miniature, this is why the budget curve of Figure 3b is flat while the pool curve of Figure 3a falls. 

Following entities could reach a complete cut. 

Every evidence turn is one hop from an entity the request refers to: Ravi, the CFO, links to E2, E5, E6, and E7; Arden, the firm, to E1, E2, E3, and E4; Nina, the wife, to E3, E4, E5, and E6. In this example, entity expansion could place a complete cut within a ten-turn pool; its effectiveness as a deletion strategy remains untested.
