Abstract 

DeepSearch agents achieve strong performance on complex information-seeking tasks through iterative search and rea-soning. However, they often issue intent-misaligned, under-constrained, or redundant search queries, resulting in poor evidence coverage and accumulating retrieval noise. To mit-igate these limitations, we propose QREF , a plug-and-play 

Query Refiner and Evidence Filter. Specifically, the Query Refiner rewrites search queries according to the current rea-soning intent while ensuring complementary coverage across each search batch. The Evidence Filter retains evidence rel-evant to both the current search batch and the reasoning ob-jective, reducing distracting retrieval results. We initialize the Query Refiner with supervised fine-tuning and optimize it through reinforcement learning based on interactions with a real-world web search environment, reducing ineffective queries. Comprehensive experiments demonstrate that our approach improves the performance of DeepSearch agents across four challenging benchmarks, achieving average rel-ative Pass@1 improvements of 33.55% on WebSailor and 11.04% on TongyiDeepResearch without modifying architec-tures or retraining their reasoning policies. Compared with the original DeepSearch backbone without QREF, the RL-trained refiner reduces the fraction of query batches that lack discriminative retrieval anchors by 95%. Compared with the SFT-trained refiner, it also lowers the filter-based ineffective query ratio. 

## 1 Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated remark-able capabilities in mathematical reasoning (Guo et al. 2025), code generation (Li et al. 2022), and logical reasoning (Wei et al. 2022). However, hallucinations and unreliable paramet-ric knowledge can still cause factual errors on knowledge-intensive or domain-specific tasks (Wang et al. 2024). To overcome these limitations, DeepSearch agents interleave reasoning with iterative web search to progressively gather external evidence for complex knowledge-intensive tasks. Existing methods have improved search performance through

However, the quality of intermediate search queries, which strongly influences retrieval effectiveness at each search step, has received much less attention. During search, the agent must translate its evolving reasoning state into one or more executable queries. These queries serve as the interface between reasoning and retrieval, yet their qual-ity is typically optimized only implicitly as part of the overall agent policy. To characterize query failures in DeepSearch agents, we analyze 300 trajectories generated by WebSailor-32B (Li et al. 2025) and identify three recurring failure patterns in intermediate search queries: Missing Discriminative Con-straints , Low Query Specificity , and Redundant Query Diversification . Missing Discriminative Constraints and Low Query Specificity reflect failures of individual queries to adequately capture the retrieval intent implied by the current reasoning state, whereas Redundant Query Diversification reflects insufficient complementary coverage of retrieval in-tents across queries within the same search batch. Analysis of 1,992 query batches shows that each of the three failure pat-terns occurs in more than 20% of batches, while 47.04% of batches contain at least one failure. Moreover, unsuccessful trajectories accumulate nearly three times as many ineffective queries as successful ones on average, with the gap emerg-ing as early as the first search round. These findings identify intermediate query quality as a critical factor for effective retrieval and multi-hop reasoning. Recent methods have begun to improve intermediate search behavior through process supervision or retrieval-grounded reinforcement learning (Tao et al. 2025a; Zheng et al. 2025a; Wen, Dong, and Dou 2026). Nevertheless, two limitations remain. First, query optimization is gener-ally learned within the search agent’s reasoning policy or trajectory-generation process, which limits its reuse with already-trained agents. Second, existing methods typically assess or rewrite queries individually, whereas a DeepSearch step may issue a query batch whose collective coverage mat-ters. In this work, we present QREF , a plug-and-play Query

Specifi-cally, QREF consists of two components. Before retrieval, the Query Refiner rewrites intermediate queries into self-contained, specific, and search-effective formulations while preserving the agent’s reasoning intent and ensuring com-plementary coverage across the search batch. After retrieval, the Evidence Filter retains evidence relevant to both the current query and the reasoning objective, filtering irrele-vant, redundant, or overly general search results to reduce retrieval noise. Our module can be integrated into existing DeepSearch agents without modifying their architectures or retraining their reasoning policies. We first initialize the Query Refiner by supervised fine-tuning (SFT) from Qwen3-8B (Yang et al. 2025). We then further optimize it with Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) (Schulman et al. 2017) using an entity-level retrieval reward computed by comparing the retrieval outcomes of refined and original queries in real-world web search. Com-pared with the SFT model, reinforcement learning further im-proves Pass@1 across seven of eight backbone–benchmark settings and reduces the ineffective-query ratio across all backbone–benchmark settings. In summary, our main contributions of this work are as follows: 

• We analyze 300 WebSailor-32B trajectories, identifying three recurring failure categories in intermediate queries that each occur in over 20% of query batches and are strongly associated with lower final task accuracy. 

• We propose QREF, a plug-and-play Query Refiner and Evidence Filter, enabling constraint-preserving, retrieval-oriented query reformulation and evidence filtering with-out modifying or retraining the underlying agent. 

• We develop a two-stage training strategy for the Query Re-finer, combining supervised fine-tuning with PPO under a fine-grained entity-level retrieval reward that directly optimizes evidence recovery in real-world web search. 

• Experiments on four benchmarks and two backbone agents demonstrate consistent improvements in retrieval quality and downstream reasoning, while reducing inef-fective and redundant queries. 

## 2 Related Works 

DeepSearch Agents The paradigm of retrieval-augmented language models has evolved from static, single-turn RAG (Lewis et al. 2020) toward autonomous DeepSearch agents that interleave long-horizon planning with iterative interaction with external search engines (Zheng et al. 2025b; Gao et al. 2025). To strengthen the reasoning–retrieval loop, one line of work leverages reinforcement learning to learn search policies directly from real-world retrieval environ-ments (Zheng et al. 2025b) and to jointly optimize reasoning and search behaviors (Jin et al. 2025; Chen et al. 2026). A second line scales training to support longer and more ef-fective search trajectories through improved data curation and rollout stability (Team et al. 2025a; Gao et al. 2025; Li et al. 2025; Wu et al. 2026a), while another line aug-ments agents with structured knowledge (Lu et al. 2025), planning and memory mechanisms (Tao et al. 2025b), or hi-erarchical retrieval interfaces (Du et al. 2026). Despite these advances, existing methods primarily pursue agent-level op-timization of reasoning, planning, or search policies, leaving intermediate query generation tightly coupled with the back-bone agent and difficult to reuse across already-trained sys-tems. Our work instead decouples query optimization from the backbone by introducing a backbone-agnostic, plug-and-play refinement module that can be seamlessly integrated into existing DeepSearch agents. 

Query Refinement Early query refinement addressed vo-cabulary mismatch through linguistically motivated expan-sion (Selvaretnam and Belkhatir 2016) and conversational ambiguity through semantic de-contextualization (Jiang et al. 2025b). With the advent of LLMs, research shifted toward semantic-level rewriting, where the Rewrite-Retrieve-Read framework established explicit rewriting criteria and trained rewriting policies with reinforcement learning (Ye et al. ## 3 Diagnosing Ineffective Queries 

3.1 Analysis Setup 

To better understand the prevalence, structure, and down-stream impact of ineffective intermediate queries, we conduct a focused analysis on 300 randomly sampled WebSailor-32B trajectories from BrowseComp (Wei et al. 2025). These tra-jectories contain 1,992 search batches and 7,545 individual queries. The category criteria are as follows: 

Cat1. Missing Discriminative Constraints The query batch fails to preserve one or more discriminative con-straints already identified in the agent’s current reasoning. 

Cat2. Low Query Specificity The query batch lacks suf-ficiently specific retrieval anchors, causing the search to remain overly broad even if it is semantically relevant. 

Cat3. Redundant Query Diversification Multiple queries in the batch repeatedly explore the same information need with only superficial wording differences, rather than cov-ering complementary search directions. Each search batch is labeled with three non-exclusive fail-ure categories, so multiple failure types may co-occur in the same batch. GPT-4.1 serves as an LLM judge and evalu-ates the formulation quality of each batch conditioned on the original question and the agent’s current reasoning trace. Im-portantly, the judge assesses the search strategy itself rather than whether the returned results already contain the correct answer. On a 100-batch subset annotated by three human raters, inter-annotator agreement is substantial for all three categories, with Fleiss’ κ of 0.786, 0.667, and 0.713 for Cat1–Cat3, respectively. GPT-4.1 also agrees well with the human majority vote, achieving κ/F1 of 0.705/0.839 on Cat1, 0.900/0.950 on Cat2, and 0.770/0.909 on Cat3. 

3.2 Failures Are Prevalent 

We first examine how frequently ineffective query batches occur in WebSailor trajectories. As shown in Figure 2a, all three failure categories occur in more than 20% of query batches. Overall, 47.04% of query batches contain at least one failure, and 86.3% of complete trajectories contain at least one ineffective batch. These results show that ineffective intermediate queries are not isolated errors, but a recurring failure pattern in Web-Sailor. Even a strong search agent frequently fails to convert intermediate reasoning into effective retrieval actions. 

3.3 Failure Categories Co-occur 

We then investigate whether different failure modes occur independently. Based on the binary annotations of the 1,992 query batches, we compute pairwise Pearson correlation co-efficients among the three categories. As illustrated in the supplementary material, all pairwise correlations are positive and statistically significant (all p < 

0.001 ), suggesting that these failure behaviors often co-occur rather than arise independently. In particular, Low Query Specificity and Redundant Query Diversification exhibit the strongest correlation ( r = 0 .537 ). This pattern suggests that batches with weak retrieval anchors are also more likely to repeat nearly identical search directions instead of covering complementary ones. 

3.4 Failures Are Associated with Lower Final Accuracy 

To quantify the association between ineffective queries and downstream reasoning, we compare the final correctness of trajectories with and without query failures using a χ2 test. The underlying counts are reported in the supplementary material. The difference is highly significant ( χ2 = 15 .997 , p < 

0.001 ): trajectories with no ineffective query batches are correct on 26.8% of examples (11/41), whereas trajecto-ries with at least one ineffective batch are correct on only 6.2% (16/259). This result indicates that intermediate query quality is strongly associated with end-to-end reasoning per-formance, rather than merely with retrieval efficiency. 

3.5 Failures Accumulate Over Search Rounds 

We further analyze how ineffective query batches accumulate during multi-step search. Figure 2b compares cumulative query failures in trajectories that ultimately succeed or fail. Although both groups exhibit approximately linear growth as search progresses, the two curves diverge from the first search round and continue separating throughout the trajec-tory. By the end of the search process, unsuccessful trajecto-ries accumulate roughly three times more ineffective batches per trajectory than successful ones. This observation suggests that ineffective queries rarely occur in isolation. Instead, they accumulate over successive search iterations, progressively increasing retrieval noise, re-ducing evidence quality, and compounding downstream rea-soning errors. 

## 4 Methodology 

4.1 QREF 

QREF consists of two components: the Query Refiner and the Evidence Filter. The overall framework is illustrated in Figure 1. 

Query Refiner The Query Refiner is an LLM-based rewriting module that takes the original question, the cur-rent reasoning trace, and the candidate query batch parsed from the agent’s <search> action. It follows four princi-ples. First, self-containment : each rewritten query should be interpretable without omitted context or pronouns. Second, 

atomic decomposition : multi-faceted information needs are split into smaller, retrieval-friendly sub-queries. Third, no answer leakage : the refiner must not inject external knowl-edge or hidden answers beyond the current reasoning state. Fourth, search-engine alignment : operators such as exact-match quotes, wildcards, negation, and source restrictions (e.g., site: , filetype: ) are used only when they im-prove retrieval precision. Beyond rewriting vague queries, the refiner removes redundant candidates and preserves al-ready effective ones, yielding a query batch that is cleaner and more diverse than the original candidate set. 

Evidence Filter The Evidence Filter is applied after the search engine returns snippets for each refined query. For ev-ery result list Draw

The filter re-tains evidence that helps identify entities, verify facts, narrow candidates, or advance the current reasoning step, while dis-carding results that are off-topic, overly general, redundant, or misaligned with the current query intent. Figure 3 illustrates this pipeline on a representative BrowseComp question: the Query Refiner introduces the discriminative constraints missing from the agent’s origi-nal queries, enabling the retrieval of the target evidence, while the Evidence Filter subsequently removes distracting off-topic pages so that the correct entity can be identified.

4.4 Reinforcement Learning 

Although supervised fine-tuning enables the model to imitate high-quality query refinement behaviors, it primarily learns the surface patterns of rewriting. It does not receive fine-grained rewards from complex and noisy web-search inter-actions, nor does it explicitly optimize refinement strategies for real-world retrieval or downstream retrieval effectiveness. Therefore, we further optimize the query refinement policy through reinforcement learning on the InfoSeek dataset (Xia et al. 2025) using PPO. Training on eight NVIDIA H20 GPUs takes approximately five hours and uses a total of about 100,000 queries. At each search step, the query refinement model receives the original question, the current reasoning context, and the candidate query batch as input, and generates a refined query batch ˜Q that is subsequently issued to the search engine. The retrieval outcomes of ˜Q are compared against those of the original queries to compute a retrieval-grounded reward, en-couraging the model to produce refined queries that recover more of the required evidence than the agent’s own queries.

## 5 Experiments 

5.1 Evaluation Benchmarks 

We evaluate our method on four challenging information-seeking benchmarks, including: (1) BrowseComp (1,266 questions) (Wei et al. 2025), an English web browsing benchmark requiring deep multi-hop web exploration; (2) 

BrowseComp-ZH (Zhou et al. 2025)(289 questions), the Chinese counterpart of BrowseComp requiring multi-hop exploration over Chinese web resources; (3) GAIA (117 questions, text-only subset) (Mialon et al. 2024), a bench-mark evaluating multi-step reasoning and tool-assisted prob-lem solving; and (4) HLE (2,158 questions, text-only sub-set) (Phan et al. 2025) a benchmark covering diverse knowledge-intensive reasoning tasks. 

5.2 Backbone Agents 

We evaluate the proposed module on two DeepSearch back-bones: WebSailor-32B and TongyiDeepResearch (Team et al. 2025b). Both agents are served with SGLang and operate in a multi-turn ReAct-style loop that alternates between reason-ing, tool use, and answer generation. 

5.3 Auxiliary Models 

The Query Refinement Module is instantiated with four re-finer configurations: (1) +Qwen3-8B , a prompt-based refiner using a locally deployed open-source LLM; (2) +GPT-5.2 ,a prompt-based refiner using a proprietary API model; (3) 

+SFT , a refiner initialized through supervised fine-tuning; and (4) +RL , a refiner further optimized from +SFT via rein-forcement learning. We use Qwen3-8B as the Evidence Filter. For final-answer evaluation, we follow the backbone models’ evaluation pipeline and use benchmark-specific prompts. 

5.4 Implementation Details 

Web Search Tools The agent interacts with the web through two tools: a search tool powered by the Serper API 1 for query execution and a visit tool powered by the Jina Reader API 2

for webpage retrieval and parsing. 

Inference Setup The backbone agents use a temperature of 0.6, top-p of 0.95, and a maximum context length of 31K tokens. To reduce contamination risk, we apply a rule-based URL blocklist that excludes known benchmark-hosting websites (e.g., huggingface ) during retrieval. 

5.5 Main Results 

Table 1 reports Pass@1 results. Both the trained Query Re-finers (SFT and RL) and GPT-5.2 as the Query Refiner consistently improve performance over the vanilla agents across both backbones. On WebSailor, +RL achieves the best performance among trained refiners, improving over the baseline by 6.79, 6.23, 5.99, and 3.11 points on BrowseC-omp, BrowseComp-ZH, GAIA, and HLE, respectively. Simi-lar gains are observed on TongyiDeepResearch, demonstrat-ing the generality of the proposed module across different DeepSearch agents. Comparing +SFT and +RL , reinforcement learning out-performs supervised fine-tuning on both backbones, sug-gesting that retrieval-based rewards provide a more effective optimization signal for query refinement. ## 6 Conclusion 

In this work, we propose QREF , a plug-and-play Query Re-finer and Evidence Filter. The Query Refiner rewrites inter-mediate search queries into self-contained, constraint-aware, and complementary formulations before retrieval, and the Evidence Filter retains only evidence relevant to both the refined query and the reasoning objective. Furthermore, we devise a two-stage training strategy that couples supervised fine-tuning with reinforcement learning under an entity-level retrieval reward in a real-world web search environment. Our method consistently improves retrieval and reasoning on four benchmarks with two backbones, delivering improved retrieval quality and downstream reasoning while substan-tially reducing the proportion of ineffective queries. This work highlights intermediate query quality as a key interface between reasoning and retrieval in DeepSearch agents.

## A Case Study 

Figure 3 presents a representative example illustrating how our two modules progressively improve retrieval quality and downstream reasoning. Given the question “Ahead of a title match, a defender who played for one of the teams... What club was this de-fender an assistant coach of in 2018”, the vanilla DeepSearch agent first generates several vague intermediate queries such as "assistant coach defender blog club website" and "de-fender account online blog assistant coach". These queries omit critical constraints (e.g., the title match, the defender’s identity, and the associated event), causing the search engine to retrieve numerous irrelevant pages centered around Bristol City. Since the target evidence never appears in the retrieved documents, the agent incorrectly infers that Bristol City is the answer. After applying the Query Refiner, the intermediate queries become substantially more search-oriented. The refiner de-composes the information need into multiple complementary queries while explicitly preserving important constraints, producing queries such as “"defender" blog "assistant coach" 2018" and “"ahead of" "title match" defender blog”. As a re-sult, the target page discussing Djimi Traoré is successfully retrieved. However, the retrieved evidence still contains many irrelevant pages because the search results include documents matching only partial query intents. Consequently, although the correct evidence is present, the reasoning process can still be distracted by redundant information and arrive at the same incorrect conclusion. Finally, the Evidence Filter removes documents that are inconsistent with the current reasoning objective while pre-serving evidence directly supporting the intermediate query. The remaining context is dominated by pages describing Djimi Traoré, allowing the reasoning process to correctly es-tablish the connection between the defender and his role as an assistant coach in 2018, ultimately producing the correct answer. This example demonstrates that the two modules address complementary bottlenecks in DeepSearch agents. Query Refinement primarily improves target evidence recall by generating more discriminative and search-effective queries, whereas Evidence Filtering further improves the signal-to-noise ratio of the retrieved context, enabling the downstream reasoning module to utilize the recalled evidence more reli-ably. Only when both modules are combined can the agent consistently retrieve the correct evidence and reason over it successfully.

We summarize the main training hyperparameters used for supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and reinforcement learning (RL). C.1 Supervised Fine-Tuning 

We first perform full-parameter SFT on the Hunyuan back-bone. The model is trained for one epoch with a maximum sequence length of 20,480 tokens using bfloat16 precision. We use a learning rate of 1×10 −5 with a cosine schedule and a warmup ratio of 0.1. DeepSpeed ZeRO-3 with parameter and optimizer offloading is used to reduce memory con-sumption. FlashAttention-2, the Liger kernel, and gradient checkpointing are enabled for efficient long-context training. Table 3 summarizes the main settings. 

C.2 Reinforcement Learning

Advantages are estimated with generalized advantage estimation (GAE), and rewards are computed by a task-specific reward function.
