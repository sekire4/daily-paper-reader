ABSTRACT Strong multi-hop retrieval should be practical to deploy without expensive corpus preparation or com-plex database infrastructure. We present SAG2, an embedding-free retrieval architecture built around a single SQLite database, BM25, and contextual evidence decisions from JEV. SAG2 follows the query-time association principle of SAG (SQL-Retrieval Augmented Generation), while replacing its event– entity and dense indexes with a lexical index over original passages. Selected passages supply terms for further retrieval, and newly retrieved evidence guides subsequent decisions. This moves semantic evidence selection to query time, eliminating offline embedding computation, LLM-based event–entity extraction, and dedicated database servers. The resulting system combines lightweight preparation with strong retrieval accuracy: on 1,000-question subsets of MuSiQue, 2WikiMultiHopQA, and HotpotQA, SAG2 achieves the highest Recall@5 and Recall@10 among the compared methods, with Recall@10 of 86.62%, 97.43%, and 99.00%. It also reaches 81.10% document Recall@10 over the 511,962-document EnterpriseRAG-Bench corpus. Indexing all 11,656 MuSiQue passages takes 2.36 seconds, produces a 25.87 MiB database, and requires no model calls. SAG2 demonstrates that accurate multi-hop evidence retrieval can be supported by compact, rapidly prepared infrastructure, making it a practical foundation for knowledge retrieval applications. 1

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Useful knowledge retrieval requires both accurate evidence and infrastructure that is easy to prepare and operate. Or-ganizational knowledge is distributed across documents, messages, and other text sources, and a single question may require facts from several passages. A passage naming a person’s birthplace can directly match a question, while a passage identifying the county containing that place becomes useful only after the birthplace is known. Independent similarity rankings can miss this intermediate connection. Multi-hop retrieval must therefore discover associations among passages as evidence accumulates (Trivedi et al., 2022). The practical challenge is to recover these connections without imposing an expensive preparation pipeline on every collection. Existing systems distribute this work between corpus preparation and query execution. Dense retrieval precomputes passage representations to support semantic matching (Karpukhin et al., 2020). Structure-augmented methods addi-tionally extract facts or construct connections among documents. SAG (SQL-Retrieval Augmented Generation) stores event–entity records, activates associations through shared entities at query time, and combines this structural path with dense retrieval and LLM selection (Wu et al., 2026). This design preserves original passages as evidence and avoids a global knowledge graph, but still requires semantic extraction and embedding computation before retrieval. A specialized decision model makes a different allocation of work possible. JEV accepts natural-language context and returns a yes-probability for each decision through its Noul interface (Almeida, 2026; TypeSafe AI, 2026). It evaluates candidate evidence directly, without generating intermediate reasoning text. A lexical index can then sup-ply inexpensive candidate access, while contextual model decisions determine which passages should guide further retrieval. This motivates our central question: can multi-hop retrieval retain strong accuracy with a single lexical database and semantic evidence selection performed at query time?

1arXiv:2610.22588v1 [cs.CL] 15 Oct 2026 We propose SAG2 , a SQLite–BM25–JEV architecture built on SAG’s principle of query-specific passage association (Figure 1). SAG2 adopts a different indexed representation and retrieval procedure: it stores original passages and lexical occurrences, then forms associations online through passage-to-term-to-passage expansion. BM25 retrieves initial candidates; JEV selects useful evidence; selected passages supply terms for further retrieval. Later decisions consider both the original question and retrieved context. SAG2 thus carries forward SAG’s associative principle while removing its event–entity extraction and dense retrieval components. All returned evidence remains original text. Lexical feedback and neural reranking provide important foundations for this design. Relevance models expand queries from initial results (Lavrenko and Croft, 2001); BERT-QE uses neural judgments to select feedback text (Zheng et al., 2020); and GAR discovers candidates through a precomputed document graph (MacAvaney et al., 2022). SAG2 combines online lexical association with a shared evidence state: contextual scores choose expansion passages and weight their terms, while the new passages update the context for subsequent decisions. This interaction allows evidence to become relevant through an intermediate connection even when its direct match to the original question is weak. The local retrieval infrastructure consists of one SQLite database; semantic decisions use a hosted JEV endpoint. Cor-pus preparation requires only local text processing and index construction, with no vector database, separate database server, or offline model inference. On MuSiQue, the complete index is ready in 2.36 seconds and occupies 25.87 MiB. This simplicity does not require weaker benchmark retrieval: SAG2 obtains the highest R@5 and R@10 among the compared systems on all three multi-hop benchmarks, and reaches 81.10% document R@10 on EnterpriseRAG-Bench. Controlled MuSiQue comparisons further show that contextual expansion recovers more evidence than enlarging a one-shot candidate pool. Together, these results motivate SAG2 as a readily deployable retrieval architecture with low preparation cost and strong evidence recovery. Our contributions are threefold: 1. We introduce an embedding-free multi-hop retrieval architecture whose local infrastructure is a single SQLite database, eliminating offline model inference and dedicated database servers. 2. We specify a passage-to-term-to-passage retrieval procedure in which contextual evidence scores guide expansion and newly retrieved passages inform subsequent decisions. 3. We demonstrate strong retrieval accuracy on three multi-hop benchmarks and the full EnterpriseRAG-Bench corpus, and quantify the contributions of expansion and context alongside indexing time, storage, and model cost. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Candidate generation and reranking. BM25 obtains lexical candidates from an inverted index (Robertson and Zaragoza, 2009), while dense retrieval uses learned representations for semantic matching (Karpukhin et al., 2020). Neural reranking makes finer relevance decisions over the retrieved candidates (Nogueira and Cho, 2019). Its attain-able recall is bounded by that candidate pool. SAG2 uses evidence judgments to select expansion passages and thereby changes the candidates available to later rounds. 

Feedback and adaptive retrieval. Relevance models derive query terms from retrieved documents (Lavrenko and Croft, 2001). BERT-QE uses neural judgments to select feedback text for contextualized reranking (Zheng et al., 2020). GAR expands the reranking pool through a precomputed corpus graph, showing that ranking and candidate discovery can interact (MacAvaney et al., 2022). SAG2 shares this adaptive principle. Its specific design obtains new passages through online term selection and lexical matching, and evaluates candidates in shared retrieved context. Our experiments use explicit RM3 and GAR-BM25 adaptations, whose configurations are described in Appendix B. 

Structured and multi-step retrieval. SAG indexes events and entities, activates shared-entity associations, and combines them with dense retrieval and LLM selection (Wu et al., 2026). IRCoT interleaves generated reasoning with retrieval (Trivedi et al., 2023). SAG2 adopts a different persistent representation and online process: passages and lexical statistics form the index; original-passage evidence decisions guide each expansion. It requires neither generated intermediate retrieval text nor a precomputed document-neighbor graph. Its relationship to SAG is architectural: both activate query-specific passage associations, while SAG2 uses lexical occurrences for candidate access and contextual JEV scores to control expansion.

3.1 Passage representation and lexical indexing

3.4 Lexical expansion and candidate retention 

For every selected term 𝑡 , SAG2 retrieves a separate list of unseen passages that match 𝑡 . Within that list, BM25 scores the OR expression containing all selected expansion terms and the original-query terms. Matching the expansion term is required; matching an original-query term is not. The distinction allows a new passage to enter through a bridge term while the original question continues to influence ranking. 4.3 Implementation and statistical analysis 

The implementation uses Python and SQLite FTS5. JEV is accessed through OpenRouter using its Noul interface; the returned model is typesafe/jev-1.13-20260917. Question and HTTP concurrency are both capped at twenty. A passage judgment is one candidate evaluated under a particular question and context. Rescoring a retained passage counts as another judgment; an HTTP request may contain many judgments. We report judgment counts, input and output tokens, and the corresponding model charges separately. Charges are computed from successful responses, including responses reused to resume an interrupted run. They exclude machine rental and answer generation. For MuSiQue comparisons, we report paired 95% percentile bootstrap intervals from 2,000 question resamples with seed 20261015. Each resample preserves question identities across methods and recomputes the mean difference. These intervals quantify variability across the evaluated questions; they do not quantify variation across repeated model runs. 

## 5 RESULTS AND ANALYSIS 

5.1 Retrieval across benchmarks 

SAG2 achieves the highest R@5 and R@10 among the compared systems on all three benchmarks. Its R@10 is 86.62% on MuSiQue, 97.43% on 2WikiMultiHopQA, and 99.00% on HotpotQA, improving on SAG by 3.25, 3.86, and 1.30 percentage points. SAG retains higher R@1 and R@2 on MuSiQue, showing that the advantage is stronger when several passages are available to the reader. Relative to BM25, SAG2 improves R@10 by 32.21, 25.93, and 12.10 points on the three datasets. On MuSiQue, complete-evidence coverage increases from 17.40% to 69.50%. The results show that a lexical index, combined with contextual online expansion, can support strong multi-hop evidence recovery without persistent semantic representa-tions.

5.5 Discussion and limitations Practical deployment. SAG2 combines a small local index, rapid corpus preparation, and strong evidence recall. It removes the need to operate a vector database, a separate relational database server, or local model inference. The application maintains a SQLite index and calls JEV for evidence decisions. This reduces deployment dependencies and avoids corpus-wide model computation before retrieval can begin. The resulting accuracy and preparation cost make the architecture useful for applications that can access a hosted decision service. 

Dependence on the JEV service. The principal deployment limitation is the decision model. At the time of this study, the detailed architecture, parameter count, and weights of the evaluated JEV release were not publicly available. Public access is through a hosted API (TypeSafe AI, 2026). Consequently, the present system cannot be independently deployed entirely on premises using publicly released model artifacts, even though its retrieval index is local. It depends on the provider for model availability, throughput, and continued access to the evaluated release; questions and candi-date text must also be sent to that service. This limits deployment where external processing is prohibited. A locally deployable decision model is therefore an important direction, but its accuracy and resource requirements remain to be evaluated. 

Scope of the evidence. The experiments establish the performance of the complete SQLite–BM25–JEV system and the contribution of expansion and shared context. They do not isolate every term-weight factor or establish that JEV is superior to other decision models. The SAG comparison uses different models and implementations, and the GAR-BM25 results apply to the stated BM25-based adaptation. Evaluation uses fixed development subsets whose supporting and distractor passages form the retrieval corpora. MuSiQue was also used during method development. Generalization to an unused split, private organizational corpora, and downstream answer generation remains to be evaluated. EnterpriseRAG-Bench broadens the evaluated scale and source types, but its corpus is synthetic. Lexical access can still miss paraphrases, aliases, and evidence without a useful 10 term connection. The low complete-evidence coverage on four-hop questions identifies this as an important remaining challenge. The cost study covers one corpus; scaling behavior and incremental updates require further measurements. 

Additional semantic associations. A useful next step is to combine SAG2 with the LLM-based entity extraction principle in SAG. Optional ontology-enriched preprocessing could identify entities, aliases, semantic types, and re-lations, linking them to their source passages. These additional connections could support retrieval over fragmented text with little surface-term overlap. JEV would continue to select evidence in context. This extension would add preprocessing cost and extraction errors, so its value should be assessed against the present lightweight architecture. 

## 6 CONCLUSION 

SAG2 combines lightweight retrieval infrastructure with strong multi-hop accuracy. A single SQLite database provides lexical candidate access, while contextual JEV decisions guide passage-to-term-to-passage expansion at query time. This removes offline embeddings and model-based event–entity extraction. SAG2 achieves the highest R@5 and R@10 among the compared methods on three multi-hop benchmarks and retrieves evidence from more than half a million EnterpriseRAG-Bench documents. The measured indexing time, storage footprint, and model cost support its practical value: accurate evidence retrieval with a compact local index and a short preparation step. Reducing dependence on hosted JEV is the main remaining deployment requirement.
