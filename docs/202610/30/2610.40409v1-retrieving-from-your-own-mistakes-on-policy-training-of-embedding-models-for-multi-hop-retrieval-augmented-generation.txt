Abstract 

Complex questions in retrieval-augmented generation require ev-idence spread over several documents, and an answer is reliable only when the retriever finds all of it. Chain embedding retriev-ers collect such evidence step by step, re-encoding the question together with the passages retrieved so far, and we find that they are already strong: trained only on gold histories, a small chain embedding retriever that calls no language model outperforms graph-based systems and is competitive with a search agent. This training, however, leaves a gap. At test time the retriever condi-tions on its own retrievals, which soon contain mistakes: by the third step, about half or more of the states it visits hold a wrong passage, and these passages lower its chance of retrieving a missing gold passage well beyond what the difficulty of the states explains. We propose on-policy state training, which trains the encoder on the states it reaches itself. At every epoch the current encoder retrieves chains for the training questions; each state it visits, in-cluding those built on its own mistakes, is labeled with the earliest gold passage still missing while the other missing gold passages are masked, so that no correct continuation is penalized; and the encoder is updated contrastively on these states mixed with teacher-forced ones, so that its training distribution follows it as it learns. The resulting retriever retrieves complete evidence more often than teacher-forced training and the systems we compare, and it transfers without further training to harder long-chain variants of HotpotQA and 2WikiMultiHopQA that we construct. On the same states, it recovers from its own mistakes far more often, and controlled comparisons attribute the gain to training on the states the encoder currently reaches. Code and benchmarks are available at https://github.com/yausankit/on-policy-state-training.

1 Introduction 

Retrieval-augmented generation grounds a language model in re-trieved documents [13], and on the Web it lets search engines and

assistants answer questions from large collections of pages that change faster than any model can be retrained. For complex ques-tions the evidence is spread over several passages, and a missing passage is often enough to make the answer wrong [21]. Two fami-lies of systems address this setting. One places a language model in the retrieval loop, either to reason between searches [10] or to organize the corpus as a graph in advance [5]. The other trains an embedding model to retrieve in a chain: at each step it encodes the question together with the passages found so far and retrieves the next one [23]. Chain embedding retrievers need no language model call and a single dense index, which makes them a practical first stage for Web-scale corpora, and a better one also helps systems of the first family. A chain retriever is trained on one kind of history and used on another. During training, the passages in its history are gold passages, and the target is the next gold passage [23]. At test time the history consists of whatever the retriever itself retrieved. As soon as one of these passages is wrong, it stays in the query for every later step, and the retriever faces a state it was never trained on. Such states are common. On four-hop MuSiQue questions, 70% of the states that a teacher-forced chain retriever visits at its third step, and 89% at its fourth, already contain a non-gold passage (Section 3). In such states the retriever retrieves a missing gold passage 28% of the time, against 67% when its history is clean. Part of this gap reflects harder states, but deleting the wrong passages from the same states shows that they alone cost the retriever 11 points (Section 5.4). We call this the state mismatch. Chain dense retrievers trained with supervised targets use teacher forcing, and those that learn from their own trajectories rely on outcome rewards [20]. Work on dense retrieval has improved the candidate side of training, through harder or cleaner negatives [16, 22], while the query-side histories remain gold. This raises two questions. Does the state mismatch limit chain dense retrieval, so that changing only the states used for training improves it? And if so, does the improvement come from the states the mismatch points to, the histories that contain the retriever’s own mistakes? To answer them we propose on-policy state training, which trains the encoder on the states that it reaches itself. Each visited state is labeled with the earliest gold passage that is still missing, while the other missing gold passages are masked so that retrieving any of them is never penalized; this target is defined by the gold set alone, so states with wrong passages receive the same dense supervision as gold states. Because the visited states are re-collected as the encoder changes, the training distribution follows the retriever as it learns. Everything else, from the architecture and the loss to the negatives and the document index, is shared with teacher-forced training, which isolates the distribution of training states. It also differs from methods that learn retrieval from outcome rewards [10, 20], which need sparse rewards over whole episodes. We evaluate on MuSiQue and, to test whether the effect persists on longer chains and larger corpora, on harder variants of HotpotQA and 2WikiMultiHopQA that we construct [7, 24]. Our study leads to four findings. (1) A trained chain embedding retriever is already a strong retriever for complex questions. Even with teacher forcing, a 0.6B chain retriever that calls no language model retrieves com-plete evidence more often than graph-based retrieval-augmented generation and is competitive with a 7B search agent, at a fraction of their retrieval cost. (2) The state mismatch still limits it. Changing only the training states raises complete coverage over teacher forcing with the same encoder on MuSiQue and on both long-chain benchmarks, the resulting retriever outperforms every system we compare on MuSiQue, and the MuSiQue-trained encoder transfers to longer chains. (3) The gain appears where the mismatch predicts. Scored on the same states, teacher-forced and on-policy encoders continue a chain from a clean history about equally well, while after one or more mistakes the on-policy encoder recovers far more often, so the gain accumulates along the chain. (4) Exposure to contaminated histories explains part of the gain, and following the current encoder explains the rest. Synthetic contamination of gold histories, matched in the number of wrong passages, closes about half of the gap to on-policy state training. Visited states collected once are worth little more than synthetic contamination, and the remaining gain comes from refreshing them as the encoder changes; resampling synthetic contamination every epoch does not reproduce it (Section 5.5). 

2 Related Work 2.1 Retrieval for Complex Questions 

Questions that require several pieces of evidence are usually an-swered by retrieving in multiple rounds. One line of work places a large language model in the loop and learns when and what to search through reinforcement learning [10]. Graph-based methods organize the corpus offline instead, as a knowledge graph traversed with personalized PageRank [5], a subgraph selected with a prize-collecting Steiner tree [6], or a relation-free entity graph [28]. Our method calls no language model at retrieval time and uses only a dense passage index. The two directions are complementary, since a stronger embedding model can serve as the retriever inside ei-ther kind of system. An embedding retriever can also be trained from the outcome of a whole episode: Q-RAG learns a value-based embedder for multi-step retrieval from temporal-difference targets [20]. Search-R1 and Q-RAG learn from sparse outcome rewards. Our method derives a dense per-step target from the gold evidence set, so every visited state is labeled without reward estimation, and neither training nor retrieval calls a language model. 

2.2 Chain and Dense Retrievers 

Chain dense retrievers condition each hop on the passages retrieved at earlier hops. MDR concatenates the question with the previously retrieved passages and encodes the result with a shared dense encoder, trained with teacher forcing: the history at hop 𝑡 consists of gold passages, the target is the next gold passage, and negatives come from a memory bank of passage vectors [23]. Its training queries therefore never contain a passage the retriever retrieved by mistake, and no target is defined for a history that does. We keep the MDR-style chain architecture and a fixed document index, and change only the distribution of histories on which the query encoder is trained. Dense retrievers are trained contrastively on question–passage pairs [11], and much of the progress comes from better negatives. ANCE re-encodes the corpus with the current checkpoint so that negatives follow the model’s own retrieval distribution [22], and RocketQA removes false negatives with a cross-encoder [16]. Con-triever and recent embedding models built on large language models scale up pretraining [9, 12, 27]. These methods improve the can-didate side of the contrastive objective. Our method acts on the query side: it refreshes the retrieval states that the encoder embeds as queries, while the negatives stay those of the frozen encoder. 

2.3 Exposure Bias and On-Policy Training 

Training a sequential predictor only on reference histories, while it conditions on its own predictions at test time, is known as exposure bias [17]. Imitation learning and sequence modeling address it by training on states the model itself visits [1, 3, 19]. DAgger rolls in with the learner and labels the visited states with an expert, and dy-namic oracles define such an expert for every state of a dependency parser, including states off the gold path [4]; AggreVaTe and LOLS label states with costs to go instead [2, 18]. The idea has recently been applied to language model agents [14, 15]. Our training pro-cedure is an instance of DAgger with a dynamic oracle. What we study is specific to chain dense retrieval: the oracle is free because the gold evidence set labels every state, a wrong passage persists in the query for all later steps, and only the query side of a contrastive objective changes. A related line makes language models robust to irrelevant retrieved context by training with distractors [25, 26]; Section 5.5 compares visited states with synthetic contamination of this kind.

3.4 How Large Is the Mismatch? 

We measure it with a chain retriever trained by teacher forcing on MuSiQue (gold histories drawn as random subsets), run for 𝑇 = 4steps on the 1,000 test questions. We count only feasible states: those at step 𝑡 ≤ 𝑛 of an 𝑛 -hop question that still miss a gold passage, since for 𝑡 > 𝑛 a missing gold passage implies a wrong one. On four-hop Qiu et al. 

questions, 19% of the states at the second step, 70% at the third and 89% at the fourth already contain a non-gold passage; on three-hop questions the share is 12% at the second step and 48% at the third (Figure 3a). The retriever retrieves a missing gold passage in 67% of the clean states and in 28% of the contaminated ones. The gap is not explained by question length: it is 71% against 33% on two-hop questions, 67% against 40% on three-hop questions and 57% against 19% on four-hop questions. Part of this gap reflects harder states. A counterfactual separates the two: deleting the wrong passages from the same contaminated states raises the success rate of the teacher-forced encoder from 20.8% to 32.1%, so its own mistakes cost it about a third of its remaining chance of continuing the chain. Teacher forcing trains the encoder only on clean states, while a large share of the states it meets after the second step are of the other kind. 

4 On-Policy State Training 

To study the effect of the state mismatch, we change a single factor of teacher-forced training: the distribution of the states on which the query encoder is trained. The architecture, the target 𝑎 ∗ (𝑠 ), the loss in Eq. (4), the negative candidates and the document index all stay as they are, so any change in retrieval can be attributed to which states the encoder sees during training. The procedure follows DAgger [19] with a dynamic oracle [4]: the learner rolls in, and an oracle defined on every state, including states off the gold path, provides the label (Figure 1b). In chain dense retrieval this oracle comes from the gold evidence set at no cost, and the design choices below adapt the procedure to a contrastive query encoder over a fixed index.

4.2 Labels for Visited States 

Visited states can be labeled at no extra cost because 𝑎 ∗ (𝑠 ) is defined by the gold set alone. When the history contains a wrong passage, the target is still the earliest gold passage not yet retrieved, and the other missing gold passages are removed from the denominator of Eq. (4), so the encoder is trained to continue the chain from a contaminated history. Every retained visited state receives a dense, per-step target. No language model, reward model or outcome re-ward is involved, which distinguishes this setting from retrievers trained with reinforcement learning [10, 20]. Because the target function is shared with teacher forcing, the two training regimes differ only in their state distributions. The rule fixes which missing gold passage serves as the positive, but since the other missing gold passages are masked, retrieving any of them is never penalized; on questions whose chain order is partly arbitrary, such as compar-isons, the ordering only selects which correct continuation receives the gradient. 

4.3 Fixed Index and Negative Rule 

Only the query encoder is updated, through low-rank adapters [8], and the document embeddings ℎ(𝑑 ) are computed once. The nega-tives 𝑁 (𝑠 ) for every state are drawn from the top 100 passages that the frozen encoder retrieves for the state text 𝑥 (𝑠 ), excluding every gold passage of the question, whether the state is teacher-forced or visited. Non-gold passages already in a visited history can there-fore appear among its negatives. We fix both for the comparison. If the corpus were re-encoded, or the negatives were mined with the current model as in ANCE [22], a gain could come from harder negatives instead of from the states. With both held fixed, the nega-tives of every state are mined by the same rule and the same model in both training regimes. The pools still depend on the state text, so a visited state receives other negatives than a gold state of the same question; the two regimes share the rule that chooses them. 

5 Experiments 

We answer four research questions. RQ1 : Does on-policy state training retrieve complete evidence chains more often than exist-ing retrievers, and does it transfer to longer chains? RQ2 : Which components of the method contribute? RQ3 : Does the gain come from recovering after the retriever’s own mistakes? RQ4 : What in the visited states matters? 

5.1 Experimental Setup 

5.1.1 Datasets. MuSiQue [21] is built so that its two- to four-hop questions cannot be answered from a single passage, which makes the history of a chain retriever matter at every step. All trained re-trievers are trained on 1,800 MuSiQue training questions and select checkpoints on 200 others. We test on the 1,000 questions of the official development set with the 11,656-passage corpus used by Hip-poRAG 2 [5]. To test whether the effect persists on longer chains and larger corpora, we evaluate the MuSiQue-trained encoders, without further training, on two harder benchmarks that we construct from HotpotQA [24] and 2WikiMultiHopQA [7]. HotpotQA-Long ex-tends bridge questions along hyperlinks in the Wikipedia abstracts to chains of three, four and five pages (100 test questions each) over a corpus of 554,812 passages. 2Wiki-Long follows functional rela-tions between Wikidata entities for three to five hops, or compares three to four entities that are each reached in two hops, with 300 test questions over 220,000 passages. In both benchmarks the ques-tion names only the starting entity, a language model of a different family verifies that the question is answerable from the gold pas-sages and has a unique answer, and questions whose final passage is retrievable by BM25 from the question alone are removed. The corpora contain the lexical nearest neighbors of every gold passage, so distractors are topically close to the evidence (Appendix E).

5.2 RQ1: Retrieval Effectiveness 

5.2.1 Overall Results. Table 1 supports the first finding. On MuSiQue, on-policy state training reaches 73.5 R@5 and 49.3 C@5. Search-R1, which reasons with a 7B language model between three searches on average, completes 36.5% of the questions within five passages, Q-RAG 36.2%, the released MDR checkpoint 31.9%, and HippoRAG 2, which calls a language model to build its graph, 23.1%. Most of the gain over the frozen encoder comes from training a chain retriever at all; the part due to the training states is isolated in Section 5.3, where teacher forcing with the same encoder and recipe reaches 44.6 C@5 and on-policy state training 50.5. Teacher forcing alone thus already completes more chains on MuSiQue than every com-pared system (44.6 against 36.5 C@5 for Search-R1), and on the long-chain benchmarks it is far ahead of the graph-based systems and close to Search-R1 on HotpotQA-Long: a trained chain em-bedding retriever, which calls no language model at retrieval time, is a strong retriever for complex questions. The gain in complete coverage is larger than the gain in recall, which reflects what a chain needs: a question counts toward C@5 only when every step succeeds. 

5.2.2 Transfer to Longer Chains. MuSiQue has at most four hops, while the long-chain benchmarks need up to five hops, or six to eight passages for comparisons, over corpora 19 to 48 times larger. The MuSiQue-trained encoder raises C@10 over the frozen encoder from 1.0 to 29.6 on HotpotQA-Long and from 2.0 to 32.8 on 2Wiki-Long. Search-R1 is the strongest competitor here. It puts fewer gold passages in its first five (C@5 3.3 and 6.0, against 20.8 and 27.7) but reaches a similar recall by the tenth passage, and leads on 2Wiki-Long in C@10. Its policy was trained with reinforcement learning on HotpotQA questions, from which part of HotpotQA-Long is built, and it spends a 7B forward pass per search. Our retriever uses a 0.6B encoder and one nearest neighbor search per step. The released MDR checkpoint, trained on HotpotQA, retrieves the most gold passages in the top five on HotpotQA-Long (51.0 R@5), but with two hops it completes few of the three- to five-hop chains (14.7 C@10, against 29.6). 

5.2.3 Results by Question Type. Figure 2 splits the results of Table 1 by question type. On MuSiQue, the gain is largest on three-hop questions, where our retriever completes 41.6% of the chains within five passages and the next system 20.9%; on four-hop questions every retriever completes fewer than 7%. On the long-chain bench-marks, our retriever has the highest complete coverage on every type of HotpotQA-Long and on the comparison questions of 2Wiki-Long. On 2Wiki-Long, Search-R1 leads on the three-hop chains, the closest to the two-hop questions its policy was trained on, and on the four- and five-hop chains, with 45 and 12 questions, no retriever completes more than one chain in ten.

5.3 RQ2: Ablations 

Table 3 removes the components of the method in turn. Each row removes one more component, so the last row also has no visited states. Removing the refresh keeps the visited states collected at the start of training. Removing the visited states leaves teacher forcing on gold states alone. Removing the chain fine-tunes the same encoder on the question alone, which also removes the notion of a retrieval state. Every component contributes, and the contributions grow on the longer chains. Removing the visited states, which leaves teacher forcing with the same encoder, recipe and number of states, costs 5.9 points of C@5 on MuSiQue and 5.7 and 10.4 points of C@10 on HotpotQA-Long and 2Wiki-Long. Without a chain, fine-tuning on the question alone gives no gain over the frozen encoder (54.6 against 55.1 R@5 on MuSiQue), which confirms that complex retrieval needs the history. 5.4 RQ3: Recovery from Own Mistakes 

The diagnosis in Section 3 predicts where on-policy state training helps most: the states whose history already holds a wrong pas-sage. We test that prediction on MuSiQue with the teacher-forced, collected-once and refreshed retrievers of Table 4. Comparing each retriever on the states it visits itself would confound the effect of training with the states each one happens to reach, so the probes below score all retrievers on one fixed set of states. 

5.4.1 Recovery on Fixed States. We take the 3,403 states visited by the teacher-forced retriever that still miss a gold passage and let every encoder choose the next passage for them (Figure 3b). On its own states, the teacher-forced encoder reproduces 99.8% of its recorded choices, so the probe matches retrieval. If on-policy state training only made the encoder better at retrieval in general, the gain would appear in clean states as well. It barely does: with a clean history, the three encoders succeed in 67.0%, 67.2% and 69.0% of the states. After one wrong passage, the rate rises from 25.0% under teacher forcing to 35.8% under on-policy state training, and after two or more from 14.5% to 29.2%, twice as often. Visited states collected once, without refresh, recover most of the way (32.2% and 25.1%). Because all encoders see the same states, the difference lies in the encoders themselves. The same pattern holds on the 3,352 states visited by the on-policy retriever: 66.8% against 68.5% with a clean history, 26.1% against 33.5% after one wrong passage and 20.0% against 24.2% after two or more. 

5.4.2 Deleting the Wrong Passages. To measure the effect of the contamination itself, we delete the non-gold passages from the contaminated states above and keep the gold ones in order; the deleted passages stay excluded from retrieval, as in the original state. On these 1,358 states, the teacher-forced encoder succeeds in 20.8% of the original states and in 32.1% once the wrong passages are removed: its own mistakes cost it 11.3 points. The on-policy encoder succeeds in 33.1% and 35.3%, so the same contamination costs it 2.2 points. Part of the low success after a mistake reflects harder states, since even the cleaned states stay well below the 67% of states that were clean to begin with. The rest is the effect of the wrong passages in the query, and on-policy state training removes most of it: on the contaminated states, the on-policy encoder (33.1%) does slightly better than the teacher-forced encoder does once an oracle has deleted the wrong passages (32.1%). It is also better on the cleaned states themselves (35.3% against 32.1%), which are gold histories with gaps, so part of the gain is skill on histories that skip a hop. 

5.4.3 Gain along the Chain. Because the encoder recovers more often, the gain accumulates over steps. The rate of retrieving a gold passage changes by one to two points at the first two steps, where most histories are still clean, and rises from 27.7% to 38.7% at the third step and from 15.2% to 23.1% at the fourth. By question length, C@5 rises from 61.6 to 67.8 on two-hop questions and from 36.7 to 44.3 on three-hop questions. On four-hop questions recall rises from 44.1 to 48.3 R@5, but complete coverage within five passages stays below 10% for both retrievers, since it requires four consecutive successful steps.

5.5 RQ4: What in the Visited States Matters 

5.5.1 Synthetic, Fixed and Refreshed States. A cheaper explanation is that any contaminated history would do, as in training language models with distractor passages [25, 26]. Table 4 separates the candidate explanations. Every row keeps half of the training states as gold states and changes the other half, under the same budget and recipe. Synthetic contamination inserts passages from the frozen encoder’s top 100 at random positions into gold histories, with the joint distribution of the number of wrong and gold passages matched to the visited states in aggregate (not per question or per epoch), and needs no roll-in; each synthetic state starts from a teacher-forced state with the drawn number of gold passages. It is either built once or resampled at every epoch. The visited half is either sampled once from a single roll-in of the teacher-forced encoder and kept for all epochs (the w/o refresh condition of Table 3), resampled at every epoch from that same roll-in, or refreshed with a new roll-in at every epoch. 6 Conclusion 

Chain embedding retrievers are trained on gold histories and run on their own retrievals. We showed that this gap is large in practice: by the third step, about half or more of the states a trained chain retriever visits contain one of its own mistakes, and these mistakes cost a teacher-forced encoder far more than the difficulty of the step explains. Training on the states the encoder visits, labeled with the earliest missing gold passage, changes this without a new architecture, objective or language model. The gain appears where the mismatch predicts, in recovery from contaminated histories, it depends on refreshing the visited states as the encoder learns, and it carries over to chains longer than those seen in training. These results have three implications within the setting we study. First, the distribution of training states is a design choice for chain retrievers in addition to negatives and targets, and reporting how often test-time states leave that distribution would make results eas-ier to interpret. Second, retrievers inside agents and reinforcement learning pipelines face the same mismatch, and dense supervision on visited states is a cheap complement to outcome rewards. Third, a trained chain embedding retriever, which needs no language model at retrieval time, is a strong baseline for graph-based and agentic retrieval for complex questions.

A Training Details A.1 Encoder 

All retrievers start from Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B [27]. Queries are the serialized states 𝑥 (𝑠 ) with the model’s retrieval instruction, and passages are encoded without an instruction. Both use last-token pooling and ℓ2 normalization. Document embeddings are computed once with the frozen model in half precision. The query encoder is adapted with LoRA [8] of rank 16 ( 𝛼 = 32, dropout 0.05) on the query, key, value and output projections of every attention layer. 

A.2 States and Serialization 

Of the 2,000 MuSiQue training questions, 200 are used for check-point selection. For a training question with 𝑛 gold passages, teacher forcing builds 𝑛 states; each draws a size 𝑘 uniformly from {0, . . . , 𝑛 −

1}, takes a random subset of 𝑘 gold passages as the history in chain order, and targets the earliest gold passage not in it. Chain order follows the question decomposition of MuSiQue. This gives about 4,700 training states (4,700 for teacher forcing and 4,691 for on-policy state training, whose two halves are rounded), the budget of every regime. On-policy state training keeps a fixed random half of them (about 2,350 states) and fills the other half by sampling uniformly from the deduplicated visited states, so questions whose chains stay incomplete for more steps contribute more visited states. The state text 𝑥 (𝑠 ) lists the retrieval instruction, the question and the passages of the history in retrieval order, each as its index, title and text, or marks an empty history. Passages are not shortened, and states stay far below the encoder’s limit of 32,768 tokens, so no input is truncated. Algorithm 1 summarizes the procedure. 

A.3 Optimization 

Each batch contains 16 states. Every state contributes its target and 15 negatives sampled from its frozen top-100 pool, and all 256 candidates of the batch serve as negatives for every state, with the state’s own missing gold passages masked. We use 𝜏 = 0.05, AdamW with learning rate 3 × 10 −4 and no weight decay, and a linear warmup over the first 5% of steps followed by cosine decay. Retrieval runs for 𝑇 = 4 steps during training and on MuSiQue, and for 𝑇 = 8 steps on the long-chain benchmarks. Training lasts at most 10 epochs, and the checkpoint is selected by chain C@5 on the 200 validation questions with a patience of 3 epochs. In the first epoch the visited states come from a roll-in of the teacher-forced encoder; from the second epoch on they are collected by the current encoder before any update of that epoch. E Long-Chain Benchmarks E.1 Chains 

2Wiki-Long uses the evidence triples of 2WikiMultiHopQA as a graph over Wikidata entities, keeping only functional relations so that every hop has a single answer. Chains are paths of three to five hops in which every hop is supported by the paragraph of its subject entity, no entity repeats, and degenerate relation pairs (for example, the spouse of a mother) are excluded. Comparison questions map three or four entities through one relation to a person and then to a date, and ask which entity’s related person has the earliest or latest date. HotpotQA-Long starts from the two pages of an original bridge question and follows hyperlinks in the 2017 Wikipedia abstracts to reach three, four or five pages. Hops go only to named-entity pages with in-degree at most 300, through sentences with fewer than five links. 

E.2 Questions and Filters 

A language model (deepseek-flash) writes one question per chain. The question may name only the starting entity, or the compared entities, and uses every relation in order. A question is kept only if it names no intermediate entity or answer, is answered correctly from the shuffled gold passages, is judged to have a unique an-swer, and is rated 4 or 5 for naturalness, all judged by the same model. A model of a different family (gpt-5.4-mini) then re-checks answerability and uniqueness; 62% of the 2Wiki-Long pool and 98% of the HotpotQA-Long pool pass. Questions whose final gold passage (for comparisons, all gold passages) appears in the top 10 of question-only BM25 over the final corpus are removed. Genealogy questions are capped at 40% of 2Wiki-Long, and question types are sampled evenly. HotpotQA-Long has 100 test questions each of three, four and five hops; 2Wiki-Long has 170, 45 and 12 chains of three, four and five hops and 73 comparison questions. The prompts are released with the benchmarks. Without passages, the reader of Section 5.2 answers 14.3% of the HotpotQA-Long and 14.7% of the 2Wiki-Long test questions, so the benchmarks require retrieval. Table 7 shows one test question of four types. 

E.3 Corpora 

Each corpus contains every gold passage of the candidate pool, then the BM25 nearest neighbors of each gold passage, taken round-robin over gold passages, then random passages from the source collection. HotpotQA-Long has 554,812 passages, of which 328,648 are nearest neighbors; 2Wiki-Long has 220,000 passages, of which 95,880 are nearest neighbors. A development split of 100 questions per benchmark is built with the same construction. Test questions come from both original splits of the source datasets, which is possible because the benchmarks are used only for evaluation.

F.3 Beam Decoding 

Beam decoding is a cheaper way to recover from an early mistake, so we decode both matched encoders with beams of two and four. Each beam is a partial chain scored by the sum of its similarities; at every step each beam expands its top candidates, the best partial chains are kept, and the passages of the best final chain are ranked first, followed by the maximum score of every passage over all encoded states. A beam of one is greedy decoding. Table 10 shows that beams improve teacher forcing by two to three points of complete coverage but leave a gap of four to six points, and that the greedy on-policy retriever outperforms the teacher-forced one with a beam of four, which encodes three times as many queries. Both encoders are those of Table 3; the re-encoded index moves greedy C@10 on HotpotQA-Long by one question.
