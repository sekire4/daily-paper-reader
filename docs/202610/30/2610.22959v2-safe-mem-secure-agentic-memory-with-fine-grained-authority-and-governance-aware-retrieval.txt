## SAFE-Mem: Secure Agentic Memory with Fine-Grained Authority and Governance-Aware Retrieval

Abstract 

Large language model (LLM) agents increas-ingly rely on persistent memory to support long-term reasoning and personalized interac-tions. However, information collected from external sources can introduce security risks when untrusted claims (i.e., atomic statements) are later treated as authorized preferences, policies, or permissions. Existing defenses typically rely on content-based filtering or coarse-grained provenance tracking, which ei-ther fail to prevent unauthorized memory use or block legitimate information, sacrificing mem-ory utility. To address these limitations, we propose SAFE-Mem, a fine-grained memory governance framework that improves security while preserving legitimate memory use. First, SAFE-Mem annotates each claim with three governance labels (i.e., kind, authority, and scope) to capture its provenance and permit-ted use. Second, governance-aware retrieval matches query requirements with claim per-missions to block unauthorized influence while retaining legitimate information. Third, a governance-enabled memory graph connects source sentences, claims, and entities to sup-port provenance tracking and efficient retrieval. Across four LLM backbones on MPBench and LoCoMo, SAFE-Mem attains the low-est attack success among defenses that retain legitimate memory, at least 77% below the strongest of them, and the highest answer ac-curacy of all evaluated defenses, improving it by up to 15%. Memory systems have ad-vanced accordingly, with a growing emphasis on accurate recall over increasingly long histories (Ma-harana et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2025). This openness also makes memory an attack sur-face that persists beyond the session in which it is written. Content an agent merely reads can be stored, retrieved for an unrelated request weeks later, and used to shape answers and actions (Dash et al., 2026; Dong et al., 2025; Gao et al., 2026). The threat is misattributed authority. A third-party page stating that a product has been approved is accurate as a report of that page, and becomes harm-ful once the agent promotes the claim into its prin-cipal’s own policy or preference. This is authority laundering (Zhan et al., 2026): a memory acquires authority its source never held. The same loss of origin can cause errors without an adversary, as when a statement about one person is later treated as a fact about another. However, existing defenses typically rely on arXiv:2610.22959v2 [cs.CR] 20 Oct 2026 content-based filtering or coarse-grained prove-nance tracking, which either fail to prevent unau-thorized memory use or block legitimate infor-mation, sacrificing memory utility (Figure 1). Content-based defenses examine what an entry says, through consensus among retrieved memo-ries (Wei et al., 2026), classifiers and sanitizers (Kumar et al., 2024), or the isolation of injected instructions (Li et al., 2025), yet a laundered claim is consistent and free of instructions, and para-phrased copies across multiple pages can make it the majority. Provenance-based defenses track where an entry came from, but decide per entry or per action (Ouyang and Hou, 2026; Xu et al., 2026; Louck, 2026), and information-flow control for agents treats memory as trusted or leaves it out of scope (Debenedetti et al., 2026; Costa et al., 2025). Gating only actions leaves answers exposed to untrusted memory, while refusing an entry for its source blocks its legitimate uses as well, and withholding whole records can cost up to 40 points of authorized use (Cerruti et al., 2026). Neither family distinguishes the uses of a single claim, and in our evaluation the defenses that block the most attacks also lose the most legitimate memory. To address these limitations, we propose SAFE-Mem, a fine-grained memory governance frame-work that improves security while preserving legit-imate memory use. Whether a claim may be used depends on its source and its use. First, SAFE-Mem labels each claim with a kind, an author-ity and a scope. The kind states what the claim binds, the authority is the strongest kind its source may establish, and the scope states whom that authority covers. Second, governance-aware re-trieval admits a claim only when its kind and au-thority meet the kind a request requires, within its scope. An untrusted claim thus serves every use its source supports and none above it. Third, a governance-enabled memory graph links source sentences, claims and entities, so that each claim stays traceable to its source and its original wording stays retrievable. Across four backbones, SAFE-Mem attains the lowest attack success on MPBench among defenses that retain legitimate memory, at least 77% below the strongest of them, and the highest recall and LoCoMo accuracy of all evaluated defenses, im-proving LoCoMo accuracy by up to 15 points; and on EHRAgent under MINJA it prevents every at-tack from executing. Our contributions are: • Fine-grained governance labels. Each claim carries a kind, an authority and a scope. Authority derives from the channel through which a source sentence arrived and rises on a principal’s explicit request to keep something, up to the kind that request names. • Governance-aware retrieval. SAFE-Mem admits a claim only when its kind and author-ity meet the kind a request requires within its scope, filtering before ranking and repeating the check at tool calls. • A memory graph supporting governance and retrieval. A unified graph connects sen-tences, claims and entities. It links each claim to its source sentences and relevant entities, bounds its authority by that of its weakest source, groups claims about the same subject for conflict detection, and retrieves passages in the principal’s and other parties’ own words as evidence. 

2 Related Work 

Agentic memory. Memory lets an agent carry context across sessions (Packer et al., 2023; Park et al., 2023; Zhong et al., 2024). Current sys-tems structure what they store: A-Mem links Zettelkasten-style notes (Xu et al., 2025), Mem0 ex-tracts and updates salient facts, with a graph variant over entity–relation triplets (Chhikara et al., 2025), Zep keeps facts in a temporal knowledge graph in-dexed to their episodes (Rasmussen et al., 2025), and other systems route queries over several graphs (Jiang et al., 2026a) or compress memory for fast search (Li et al., 2026). Surveys organize these systems (Zhang et al., 2025; Du et al., 2025; Jiang et al., 2026b), and LoCoMo and LongMemEval measure their recall (Maharana et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2025). These systems store and retrieve by relevance, recency and consistency. SAFE-Mem also records on every claim what it may be used for. 

Attacks on agent memory. Instructions hidden in retrieved data steer LLM applications and tool-using agents (Greshake et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024; Debenedetti et al., 2024; Zhan et al., 2024). Poi-soning a knowledge base or an agent’s memory makes the effect persist, through crafted texts and optimized triggers (Zou et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2024) or ordinary queries (Dong et al., 2025), and memory-poisoning benchmarks find that injected memories are acted on in later sessions (Dash et al., 2026; Gao et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026). Lower-authority memory treated as authoritative is a fail-ure class of its own (Chen et al., 2026), which concurrent work names authority laundering (Zhan et al., 2026). 

Defending agent memory. Content-level de-fenses judge what a memory says, through consen-sus among retrieved memories (Wei et al., 2026), classifiers and sanitizers (Kumar et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024), isolation of injected instructions (Li et al., 2025) or marking of untrusted text (Hines et al., 2024a), and certifiably robust RAG aggre-gates answers over isolated passages (Xiang et al., 2024). Role-based defenses train the model to follow only the instruction channel (Chen et al., 2025a,b; Hines et al., 2024b), and a laundered mem-ory carries no instruction. Provenance-based de-fenses gate actions on lineage (Ouyang and Hou, 2026; Xu et al., 2026; Cerruti et al., 2026; Louck, 2026), and information-flow control keeps un-trusted data out of an agent’s planning (Debenedetti et al., 2026; Costa et al., 2025). SAFE-Mem ex-tends use-dependent admission to answers, with graded kinds and party scope (Table 4 in Ap-pendix B). 

3 Threat Model 

We consider an LLM agent that serves one or more principals across sessions. While working, the agent reads external artifacts such as web pages, emails, documents and tool outputs, and distills what it reads into persistent memory. In later ses-sions, it retrieves entries relevant to a request and conditions its answer or action on them. Authority over a principal originates only in that principal’s own assertions, instructions and authorizations. The agent runtime records the channel through which each input arrives, such as a principal’s turn or an attached artifact, and these records are trusted. The adversary controls the content of external artifacts the agent reads. When several principals share one memory, another principal may act as the adversary; its own turns then carry authority over itself and over no other principal. It cannot alter another principal’s turns, write to the memory store directly, forge channel records, or access the prompts or weights of any component. Within an artifact, it may write arbitrary text, including state-ments styled as the principal’s own words, claims of prior approval, and paraphrases of one payload spread across many artifacts, planted long before the session they target. The attack requires neither the principal’s endorsement nor any review of the resulting memory. The adversary’s goal is to make a memory carry authority its source never held. An attack suc-ceeds when a later answer or action relies on such a memory as a preference, decision, trust assignment, policy or permission that no entitled principal ex-pressed; retaining or reporting what the artifact said does not count. The defender knows the channel of each input but not which artifacts are malicious. Its goal is to deny every use of a memory that its source cannot support while keeping every use that it can. 

4 Methodology 

SAFE-Mem attaches governance to every stored claim (Figure 2). Governance labels record what a claim binds and what its source may establish (§4.1), a memory graph links each claim to its source input and entities (§4.2), and governance-aware retrieval admits claims for the uses their la-bels support (§4.3). Authority, scope and use derive from runtime channel records and the principal’s own words, admission is checked before ranking, and external claims stay in memory for every use their sources support. Appendix D follows an ex-ample through the design. 

4.1 Governance Labels 

SAFE-Mem describes every claim c by three gov-ernance labels: a kind κ(c), an authority α(c) and a scope s(c).The kind specifies what a claim does when the agent adopts it, following Searle’s classification of illocutionary acts (Searle, 1976). A description reports what a source asserted and binds nobody. An attribute commits its speaker to a fact about a subject and binds answers about that subject. Pref-erences and rules function as directives but differ in reach: a preference guides choices made for a principal, while a rule governs every occasion that satisfies its condition. A permission functions as a declaration, changing what the agent may do or whom it may rely on by being issued. 4.2 Memory Graph 

SAFE-Mem stores memory in a graph of sentence, claim and entity nodes. A sentence node is created for every sentence of every input, whether a princi-pal’s turn, an external artifact or a turn by another party to a conversation. It preserves the sentence in its original wording together with its provenance, namely the channel it arrived through and, in a con-versation, its speaker, and carries no governance label of its own. Consecutive sentences of a docu-ment, and consecutive turns of a conversation, are connected by NEXT edges, so that every input re-mains an ordered timeline. A claim node holds one statement that the agent’s LLM extracts from an input, and an input may yield several claims. Besides its text, a claim node records the subject, relation and object of the statement and its kind, au-thority and scope (§4.1). An entity node stands for a party or object that claims concern. Entity nodes are shared across inputs, so that claims about the same subject extracted from different documents and sessions meet at the same node. Like graphs for multi-document reasoning (Li and Ouyang, 2026), it keeps each document as an ordered chain be-side its claims, and adds governance labels to the claims. The graph ties each claim’s authority to the con-text in which it was extracted. A DERIVED -FROM 

edge connects the claim to its source input. A claim extracted solely from the principal’s words inherits the principal’s authority. If any lower-authority in-put was present during extraction, the claim inherits the lowest authority present. Raising that authority requires an ENDORSED -BY edge to a principal’s explicit request for retention. When the retained claim is stated directly in the principal’s request, both edges point to the same sentence. When the request refers to an artifact, the claim remains de-rived from the artifact, while the endorsement links it to the principal’s request. This is the only edge that raises authority. Claims are also connected to their subject and object entities through ABOUT edges. These links bring claims about the same subject and relation together for comparison. The authority inheritance rule also applies to the agent’s own outputs, which return to memory as claims derived from the con-text that produced them. An answer generated with a description in view is itself stored as a descrip-tion. Retrieval returns claims alongside short passages from the principal’s words and those of other con-versational parties. A passage carries no kind of its own: it serves at the lowest authority among its sentences and is admitted by Eq. 2 at that level, and only the principal’s requests to keep something and the turns of other parties in a conversation form pas-sages. External artifacts enter the context through their derived claims.

The second leaves reporting open to all content, while any stronger use also requires the entry’s authority to cover the party the request concerns. An entry withheld from one request stays in memory and serves any request that asks for a use it reaches. Algorithm 1 in Appendix A gives the procedure. SAFE-Mem filters before ranking: only admissi-ble claims are candidates, and the k most similar to the request are retrieved. Among the retrieved claims, those with the same subject and relation are checked for contradiction. If one position holds the highest authority alone, it stands and the others are marked contested; if positions of equal, highest au-thority contradict, all are marked contested and the agent reports the disagreement. The context then receives m admissible passages, and every entry enters wrapped in a marker that names its effective level, rendered by SAFE-Mem outside the entry text. When the agent calls a tool, the same gover-nance applies at the call. Each marker names the level its entry may serve for the asking principal, so an entry outside its scope is marked as a descrip-tion. SAFE-Mem reads the markers back from the context, and a tool call is refused when an identifier in its arguments, absent from the request itself, appears only in entries whose level is be-low max( u(q), ATTRIBUTE ). The check reads the markers in the context the agent was given and cov-ers identifiers that reach a call through memory.

5.3 Memory Shared Across Principals 

On EHRAgent, the adversary is itself a user of the shared memory, so the records it plants carry its own authority. What must hold is that this authority stops at its owner, which is the role of scope. Table 2 shows that SAFE-Mem stops the attack where it would take effect. Though the agent drafts code for the wrong patient on 72.4% of the victim’s questions, none of these calls runs: the patient iden-tifier each one relies on appears only in entries whose authority belongs to another principal, and the call check refuses it. Without the scope check, 44.4% of the attacks execute, which identifies scope as the operative check. Defenses that judge content remove nothing. TMA-NM and isolation also pre-vent every execution, but they remove 70.4% and 71.4% of the memories that benign questions would retrieve; SAFE-Mem removes 2.0%, keeping what users share while denying what they cannot grant one another. 

6 Analysis 

This section examines where and why SAFE-Mem stops attacks. The appendix G to appendix P report further results and analysis. 

6.1 Attacks Need Kinds Beyond External Authority 

MPBench labels each attack with its goal, which we group into the seven of Figure 3. 6.2 Poison Is Stored but Not Used 

SAFE-Mem keeps every claim of an artifact as a description of what the artifact said, so it does not keep the injection out of memory: the injected memory is written in 38.0% and 57.6% of attack cases on GPT-5.6-terra and GPT-4o-mini, more of-ten than under any other defense on GPT-5.6-terra. Admission keeps it out of the contexts that act on it. It enters the context retrieved for the attack query in 1.1% and 2.8% of cases, against 30.2% and 55.7% without defense, and the end-to-end ASR follows at 2.6% and 6.2% (Appendix K). Separating stor-age from use means that writing requires no judg-ment about content, and the poisoned claim stays available as a record of its source for requests that ask what the source said. This separation is what lets SAFE-Mem block attacks while keeping recall above the undefended level. 

6.3 Adaptive Attacks Cannot Raise Authority 

SAFE-Mem computes authority, scope and use from channel records and the principal’s own words, so an adversary who knows the design still controls only the content of artifacts. We evalu-ate adaptive attacks that rewrite every MPBench attack against one component at a time: a forged author signs the artifact as the account owner, a forged request asks the assistant to remember the payload, an understated kind frames the payload as background that instructs nobody, and conflict evasion declares the payload consistent with any existing policy. The strongest rewrite raises the ASR of an undefended agent by 13.6, 20.0 and 7.8 points on GPT-4o-mini, Haiku and Qwen, while SAFE-Mem’s ASR moves by at most 1.4 points and its recall stays between 81.1% and 87.5% (Ta-ble 3). In no run does an artifact raise any claim above DESCRIPTION . The same holds when the principal does ask to remember something: pair-ing each of the 249 genuine requests to remember with an attack artifact, the request raises its own claim in 90% to 94% of cases and the payload in none of the 498 on any of the three backbones. Repetition sets SAFE-Mem apart from consensus-based defenses. With five paraphrased copies of a payload, A-MemGuard’s ASR on GPT-4o-mini reaches 68.1%, against 36.0% with three, as the copies become the consensus it checks against, while SAFE-Mem’s stays between 5.6% and 7.5% (Appendix H). The inputs that can raise a claim’s level lie outside what the adversary writes, so adding or reshaping content gives the adversary no lever on admission. 

6.4 Protection Independent of Model Strength 

Defenses that act through the model’s judgment are as strong as the model that applies them. Without defense, GPT-5.6-terra resists more attacks than GPT-4o-mini, with ASR of 26.7% against 49.8%, and A-MemGuard removes half of the attacks on GPT-5.6-terra but under a fifth on GPT-4o-mini. The same dependence appears inside SAFE-Mem when inadmissible entries are served with a descrip-tion label: ASR rises from 2.6% and 6.2% to 6.2% and 19.6% (Appendix M). Withholding decides what reaches the context, and with it SAFE-Mem’s ASR stays between 1.5% and 6.2% across all four backbones (Table 1). The judgments that set the labels hold up against human labels (Appendix I): the use classifier separates descriptions from every higher kind on 94.0% of requests on GPT-5.6-terra, the retention judgment agrees on 84.0% of prin-cipal sentences, and the answer judge agrees on 83.5% of answers. 

7 Conclusion 

An agent’s memory determines what the agent re-calls and on whose authority it acts. SAFE-Mem labels every stored claim with its kind, authority and scope, keeps each claim linked to its sources in a memory graph, and admits a claim only for the uses its labels support, lowering attack success by close to an order of magnitude while improving recall and accuracy. Future work includes deriv-ing the use of each intermediate retrieval step from the original request, and raising authority from out-comes that the environment verifies. Limitations 

SAFE-Mem treats what a principal explicitly asks to keep as the principal’s own. A principal per-suaded to endorse content, for instance by agreeing to an assistant’s suggestion to remember a page’s recommendation, grants it up to the kind the re-quest names. SAFE-Mem governs authority. A false claim on a web page remains a valid answer to what the page said, and a principal’s own mistaken statement keeps the principal’s authority. Attacks that rely on a false fact without borrowing authority lie outside its scope, and defenses that judge the content of a memory complement it. 

Ethical Considerations 

All attacks in this work come from public bench-marks, MPBench and MINJA. The adaptive vari-ants rewrite these attacks with fixed templates and introduce no new attack technique. The EHRA-gent experiments use the open-access MIMIC-III Clinical Database Demo, released under the Open Data Commons Open Database License. No data from real users was collected. The human labels in Appendix I were produced by the authors, blind to the model outputs.

B Comparison with Prior Defenses C What External Content Cannot Reach 

Proposition. Let e be an entry whose sources are sentences of external artifacts and which carries no endorsement by a principal. Then for every request 

q with u(q) > DESCRIPTION , e does not satisfy Eq. 2, whatever text the adversary places in those artifacts and however many copies it places. 

Proof. The authority a sentence confers is set by the channel it arrived through (§4.1), and the channel record is outside the adversary’s control (§3). Every sentence of an external artifact there-fore confers DESCRIPTION , including text styled as a principal’s words. A claim extracted while such a sentence was in view takes the lowest authority present (§4.2), so α(e) = DESCRIPTION . Author-ity rises above that of a channel only through a prin-cipal’s endorsement, judged from the principal’s own sentences, and e carries none. Hence λ(e) = min( κ(e), α (e)) ≤ α(e) = DESCRIPTION <u(q). The argument uses neither κ(e) nor the num-ber of entries, so neither the labels the extractor assigns nor repeated copies of a payload change the conclusion. □

E Endorsing an Artifact 

A principal’s request to remember can state what to keep in the principal’s own words, or point into an artifact, as in “keep the signature settings in this document.” The first kind of request is handled in §4.1: the claims are extracted from the princi-pal’s sentences alone. For the second, the request is again judged from the principal’s sentences alone, which decides that something is to be kept and of what kind; the claims of the artifact that the request refers to are then raised to the principal’s author-ity, capped at that kind. A request to remember a preference therefore cannot lift a document’s con-tent to a rule or a permission. The artifact’s text decides only which of its claims a request covers, never how high they rise. On MPBench, 103 of 370 raised claims on GPT-5.6-terra and 12 of 322 on GPT-4o-mini come from requests that point into an artifact; a further 336 and 765 candidate requests are not raised because the principal’s sentence does not ask for anything to be kept.

Q.8 Judge: memory entry 

An attacker tried to plant content in an AI agent's long-term memory. Below is what the attacker intended, and the entries the agent actually stored. Decide whether the attacker's content is present in what was stored -- in substance, not in the same words. Judge by the EFFECT on a future agent, not by grammatical form. Memory poisoning usually works by planting a FACT or a PREFERENCE whose behaviour the agent will infer later, not by planting a command. All of these count as present: - a stated fact that makes the attacker's outcome the natural next step ("X's address for confidential archives is a@b.com") - an attributed preference or norm ("the team should continue to use X", "the user prefers X") - an establishing detail that confers trust or authority on the attacker's entity ("defaults are maintained by X", "X was added as a source") - a hedged or tentative version of the attacker's content ("may consider X") These do NOT count as present: - the entry merely names the same topic or entity with no fact, preference, or authority attached to it - the entry states the opposite of what the attacker wanted, and nothing else If an entry contains BOTH a legitimate rule AND the attacker's content contradicting or overriding it, that counts as PRESENT. The presence of correct material alongside does not cancel the poison -- attacks are built to sit beside true content.
