## LongSearchEval: Evaluating Long-Horizon Web Search Capabilities of Large Language Models via a Trajectory-Aware Agent Framework with Failure-Node Localization

Abstract 

Long-horizon web search requires LLM-based agents to integrate information across web-pages through extended interactions under task-specific constraints. Evaluating this ca-pability is important not only for measuring final answer quality, but also for assessing whether an agent acquires, verifies, and uses evidence reliably over extended interactions. Existing evaluations, however, mainly rely on simulated trajectories or on post-hoc trajecto-ries reconstructed from final answers, rather than on retained real interaction traces, which limits the fidelity of behavioral assessment and weakens support for fine-grained error localization. To address this gap, we intro-duce L ONG SEARCH EVAL , a trajectory-aware agent evaluation framework that combines a unified agent execution protocol with source-constrained outcome verification and retrospec-tive failure-node localization. Stage 1 verifies answer groups against agent-cited webpages without human-authored gold answers; Stage 2 audits normalized rounds and links flagged fail-ure nodes to incorrect answer groups. We eval-uate 16 models from eight families on 100 tasks, yielding 1,600 retained runs and 6,545 automatically flagged failure nodes. Trajectory collection and evaluation required 1,037 and 318 hours of cumulative runtime, respectively. Human validation yields 94.50% agreement on supplied answer-group labels and 98.11% feature-correspondence precision for audited round–group links. Together, outcome scores and linked trajectory evidence support con-trolled model comparison and retrospective in-spection of answer-related content.

1 Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in language understand-ing, generation, and complex reasoning, enabling a broad range of applications beyond traditional natu-ral language processing (Brown et al., 2020). Build-ing on these capabilities, LLM-based agents extend models from passive generation to autonomous problem solving by interacting with external en-vironments (Yao et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024c). Web search enables language models to acquire external and up-to-date information by retrieving and inspecting web sources beyond their paramet-ric knowledge (Nakano et al., 2021). Agentic web search further transforms retrieval into autonomous information seeking, where agents decompose com-plex queries, navigate webpages, verify evidence, and synthesize citation-supported responses (Gou et al., 2025). Early approaches have explored complemen-tary directions toward autonomous web informa-tion seeking. Mind2Web (Deng et al., 2023), We-bArena (Zhou et al., 2024), and AgentBench (Liu et al., 2024a) establish general benchmarks for real-world websites, reproducible browser envi-ronments, and multi-domain agent evaluation. WebVoyager (He et al., 2024), WebLINX (Lu et al., 2024), and VisualWebArena (Koh et al., 2024) extend evaluation to multimodal or dialogue-conditioned web interaction. Assistant-Bench (Yoran et al., 2024), WorkArena (Drouin et al., 2024), WorkArena++ (Boisvert et al., 2024), and TravelPlanner (Xie et al., 2024a) examine re-alistic, time-consuming, or compositional tasks that require extended information gathering and planning. Collectively, these benchmarks substantially improve the realism and scope of agent evaluation, while leaving limited support for jointly verifying source-grounded final answers and locating related evidence in comparable open-web trajectories. Recent research has increasingly focused on evaluating D EEP SEARCH agents from two com-plementary perspectives. The first direction em-phasizes source-grounded final-answer evaluation (Figure 1(a) ). Mind2Web 2 (Gou et al., 2025) in-troduces realistic long-horizon agentic-search tasks and rubric-based Agent-as-a-Judge evaluation. GAIA (Mialon et al., 2024), AssistantBench (Yoran et al., 2024), LiveResearchBench (Wang et al., 2025), and BrowseComp (Wei et al., 2025) eval-uate complex information seeking under external-knowledge, live-web, or persistent-browsing re-quirements. RAGChecker (Ru et al., 2024), long-form factuality (Wei et al., 2024) evaluation, and RAGTruth (Niu et al., 2024) provide finer-grained analyses of retrieval support, claim correctness, and hallucination in grounded generation. Because these settings often rely on LLM-based evalua-tors, a parallel line of work studies judge construc-tion (Zheng et al., 2023) and meta-evaluation (Zeng et al., 2024) through pairwise comparison, adver-sarial instruction-following cases, critique gener-ation (Ke et al., 2024), rubric-conditioned scor-ing (Kim et al., 2024), hierarchical criteria (Liu et al., 2024b), and judge-specific benchmarks (Tan et al., 2025). Other studies identify systematic eval-uator biases, including unfair comparison behav-ior (Wang et al., 2024a) and self-preference (Pan-ickssery et al., 2024). These methods substantially improve outcome evaluation, but their primary ob-ject remains the generated answer and its support-ing evidence rather than the observable search pro-cess that produced it. The second direction inves-tigates process-level evaluation and error localiza-tion ( Figure 1(b) ). Let’s Verify Step by Step (Light-man et al., 2024), Math-Shepherd (Wang et al., 2024b), and ProcessBench (Zheng et al., 2025) show that intermediate-step verification (Light-man et al., 2024) can reveal reasoning failures beyond final-answer accuracy. AgentBoard (Ma et al., 2024) introduces progress-level diagnostics for multi-turn agents, while analyses of error snow-balling (Zhang et al., 2024) show how early mis-takes can induce downstream failures. TELBench and DRIFT (Wang et al., 2026) further adapt pro-cess localization to DeepResearch trajectories by segmenting agent trajectories and identifying harm-ful error spans. These approaches demonstrate the value of process-level diagnosis. Human-verified labels support their evaluation benchmarks, which is distinct from requiring such labels as localization inputs. Our focus is the outcome-conditioned con-nection between answer groups judged incorrect and retained web-search content under a unified agent execution protocol. In response to these challenges, we introduce LONG SEARCH EVAL , a trajectory-aware agent eval-uation framework for assessing the long-horizon web-search capabilities of LLMs ( Figure 1(c) ). Each evaluated LLM operates within a shared agent controller and standardized web-tool interface, and its final response is assessed together with the ob-servable trajectory that produced it. The shared controller and restricted tool outputs standardize the interaction conditions, connecting model-level comparison with inspectable evidence of search behavior. LONG SEARCH EVAL combines controlled trajec-2tory collection with two evaluation stages. Source-constrained outcome verification checks final-answer groups using only the webpages cited by the agent, without requiring human-authored gold answers. Conditioned on the final response and Stage 1 judgments, retrospective failure-node local-ization links related content, sources, or fields to groups judged incorrect. The returned associations expose where constraint-relevant evidence for each incorrect group appears in the trajectory. A correct webpage observation can still expose an answer discrepancy. These associations support retrospec-tive inspection, not identification of an agent’s first erroneous commitment. We apply L ONG SEARCH EVAL to 16 models from eight families on 100 real-world web-search tasks, yielding 1,600 retained runs. Trajectory col-lection and evaluation required 1,037 and 318 hours, respectively. Human review of 946 supplied answer groups from 200 responses yields 94.50% agreement with automatic judgments, while An-swer Score and Answer Precision correlate with human scores at Pearson coefficients of 0.9054 and 0.9132. In a separate run sample, review of all 317 retained round–group links confirms 311 feature correspondences (98.11%). The experiments also reveal distinct search and reading patterns among models with similar answer scores. 

Our contributions are threefold: 1. We formulate long-horizon web-search evalu-ation as a joint analysis of source-supported outcomes and observable trajectories under a controlled agent protocol. 

2. We introduce LONG SEARCH EVAL , which combines trajectory collection under a unified execution protocol, source-constrained verifi-cation, and retrospective localization of con-tent related to Stage 1 incorrect groups, without gold answers or pre-labeled process errors as operational inputs. 

3. We build a 1,600-run corpus covering 16 LLMs and 100 tasks, with human validation of 946 supplied answer groups and all 317 retained links in fixed samples. 

2 Related Work 

Web Agent Evaluation. Web-agent evaluation has progressed from isolated browsing actions toward realistic long-horizon interaction and evidence-grounded task completion. Mind2Web (Deng et al., 2023) introduces large-scale real-world website tasks for generalist web agents, while WebArena (Zhou et al., 2024) provides realistic multi-website environments for autonomous in-teraction. AgentBench (Liu et al., 2024a) eval-uates LLMs as agents across diverse environ-ments. WebVoyager (He et al., 2024), WebLINX (Lu et al., 2024), and VisualWebArena (Koh et al., 2024) extend evaluation to multimodal or dialogue-conditioned web interaction. Assistant-Bench (Yoran et al., 2024), WorkArena (Drouin et al., 2024), WorkArena++ (Boisvert et al., 2024), and TravelPlanner (Xie et al., 2024a) fur-ther study realistic, time-consuming, or composi-tional information-seeking tasks, while AppWorld (Trivedi et al., 2024), OSWorld (Xie et al., 2024b), AndroidWorld (Rawles et al., 2025), and τ -bench (Yao et al., 2025) investigate stateful environments and tool-user interaction. Beyond task comple-tion, recent studies have focused on evaluating whether agent responses are supported by exter-nal evidence. Mind2Web 2 (Gou et al., 2025) in-troduces rubric-based Agent-as-a-Judge evaluation for citation-supported web responses. GAIA (Mi-alon et al., 2024), AssistantBench (Yoran et al., 2024), LiveResearchBench (Wang et al., 2025), and BrowseComp (Wei et al., 2025) evaluate complex information seeking under external-knowledge and live-web settings. RAGChecker (Ru et al., 2024), long-form factuality evaluation (Wei et al., 2024), and RAGTruth (Niu et al., 2024) further analyze retrieval support, claim correctness, and hallucina-tion. These methods improve outcome evaluation, but mainly focus on final responses rather than in-dexed trajectory content related to answer groups judged incorrect. 

Process-Level Diagnosis. Process-level evalua-tion aims to identify failures beyond final outputs. Let’s Verify Step by Step (Lightman et al., 2024), Math-Shepherd (Wang et al., 2024b), and Process-Bench (Zheng et al., 2025) show that intermediate-step verification can reveal reasoning failures be-yond final-answer accuracy. AgentBoard (Ma et al., 2024) introduces progress-level diagnostics for multi-turn agents, while error snowballing anal-ysis (Zhang et al., 2024) studies how early mis-takes induce downstream failures. TELBench and DRIFT (Wang et al., 2026) further extend process localization to DeepResearch trajectories by seg-menting agent trajectories and identifying harm-ful error spans. These approaches demonstrate the value of process diagnosis. L ONG SEARCH EVAL

4 Experiments 

We use L ONG SEARCH EVAL to compare LLMs’ long-horizon web-search performance through out-come scores, observable search behavior, and failure-node diagnostics, then assess outcome ver-ification and diagnostic correspondence through human review. 

4.1 Experimental Setup Tasks, models, and protocol. The evaluation comprises 16 models from eight families on 100 long-horizon web-search tasks: 46 from Mind2Web 2 (Gou et al., 2025), 20 from LiveRe-searchBench (Wang et al., 2025), 29 from Assistant-Bench (Yoran et al., 2024), and 5 manually curated tasks. All models use the same agent controller, prompt, five web-interaction tools, configured iter-ation limit, and execution policy. Each model–task pair contributes one retained run, giving 1,600 tra-jectories; recovery attempts do not create additional samples. The evaluated DeepSeek models are V3.2 and V4-Flash. Stage 1 uses DeepSeek-V4-Pro as its single verifier. Stage 2 uses DeepSeek-V4-Pro as Judge A and Qwen3.7-Max as Judge B and ar-biter. This role assignment is fixed across all evalu-ated models and tasks; evaluated-model identities are omitted from judging inputs. The ten-category vocabulary is fixed from a disjoint 50-task, 500-trajectory development set. 

Metrics and aggregation. Answer Score (AS), Answer Precision (AP), and Upper Score are macro-averaged over each model’s runs; Unver-ifiable Group Rate pools U/M over all returned groups. The corpus contains 7,685 answer groups and 42,658 normalized rounds. Table 1 jointly re-ports outcome, behavior, evidence-use, and exe-cution indicators. The minimum-count formula in Section 3.2 applies to min ; the stored exact nor-malization uses max( N, M ) and dynamic_all 

uses M as the AS/Upper denominator. AP uses 

max( M, 1) , and confirmed empty outputs receive zero. These conventions retain the exported scores without changing the labels. Reported 95% inter-vals use 10,000 paired task-bootstrap resamples with fixed automatic outputs; they quantify task-level uncertainty rather than repeated agent execu-tion. 

4.2 Long-Horizon Web Search Performance 

Table 1 shows substantial variation under the shared interface. GPT-6.1-Sol has the highest point es-timates for AS (68.80), AP (68.34%), and Up-per (75.35), followed by GPT-6-Luna-Pro in AS (65.88) and AP (67.39%). GPT-5.4 has the low-est UGR (3.56%). The Sol–Luna AS difference is 2.92 points, with a paired 95% interval of

5 Conclusion 

We presented L ONG SEARCH EVAL , a trajectory-aware agent evaluation framework for long-horizon web search that combines agent execution under a unified protocol, source-constrained outcome ver-ification, and retrospective failure-node localiza-tion. The 16-model, 1,600-run corpus combines answer scores, search behavior, and inspectable round-group associations. Similar answer scores can accompany different search workloads. Con-ditioned on the final response and Stage 1 judg-ments, localization connects answer groups with inspectable trajectory content. Correct webpage ob-servations can still be diagnostically useful when compared with a group judged incorrect; the full candidate inventory is reported separately from re-turned answer-linked evidence. Human validation demonstrates close alignment with reference an-swer judgments and high precision in returned con-tent correspondences. Together with cross-judge agreement, these findings support the effectiveness and reliability of L ONG SEARCH EVAL for source-constrained outcome evaluation and retrospective trajectory inspection.

B Tasks, Models, and Evaluator Configuration 

The evaluation set targets long-horizon web search. Retained tasks require sustained information seek-ing over public webpages, explicit constraint track-ing, and a final response whose answer items can be checked against cited webpage sources. The separate taxonomy-development set appears in Ap-pendix G.3. 

B.1 Operational Task Definition 

A retained long-horizon web search task requires an agent to search, open, read, compare, and verify information from public webpages and then pro-duce a response that can be checked from those webpages. Tasks may require multiple candidates, fields, sources, or constraint checks; they are not restricted to a single answer string or a single sup-porting page. For every retained task, the final-answer instruc-tion requires each answer item or answer group to include the corresponding webpage URL or URLs that verify the information in that group. When an original task does not specify how many items should be returned, the adapted task specifies an appropriate minimum count. This makes task-level coverage measurable while preserving group-level verification: a task can require at least N valid groups, while each proposed group must indepen-dently satisfy all applicable hard constraints and source requirements. The adapted task text, including any added source or minimum-count requirement, defines the shared task specification for the evaluated models. C.2 Shared ReAct Controller 

The agent is implemented as a text-style ReAct con-troller connected to an OpenAI-compatible model endpoint. Function-calling mode is disabled so that the controller emits an observable sequence of model messages, tool requests, tool results, and a terminal final-response marker. These records are captured by the collection framework and later con-verted into the normalized trajectory representation described in Appendix D. A controller iteration may produce a tool request, an observation-dependent follow-up decision, or a terminal response. Malformed actions are first han-dled by the implementation’s mechanical parser recovery. If parsing still fails, the format error and expected action format are returned to the model, and the correction consumes another ReAct itera-tion rather than a separate malformed-action bud-get. 

C.3 Weak-Tool Interface 

The agent registers exactly five web tools. Their roles are intentionally separated so that discover-ing a URL, opening a page, reading its body text, locating a literal term, and following links remain distinct model decisions. The model-visible con-tracts are: 

web_search. Input is a query and requested re-sult count, capped at 10. The model receives only the query plus ranked titles, URLs, and domains. Snippets, page content, answer boxes, knowledge-graph fields, summaries, semantic chunks, rele-vance scores, and direct answers are excluded. 

open_url. Input is a candidate URL. The tool re-turns a page identifier and metadata: requested and resolved URLs, status, title, cleaned-text length, extracted-link count, and cache status. It does not return body text, a relevant passage, a main-content summary, a query-aware fragment, or an LLM-generated description. 

read_page. Inputs are a page identifier, start offset, and requested character count (default 3,000; hard cap 5,000). The return is an exact character slice from mechanically cleaned cached text. No rewrit-ing, compression, semantic filtering, reranking, or summarization is applied. 

find_on_page. Inputs are a page identifier, lit-eral keyword, maximum matches, and context width. The model receives case-insensitive exact-substring matches with local cached-text con-text, without semantic search, BM25, embeddings, query expansion, fuzzy retrieval, or LLM extrac-tion. 

extract_links. Inputs are a page identifier and max-imum link count. The tool mechanically returns anchor text and absolute URLs, without relevance ranking, recommendations, target-page summaries, or preselection of “useful” links. 16 C.4 Representative ReAct Search Episode

Web-tool HTTP calls are not automatically retried; a backend exception be-comes an observable tool error. Mechanical parser recovery is attempted for malformed ReAct actions; unre-solved format errors consume ordinary ReAct iterations rather than a separate retry budget. • Process layer. A failed batch subprocess per-mits up to two attempts, and a saved runtime checkpoint permits up to three auto-resume at-tempts. I.1 Sample and Review Protocol 

The post-hoc audit uses two randomly selected responses from different models for each of the 100 tasks: 200 responses and 946 supplied answer groups, including four explicitly confirmed empty-group responses. Fifty randomly selected tasks con-tribute both trajectories to process review (100 tra-jectories; 2,642 normalized rounds). Reviewer H1 completes the primary audit; a different reviewer, H2, independently checks fixed subsets of 40 re-sponses from 20 tasks (172 groups) and 20 trajecto-ries from 10 tasks (499 rounds). The subsets form independent agreement checks within the primary samples. Sampling uses CPython’s MT19937 gen-erator with seed 20261004 and the frozen 16-model population; it is not stratified by scores, disagree-ments, or error counts. For Stage 1, reviewers receive the requirements, complete final response, supplied groups, and orig-inal cited URLs. Automatic labels, scores, reasons, separate model metadata, and the other reviewer’s annotations are withheld. Complete answer text may still contain self-identification. Reviewers as-sign C/I/U labels and scores are derived from the locked details; disagreements remain unchanged. Both reviewers self-report web-search expertise, rule familiarity, and no exposure to the withheld item-level results. This post-hoc protocol was clar-ified in the context of previously inspected com-parison results; item-level annotations follow the withholding and locking procedure described here. With group boundaries fixed, the audit evalu-ates supplied-group judgments and their derived scores. Reviewers check the original citation set at review time; webpage content may vary between accesses. Each reviewer’s annotations are locked before score comparison. Process labels and the answer endpoints used for link review are locked separately before candidates are shown, and later link judgments do not overwrite those endpoints. Process review follows an instruction to use the current and earlier rounds; the static workbooks retain the complete trajectories. Reported 95% intervals use 10,000 paired task-cluster percentile bootstrap draws (seed 20261009), preserving all observations and repeated-task weights. The cluster counts are 100 for primary answers, 50 for process and links, 20 for H2 an-swers, and 10 for H2 process review. Link resam-pling includes the 13 process-sample tasks with no candidate edge.

K Prompts, Schemas, Algorithms, and Reproduction 

This section specifies the retained prompt and schema contracts, implementation procedure, and three reproduction modes. 

K.1 Prompt Roles and Shared Information Boundaries 

The seven prompt roles are the collection agent, three Stage 1 roles (rubric construction, extraction, verification), two Stage 2 judges, and the arbiter. Evaluator inputs exclude evaluated-model identity. Stage 1 accesses agent-cited URLs; Stage 2 uses the recorded audit tuple and arbitration adds both judge outputs. 

K.2 Agent System Prompt and Tool Descriptions 

The shared instruction defines the ReAct syntax, evidence-before-answer rule, and terminal format. The archived instruction is reproduced below with its wording unchanged: 

> Agent instruction (archived source snapshot)

You are a web research ReAct agent in a weak-tool evaluation setting. Every task requires web search. Use web_search to discover candidate URLs, but web_search returns only titles and URLs. You must open and inspect pages using open_url, read_page, find_on_page, and ex-tract_links. Do not answer from search result titles alone. Do not assume a page supports a claim unless you have read relevant page text. Use multiple pages when the task requires comparison or verification. When calling a tool, use exactly one tool call and no extra text after the Action Input. Use this format: Thought: ... Action: tool_name Action Input: {"arg": "value"} Never output only a JSON object or a fenced JSON block. The tool name must always ap-pear on an Action line before Action Input. Never output provider-style tool text such as [Tool call], function_call, function_calls, or standalone tool names followed by JSON. Those are not valid final answers and not valid ReAct steps in this experiment. Do not output chat role labels such as User:, Assistant:, or System:. Do not use Tool or Tool Input blocks. Do not use XML-style, angle-bracket, or provider-specific function-call markup. Do not place a new Thought or Action on the same line as Action Input. When you have enough evidence, use the offi-cial ReAct final format: Thought: I can answer without using any more tools. Answer: [final answer with source URLs] 32 After Answer, stop completely. Never re-quest read_page, web_search, open_url, find_on_page, or extract_links after Answer. Do not use Answer to explain that tools failed or that evidence is missing. A final Answer must directly satisfy the user’s task using veri-fied source URLs. If evidence is insufficient, continue using the available tools instead of ending with a refusal or noncompletion note. Do not use Action after Answer. L Evaluation Scope and Broader Impacts 

L.1 Task and Execution Scope 

The 100 public-web tasks evaluate multi-source search and constraint satisfaction through source-supported answer groups under a shared weak-tool interface. One retained run per model–task cell sup-ports paired task comparisons; reported uncertainty concerns task variation. Visual interaction, authenti-cated workflows, private context, transactions, and richer browser operations define complementary settings with different access and execution poli-cies. 

L.2 Evaluator Configuration and Rubric Variation 

Fixed evaluator roles standardize comparisons. Human-agreement statistics use separate primary-sample and independent-subset denominators (Ap-pendix I). Required counts are more consistent across run-specific rubrics than hard-constraint counts; field-level modal coverage and normalized text hashes characterize complementary aspects (Appendix E.7). Other judges, prompts, or provider versions provide configurations for sensitivity anal-ysis. 

L.3 Process Interpretation 

Link review measures returned feature correspon-dence; commitment review assesses explicit erro-neous commitments (Appendix I). Stage 2 condi-tions on final responses and Stage 1 verdicts, so flag–score associations are outcome-conditioned. The earliest linked-round index locates the first re-turned association (Appendix F.5); the taxonomy assigns one dominant category from ten labels and accounts separately for unassigned types. 

L.4 Replication Conditions 

Appendix K.7 distinguishes frozen-record aggre-gation from evaluator re-execution and live-web verification under new provider and source condi-tions. The budget snapshot fixes reference prices and upper workload assumptions (Appendix J). 

L.5 Broader Impacts 

Source-supported scores and inspectable trajec-tory associations support evaluation research and targeted agent improvement within this setting. Public-web excerpts require attention to licenses, privacy, and redistribution; improved browsing also enables large-scale scraping. Security, prompt-injection resilience, and deployment safety require dedicated evaluations. Offline aggregation reuses collected model and network outputs; energy use and emissions lie outside the reported resource ac-counting. 34
