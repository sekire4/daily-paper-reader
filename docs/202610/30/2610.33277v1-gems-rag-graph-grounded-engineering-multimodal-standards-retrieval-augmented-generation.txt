Abstract 

Engineering standards are challenging knowledge sources for large language models (LLMs) because they are hier-archical, multimodal, and densely connected through fig-ures, tables, cross-references, and normative rules. Exist-ing retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) methods often rely on unstructured text retrieval and struggle with ques-tions requiring joint reasoning over textual provisions, visual specifications, and document structure. We propose GEMS-RAG (Graph-grounded Engineering Multimodal Standards Retrieval-Augmented Generation), a framework for grounded reasoning over engineering standards. GEMS-RAG com-bines hierarchical parsing, fine-grained visual entity extrac-tion, multimodal knowledge graph construction, and graph-grounded hybrid retrieval. Instead of treating figures as mono-lithic images, it decomposes them into meaningful visual entities linked to textual provisions, engineering concepts, and regulatory rules. During inference, dense, sparse, and vi-sual retrieval are combined with graph expansion to identify relevant evidence across text, figures, and document struc-ture. We evaluate GEMS-RAG on the Manual on Uniform Traffic Control Devices (MUTCD), a large engineering stan-dard containing regulations, technical drawings, and stan-dardized traffic-control devices. Results show that graph-grounded multimodal retrieval improves retrieval effective-ness and question-answering performance over text-only, mul-timodal, and graph-based RAG baselines. Beyond MUTCD, GEMS-RAG offers a general framework for reasoning over engineering codes, technical manuals, regulatory documents, and scientific standards in safety-critical applications.

## Introduction 

Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated remark-able capabilities in language understanding, reasoning, and question answering. However, their effectiveness remains fundamentally constrained by the knowledge encoded dur-ing pretraining (Kuai et al. 2026a). Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) has emerged as a powerful paradigm for grounding LLMs in external knowledge by retrieving rele-vant documents at inference time and incorporating them into the generation process (Lewis et al. 2020, 2021; Asai et al. 

Copyright © 2027, Association for the Advancement of Artificial Intelligence (www.aaai.org). All rights reserved. 

2023; Zhai 2024). While recent RAG systems have achieved substantial success on web documents (Yang et al. 2024), scientific literature (Lála et al. 2023), and domain knowl-edge bases (Kuai et al. 2026b), they remain poorly suited for regulatory and engineering standards documents that ex-hibit rich hierarchical organization, multimodal content, and normative reasoning requirements. Engineering standards documents constitute a particu-larly challenging class of knowledge sources for retrieval-augmented generation. Unlike conventional text corpora, they combine hierarchical organization, normative language, technical specifications, figures, tables, design examples, and dense cross-references. For example, the Manual on Uni-form Traffic Control Devices (MUTCD) (Federal Highway Administration 2025) contains textual regulations, engineer-ing guidance, standardized traffic-control devices, technical drawings, sign specifications, warrants, and cross-referenced provisions. In such documents, critical knowledge is rarely contained in a single passage; instead, correct interpretation often requires connecting textual rules with visual specifi-cations, document structure, and regulatory context (Doris et al. 2025; Shi et al. 2026). Conventional RAG systems face three key limitations when applied to engineering standards documents such as the MUTCD. First, many retrieval pipelines represent docu-ments as collections of independent text chunks, which can weaken the hierarchical context that determines the scope and interpretation of regulatory provisions. Second, even when multimodal content is considered, figures and diagrams are often indexed only at the page, caption, or image level, lim-iting access to the fine-grained visual semantics embedded in engineering symbols, standardized devices, and technical drawings. Third, standard dense or sparse retrieval does not explicitly model cross-references, regulatory dependencies, or relationships among sections, figures, tables, devices, and rule types. As a result, existing systems may produce in-complete, decontextualized, or inconsistent answers when standards-compliant reasoning requires joint evidence from text, visuals, and document structure. To address these limitations, we propose GEMS-RAG ,a Graph-grounded Engineering Multimodal Standards Retrieval-Augmented Generation framework for engineering standards documents which combines hierarchical document parsing, multimodal figure decomposition, dense and sparse arXiv:2610.33277v1 [cs.AI] 23 Oct 2026 retrieval, and graph-based knowledge organization within a unified retrieval architecture. Instead of treating figures as auxiliary content, we explicitly extract and index individual visual entities, such as traffic signs, plaques, and signal il-lustrations, and connect them to their corresponding textual provisions through a document knowledge graph. The re-sulting system enables retrieval over both textual and visual semantics while preserving the structural relationships that govern interpretation of the standard. More specifically, we construct a multimodal knowledge graph that captures relationships among sections, figures, traffic control devices, regulatory concepts, and normative statements. During retrieval, graph-based expansion supple-ments traditional dense retrieval by identifying structurally related content, allowing the system to recover relevant provi-sions even when they are distributed across multiple sections and modalities. Retrieved textual and visual evidence are then jointly provided to a vision-language reasoning model for grounded response generation. The contributions of this paper are threefold: 

• We introduce a standards-aware hierarchical multimodal framework that represents engineering standards as struc-tured collections of text, figures, tables, visual enti-ties, cross-references, and rule types rather than isolated chunks. 

• We propose a graph-grounded hybrid retrieval mecha-nism integrating hierarchical parsing, textual and visual retrieval, graph expansion, and rule-aware ranking to sup-port reasoning across sections, devices, figures, and nor-mative relationships. 

• We develop a multimodal RAG benchmark and system for the MUTCD, as an example, showing that fine-grained visual grounding, preserved document structure, and nor-mative rule modeling improve retrieval and question an-swering over complex engineering standards. 

## Related Work 

Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Domain-Specific Knowledge 

RAG has become a widely adopted approach for ground-ing large language models in external knowledge. By re-trieving relevant evidence at inference time, RAG reduces reliance on parametric memory and improves factuality in question answering over open-domain documents, scientific literature, and domain-specific knowledge bases (Karpukhin et al. 2020). Recent advances have further improved retrieval quality through hybrid dense–sparse retrieval, reranking, it-erative retrieval, self-reflection, and adaptive retrieval plan-ning (Asai et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2024; Zhai 2024). These methods have demonstrated strong potential for knowledge-intensive reasoning tasks. Most existing RAG systems as-sume that knowledge can be represented as independent tex-tual chunks. This assumption is inadequate for engineering standards, where meaning is distributed across section hi-erarchies, cross-references, tables, figures, captions, device drawings, and normative statements. A locally relevant pas-sage may therefore be insufficient without its structural lo-cation, visual specifications, and related provisions. Conse-quently, text-centered RAG may retrieve relevant fragments yet still produce noncompliant answers. This limitation mo-tivates a standards-aware architecture that preserves docu-ment hierarchy and regulatory context throughout retrieval and generation. 

Multimodal Understanding of Engineering Standards Documents 

Multimodal retrieval and vision-language models (Mathew, Karatzas, and Jawahar 2021; Xia et al. 2025; Li et al. 2025; Kuai et al. 2026b) provide an important foundation for rea-soning over visual and textual information. Shared image– text embedding models and recent multimodal foundation models have shown strong capabilities in visual question an-swering, document understanding, chart interpretation, and image-grounded generation. These developments have mo-tivated multimodal RAG systems that retrieve images, ta-bles, document pages, or visual regions alongside textual evidence (Yu et al. 2025). Engineering standards present a specialized form of multimodality. Their visuals often en-code formal concepts, standardized devices, geometric lay-outs, operational constraints, and regulatory specifications. For example, traffic signs, plaques, lane-use arrows, signal faces, and pavement markings are regulated entities with defined meanings, usage conditions, and governing provi-sions. Treating a figure as a monolithic image or relying only on its caption therefore obscures the fine-grained semantics required for grounded reasoning. Standards-centric multi-modal RAG must explicitly extract, index, and link visual entities to their textual and regulatory context. 

Graph-Grounded Retrieval and Structural Reasoning 

Knowledge graph-augmented retrieval offers a natural mech-anism for representing relationships among entities, con-cepts, documents, and rules (Procko and Ochoa 2024; Park et al. 2026; Hsiao et al. 2026; Luo et al. 2026; Zhu et al. 2025; Wu et al. 2026). Graph-based RAG methods can expand retrieved evidence through neighboring nodes, connect se-mantically related concepts, and support multi-hop reasoning beyond lexical similarity. These capabilities are especially relevant for engineering standards, where correct interpreta-tion often depends on relationships among sections, figures, tables, definitions, cross-references, and rule types. Never-theless, most existing graph-RAG approaches build graphs from textual entities, citations, or domain ontologies. They rarely treat engineering visuals as first-class entities or jointly represent visual objects, document hierarchy, regulatory con-cepts, and normative statements. Likewise, document intel-ligence methods can parse layouts, tables, and figures, but usually serve only as preprocessing rather than supporting graph-grounded retrieval and reasoning. To address these limitations, this paper proposes GEMS-RAG, a graph-grounded multimodal RAG framework for engineering standards. GEMS-RAG preserves document hi-erarchy, decomposes figures into fine-grained visual entities, and constructs a multimodal knowledge graph linking sec-tions, figures, traffic-control devices, regulatory concepts, Figure 1: Framework of GEMS-RAG and normative statements. By combining dense, sparse, and visual retrieval with graph expansion, it supports grounded reasoning across the textual, visual, and structural semantics of engineering standards. As shown in Figure 1, GEMS-RAG parses standards by their native hierarchy, extracts captioned figures and tables, links textual and visual elements in a knowledge graph, retrieves multimodal evidence, and adap-tively gates visual evidence per query. 

## Methodology 

Problem Formulation 

Let D = ( d1, d 2, . . . , d N ) denote a standards document con-taining textual passages, figures, tables, and cross-references. We represent each document element as ci = ( xi, v i, m i),where xi denotes the textual content, vi denotes the visual content, and mi contains structural metadata, including the page number, section identifier, rule type, and figure or ta-ble identifier. Given a user query q, the system retrieves a supporting evidence set 

R(q) = {ci1 , c i2 , . . . , c ik } , (1) and generates an answer

Hierarchical Document Parsing 

Many engineering standards organize their content into parts, chapters, sections, and typed paragraphs. GEMS-RAG pre-serves this native structure rather than treating the document as an unstructured sequence of pages. Each textual chunk is assigned a hierarchy label 

hi = ( pi, c i, s i, r i), (3) where pi, ci, si and ri denote the part, chapter, section, and normative rule type of chunk i, respectively. Text is segmented by paragraph within each section, with each chunk retaining its heading, page number, and related figure, table, and section references. The parser also normalizes identifier variants to ensure consistent cross-reference matching. 

Caption-Anchored Figure and Table Extraction 

GEMS-RAG uses detected captions as spatial anchors to extract figures and tables from each page. For a caption b

on a page of height H, the corresponding visual region is defined by 

region( b) = [ ybot (b) + δ1, min ( ytop (b′), H − δ2)] , (4) where b′ denotes the next caption below b, ybot (b) and 

ytop (b′) define the relevant caption boundaries, and δ1 and 

δ2 provide small spatial margins. The extraction procedure adapts to variations in page lay-out, including multi-column pages, horizontally aligned vi-suals, and figures distributed across multiple panels or pages. Spatial cues from caption position, column boundaries, and neighboring document elements are used to isolate the cor-responding visual content. Visual components that share the same document identifier are subsequently consolidated into a single canonical entity during graph construction. A secondary recovery procedure is applied when an iden-tified figure or table is not successfully extracted during the initial pass. This procedure relaxes the spatial constraints to improve coverage of small or irregularly formatted vi-sual elements. Extraction completeness is verified through a document-wide consistency check between referenced vi-sual identifiers and the figures and tables recovered by the pipeline. Identifiers corresponding to external documents are excluded from this comparison. Any unresolved references are flagged for additional extraction or review before inges-tion is completed.

Retrieval Procedure 

For a query q, GEMS-RAG extracts figure, table, sec-tion, and traffic-control-device identifiers and maps them to knowledge-graph nodes. It retrieves text using dense and learned sparse search, fuses rankings through reciprocal rank fusion, and scores candidates using graph proximity, rule-type weights, and hierarchy priors in Eq. (14). The cross-encoder in Eq. (15) then produces the textual evidence set

Finally, the frame-work assembles textual evidence, visual crops, captions, and page renderings into the generation context and produces an answer with graph-verified section, figure, table, and rule-type citations.

## Conclusion 

This paper presented GEMS-RAG, a graph-grounded mul-timodal RAG framework for engineering standards. Unlike conventional pipelines that treat standards as independent text chunks, GEMS-RAG preserves document hierarchy, de-composes figures into fine-grained visual entities, and links provisions, figures, devices, rule types, and cross-references in a multimodal knowledge graph. By combining dense, sparse, and visual retrieval with graph expansion and rule-aware ranking, it enables standards-grounded question an-swering sensitive to visual specifications and regulatory structure. Using the MUTCD as a representative engineering stan-dards corpus, we constructed a multimodal knowledge base containing textual sections, figures, tables, visual entities, and graph relations. The experimental evaluation demon-strates that explicitly modeling multimodal and structural relationships can improve evidence retrieval, visual ground-ing, and downstream question answering compared with text-only, multimodal, and graph-based RAG baselines. The case study also illustrates that GEMS-RAG can retrieve not only the relevant textual provisions, but also associated visual en-tities, rule categories, and graph-connected context, leading to more complete and interpretable responses. Beyond the MUTCD, further work will apply GEMS-RAG to other en-gineering codes, design manuals and scientific standards.

## Reproducibility Assets 

The benchmark assets were frozen before model execution. The runtime question file contained only the question identi-fier and question text. A separate locked gold file contained the gold answer, required sections, source pages, figures, tables, answerability labels, and M-SDI annotations. The answering models did not load the gold file. This separa-tion prevented answer leakage and kept the generation runs independent from the evaluation annotations. The benchmark contained 150 questions, with 120 answer-able and 30 unanswerable questions. The primary-modality distribution was 60 text, 30 table, 30 figure, and 30 mixed questions. The development split contained 30 questions and the test split contained 120 questions. The development split supported prompt and retrieval configuration. The test split remained fixed for final reporting. We retained the source document, question-only bench-mark, locked annotations, model registry, runtime manifests, answer files, retrieval files, error logs, item-level scores, abla-tion outputs, and per-response rationale records. Each model run wrote separate JSONL files for answers and retrieval traces together with a manifest that recorded the model iden-tifier, run identifier, runner version, and execution settings. These files provide the audit trail used to reproduce the re-ported tables and figures. Version control recorded the implementation state used for the experiments. The updated ablation archives identify harness version ablations_v3 

and Git commit ba26b07031398fb25efca 8ba793a42c6ebb4ae03 . The main benchmark records also identify runner version 1.1.2 and preserve the exact provider model identifiers. Recording both the run manifest and source revision distinguishes changes in model behavior from changes in the evaluation code. 

## Implementation Hyperparameters 

The values in Tables 1 and 2 come from the saved configura-tion, run manifests, provider adapter, and ablation metadata. Parameters that the code did not set explicitly are identified as provider defaults. 

Retry, Resume, and Failure Handling 

The runner wrote each completed record to disk before mov-ing to the next question. Resume mode skipped valid records that already existed and continued from missing or failed question identifiers. The runner allowed at most five provider attempts and placed a 2.0-second interval between requests. For Claude, the provider adapter used a 16,000-token output cap and performed one truncation-specific retry with twice the limit, capped at 32,000 tokens. We reran records only for operational failures, missing records, corrupted outputs, provider errors, or materially incomplete retrieval logging. We did not rerun an answer because it was factually incorrect or received a low score. When a retry succeeded, the evaluator retained the first valid response for the question and preserved the original failure record for audit. This rule avoided selecting a more favorable answer after observing model quality. 

Role of GPT-5.6 and the Evaluated Language Models 

The authors used GPT-5.6 to review benchmark-runner code, provider adapters, runtime validation checks, scoring scripts, plotting code, and technical documentation. The authors executed the code, inspected saved records, selected the final settings, and resolved failures before accepting an output. Model-generated code or prose did not enter the reported results without execution and record-level verification. During evaluation, GPT-5.6 supported benchmark con-struction, evidence review, and application of the locked answer-quality rubric. It did not generate the retrieval ranks, latency values, calibration statistics, or composite scores. Fixed scripts calculated those quantities from the saved records. The evaluated vision-language models received only the user question and retrieved evidence. They did not receive the locked gold answer file. The cross-model study used twelve vision-language con-figurations under the same benchmark and scoring frame-work. The selected model also controlled visual filtering when the filter-model override was null. Claude Fable 5 pro-duced the answers for the detailed modality, difficulty, and ablation analyses after it achieved the highest provisional score in the cross-model comparison. The ablation study kept Claude Fable 5 and the few-shot prompt fixed while removing one retrieval component at a time.

1. T002 Text, Easy, Answerable, M-SDI 1/10 

Question : Does the applicability of the MUTCD to a facility open to public travel depend on whether the facility is publicly or privately owned or on the source of its funding? 

Locked Gold Answer : No. Applicability is independent of public or private ownership or jurisdiction and independent of whether funding is Federal, State, local, or private. 

Locked Evidence : Section 1A.01, PDF page 42. 

2. T005 Text, Easy, Answerable, M-SDI 3/10 

Question : How do the MUTCD headings Standard, Guidance, Option, and Support differ, including the verbs typically associ-ated with them? 

Locked Gold Answer : A Standard is mandatory or prohibitive and typically uses ’shall.’ Guidance is recommended practice with justified deviations allowed and typically uses ’should.’ An Option is permissive and typically uses ’may.’ Support is infor-mational and conveys no mandate, recommendation, permission, prohibition, or enforceable condition. 

Locked Evidence : Section 1C.01, PDF page 54. 

3. T025 Text, Medium, Answerable, M-SDI 5/10 

Question : What must a Speed Limit sign display, in what in-crements, and may it be used to show an advisory speed for a roadway condition? 

Locked Gold Answer : It must display the speed limit estab-lished by law, ordinance, regulation, or an authorized agency based on an engineering study, in multiples of 5 mph. It should not be used for an advisory speed; an advisory speed plaque below an appropriate warning sign should be used instead. 

Locked Evidence : Section 2B.21, PDF pages 126, 127.

5. TB002 Table, Medium, Answerable, M-SDI 4/10

Medium Questions: 10 Locked Examples 

1. T009 Text, Medium, Answerable, M-SDI 4/10 

Question : When a non-compliant traffic control device is re-placed because it is damaged, missing, or no longer serviceable, what is the general rule and the limited replacement-in-kind ex-ception? 

Locked Gold Answer : The replacement must generally be a compliant device. A non-compliant device may be replaced in kind only when engineering judgment finds that a lone compli-ant device among adjacent non-compliant devices would confuse users and/or the planned replacement schedule for the whole se-ries will achieve timely compliance. 

Locked Evidence : Section 1B.03, PDF page 47. 

2. T012 Text, Medium, Answerable, M-SDI 5/10 

Question : What public-domain condition applies to a new traffic control device proposed for experimentation or interim approval? 

Locked Gold Answer : The traffic-control message, design, or application must be in the public domain and not protected by patent, trademark, or copyright. Legally binding statements are required from the relevant agency/developer/supplier for experi-mentation. Protection of internal components or manufacturing methods can still be allowed. 

Locked Evidence : Sections 1B.05, 1D.06, PDF pages 50, 74, 75. 

3. T035 Text, Medium, Answerable, M-SDI 5/10 

Question : Does satisfying a traffic signal warrant automatically require installation of a traffic control signal? 

Locked Gold Answer : No. A warrant is a threshold that triggers analysis; it is not conclusive justification. Installation must be based on an engineering study considering safety, operation, alternatives, and the site. A signal can also be installed where no warrant is met if an engineering study documents why it is the best solution. 

Locked Evidence : Sections 4B.04, 4C.01, PDF pages 691, 694. 

4. T046 Text, Medium, Answerable, M-SDI 4/10 

Question : When two-way traffic must share one lane for a lim-ited distance, what coordination rule and control methods apply? 

Locked Gold Answer : Movements from both ends must be co-ordinated, except for a short, low-volume, mutually visible work zone that can self-regulate. Methods include flaggers, flag trans-fer, a pilot car, temporary traffic signals, or stop/yield control. 

Locked Evidence : Section 6E.01, PDF page 828.

## Reproducibility Assets 

The benchmark assets were frozen before model execution. The runtime question file contained only the question identi-fier and question text. A separate locked gold file contained the gold answer, required sections, source pages, figures, tables, answerability labels, and M-SDI annotations. The answering models did not load the gold file. This separa-tion prevented answer leakage and kept the generation runs independent from the evaluation annotations. The benchmark contained 150 questions, with 120 answer-able and 30 unanswerable questions. The primary-modality distribution was 60 text, 30 table, 30 figure, and 30 mixed questions. The development split contained 30 questions and the test split contained 120 questions. The development split supported prompt and retrieval configuration. The test split remained fixed for final reporting. We retained the source document, question-only bench-mark, locked annotations, model registry, runtime manifests, answer files, retrieval files, error logs, item-level scores, abla-tion outputs, and per-response rationale records. Each model run wrote separate JSONL files for answers and retrieval traces together with a manifest that recorded the model iden-tifier, run identifier, runner version, and execution settings. These files provide the audit trail used to reproduce the re-ported tables and figures. Version control recorded the implementation state used for the experiments. The updated ablation archives identify harness version ablations_v3 

and Git commit ba26b07031398fb25efca 8ba793a42c6ebb4ae03 . The main benchmark records also identify runner version 1.1.2 and preserve the exact provider model identifiers. Recording both the run manifest and source revision distinguishes changes in model behavior from changes in the evaluation code. 

## Implementation Hyperparameters 

The values in Tables 1 and 2 come from the saved configura-tion, run manifests, provider adapter, and ablation metadata. Parameters that the code did not set explicitly are identified as provider defaults. 

Retry, Resume, and Failure Handling 

The runner wrote each completed record to disk before mov-ing to the next question. Resume mode skipped valid records that already existed and continued from missing or failed question identifiers. The runner allowed at most five provider attempts and placed a 2.0-second interval between requests. For Claude, the provider adapter used a 16,000-token output cap and performed one truncation-specific retry with twice the limit, capped at 32,000 tokens. We reran records only for operational failures, missing records, corrupted outputs, provider errors, or materially incomplete retrieval logging. We did not rerun an answer because it was factually incorrect or received a low score. When a retry succeeded, the evaluator retained the first valid response for the question and preserved the original failure record for audit. This rule avoided selecting a more favorable answer after observing model quality. 

Role of GPT-5.6 and the Evaluated Language Models 

The authors used GPT-5.6 to review benchmark-runner code, provider adapters, runtime validation checks, scoring scripts, plotting code, and technical documentation. The authors executed the code, inspected saved records, selected the final settings, and resolved failures before accepting an output. Model-generated code or prose did not enter the reported results without execution and record-level verification. During evaluation, GPT-5.6 supported benchmark con-struction, evidence review, and application of the locked answer-quality rubric. It did not generate the retrieval ranks, latency values, calibration statistics, or composite scores. Fixed scripts calculated those quantities from the saved records. The evaluated vision-language models received only the user question and retrieved evidence. They did not receive the locked gold answer file. The cross-model study used twelve vision-language con-figurations under the same benchmark and scoring frame-work. The selected model also controlled visual filtering when the filter-model override was null. Claude Fable 5 pro-duced the answers for the detailed modality, difficulty, and ablation analyses after it achieved the highest provisional score in the cross-model comparison. The ablation study kept Claude Fable 5 and the few-shot prompt fixed while removing one retrieval component at a time.
