# From Relevant Memories to Sufficient Evidence: Rethinking Memory Utilization in LLM Agents 

Shixuan Li 1*, Wei Yang 1*, Peiyu Zhang 1, Anzhe Cheng 1, Heng Ping 1,Xiaole Zhang 1, Jesse Thomason 2, Xuezhe Ma 1, Yue Zhao 1, Paul Bogdan 1,

Abstract 

Long-term memory enables LLM agents to reuse past experiences, yet retrieving relevant memories does not necessarily provide suffi-cient evidence for answering a query. Essen-tial information may remain fragmented across memory records, obscured by abstraction, or disconnected from the relations required for reasoning. We identify this relevance– sufficiency gap and propose QEMem, a frame-work that reformulates memory utilization as the construction of query-specific evidence sets, shifting the focus from individual retrieval rel-evance to collective evidential support. QE-Mem integrates three complementary mecha-nisms: query-guided discovery retrieves evi-dence across multiple memory granularities; source-grounded reification organizes it into structured ViewCards with explicit evidential roles and source references; and sufficiency-guided refinement selectively recovers miss-ing information. Operating on existing mem-ory stores without modifying memory forma-tion, QEMem achieves 91.43% LLM-judge accuracy on LoCoMo with Claude Opus 4.5, outperforming the strongest evaluated base-line, MemR 3, by 7.6 percentage points and NEMORI by 10.7 points under a shared mem-ory store. Further analyses demonstrate im-proved evidence coverage and strong perfor-mance on questions requiring cross-session ev-idence integration. Code is available at https: //github.com/shixuanli01/QEMem .

1 Introduction 

Long-term memory enables large language model (LLM) agents to maintain continuity across ex-tended interactions and reuse past experiences. Recent systems, including Mem0 (Chhikara et al., 2025), A-Mem (Xu et al., 2025), and NEMORI (Nan et al., 2025), have advanced the persistent storage and structured organization of 

> *These authors contributed equally to this work.

memories, while SimpleMem (Liu et al., 2026) and related approaches improve memory consolidation and retrieval. Although these advances make past information increasingly accessible, retrieving rel-evant memories does not necessarily provide suffi-cient evidence to answer a query. A central difficulty lies in the mismatch between the granularity of retrieved memories and the facts and relations required to answer a query. Rele-vant information may remain fragmented across memory records or obscured by abstraction, while the relationships needed to connect individual facts may remain implicit. Recent approaches, includ-ing MemGAS (Xu et al., 2026), REMem (Shu et al., 2026), RaMem (Yang et al., 2026b) and MemR 3 (Du et al., 2025), have advanced multi-granular memory access, episodic reasoning, and reflective retrieval, respectively. Even with these advances, individually relevant memories may still fail to jointly provide the facts and relations needed for answering. We characterize this disconnect as the relevance–sufficiency gap , raising a fundamen-tal question: How can an agent construct a source-grounded evidence set that collectively satisfies a query’s information requirements? To address this question, we shift the fundamen-tal unit of memory utilization from individual mem-ory records to a query-specific evidence set , whose value lies in the joint support of complementary evidence rather than isolated retrieval relevance. Constructing such a set requires recovering the nec-essary facts and relations, preserving their source references, and identifying missing information within limited retrieval and reasoning budgets. Our diagnostic analysis illustrates why retaining past information is not enough to make the required ev-idence accessible. On LoCoMo (Maharana et al., 2024), although NEMORI retains nearly all origi-nal interaction records, its retrieved episodes cover the source turns of all annotated supporting evi-dence for only 68.8% of questions. On questions 1arXiv:2610.22668v1 [cs.AI] 15 Oct 2026 with complete source coverage, answer accuracy reaches approximately 93% (Section 5.2), suggest-ing that access to stored evidence remains a major bottleneck even for strong LLMs. These findings motivate query-specific evidence construction as a central objective of long-term memory utilization. To operationalize this perspective, we pro-pose QEMem, a framework that constructs query-specific evidence sets over an existing memory store. QEMem begins with query-guided evidence discovery , identifying the information requirements of a query and recovering complementary evidence across episodic memories, semantic facts, and orig-inal interaction turns. Through source-grounded evidence reification, retrieved fragments are orga-nized into structured ViewCards that make query-relevant claims, evidential relations, temporal in-formation, and supporting source spans explicit. Fi-nally, sufficiency-guided evidence refinement iden-tifies unresolved information needs and selectively retrieves additional evidence, providing a more complete basis for answer generation. Our main contributions are: (i) We identify the relevance–sufficiency gap in long-term agent memory and introduce the 

query-specific evidence set as a perspective that shifts memory utilization from individual relevance to collective evidential support. (ii) We propose QEMem , a framework that uni-fies query-guided evidence discovery, source-grounded evidence reification, and sufficiency-guided refinement to construct structured, source-grounded evidence for reasoning. (iii) Comprehensive experiments on LoCoMo demonstrate that QEMem achieves 91.43% LLM-judge accuracy, outperforming the strongest evaluated baseline, MemR 3, by 7.6 percentage points and NEMORI by 10.7 points under an identical memory store. 

2 Related Work 

Our work connects long-term agent memory (Park et al., 2023; Hu et al., 2025) with retrieval and evidence reasoning, focusing on how stored infor-mation is assembled into query-specific evidence. 

Long-Term Memory for LLM Agents. 

MemGPT (Packer et al., 2024), Memory-Bank (Zhong et al., 2024), and MemoryOS (Kang et al., 2025) support persistent storage and hierar-chical memory management. Mem0 (Chhikara et al., 2025) extracts reusable facts, while A-Mem (Xu et al., 2025) and Zep (Rasmussen et al., 2025) organize interconnected knowledge. LightMem (Fang et al., 2025), SimpleMem (Liu et al., 2026), and MemGAS (Xu et al., 2026) improve consolidation and multi-granular access. NEMORI (Nan et al., 2025), AgeMem (Yu et al., 2026), and MemAgent (Yu et al., 2025) further explore adaptive retention and memory operations. These advances improve memory storage and access, while QEMem focuses on assembling complementary evidence for a given query. 

Retrieval and Evidence Reasoning. Retrieval-augmented generation (Lewis et al., 2020) accesses external knowledge through dense and lexical re-trieval (Karpukhin et al., 2020; Robertson and Zaragoza, 2009), although additional context does not necessarily improve evidence utilization (Liu et al., 2024). GraphRAG (Edge et al., 2024) and LightRAG (Guo et al., 2025) exploit struc-tured knowledge, while ReAct (Yao et al., 2022), Self-RAG (Asai et al., 2024), and Search-R1 (Jin et al., 2025) enable adaptive information acqui-sition. For conversational memory, MemR 3 (Du et al., 2025) combines retrieval and reflection, RE-Mem (Shu et al., 2026) supports graph-guided episodic reasoning, and MRAgent (Ji et al., 2026) and E-mem (Wang et al., 2026) explore memory reconstruction. As conversational evidence spans sessions, temporal contexts, and abstraction lev-els (Maharana et al., 2024; Jia et al., 2025), QE-Mem explicitly organizes retrieved fragments into source-grounded evidence sets and refines them to address missing information. Further discussion appears in Appendix A.

4 Method 

We propose QEMem , a framework that refor-mulates long-term memory utilization as query-conditioned evidence-set construction, as illus-trated in Figure 1. Rather than directly optimiz-ing the conceptual objective in Eq. (1), QEMem progressively constructs an evidence set through three complementary mechanisms: query-guided evidence discovery , source-grounded evidence reifi-cation , and sufficiency-guided evidence refinement .As illustrated in Figure 1, these mechanisms trans-form heterogeneous memory representations into complementary, source-grounded evidence that col-lectively supports the query, without modifying the underlying memory formation process. 

4.1 Query-Guided Evidence Discovery 

Constructing a sufficient evidence set requires re-covering complementary information that may be distributed across memory representations and in-teraction sessions. Conventional relevance-based retrieval ranks individual memories independently, potentially overlooking evidence whose contribu-tion becomes apparent only when combined with other facts. QEMem therefore conditions evidence discovery on the information requirements of the query rather than its surface-level semantic similar-ity alone.

5 Experiments 

Experimental setup. We compare QEMem with seven baselines on 1,540 LoCoMo questions (Ma-harana et al., 2024), using Haiku 4.5 and Opus 4.5. Details appear in Appendix B.1. 

5.1 RQ1: Overall Performance 

QEMem achieves the highest overall accuracy (Table 1): 89.61% with Haiku 4.5 and 91.43% 

with Opus 4.5, surpassing the strongest baseline, MemR 3, by 9.16 and 7.60 percentage points, re-spectively. Both gains are significant under paired evaluation ( p < 10 −5; 95% CIs: [7.1, 11.2] and [5.8, 9.3] points). Gains are pronounced on multi-hop questions (+8.16 and +10.29 points over Sim-pleMem) and temporal reasoning (+6.23 and +4.36 points over MemR 3). These improvements are con-sistent with more effective recovery and integration of complementary evidence across memory records. QEMem leads all four categories under Haiku and three under Opus, where open-domain reasoning is the only exception. Performance across back-bones. Switching from Opus to Haiku reduces QEMem’s accuracy by only 1.82 points, compared with 4.22 for NEMORI and 3.38 for MemR 3. QE-Mem with Haiku outperforms every baseline us-ing Opus, supporting its effectiveness across both backbones. Its gains over NEMORI under an iden-tical memory store highlight the value of improving query-time memory utilization.

5.5 RQ5: Reasoning over Complex and Distributed Evidence 

Table 4 evaluates questions requiring multiple sup-porting turns, cross-session evidence, and cross-session list/count aggregation. QEMem achieves the highest accuracy in every reported group under both backbones. On questions with at least three supporting turns, it reaches 81.0% accuracy with Haiku and 82.1% with Opus, exceeding the strongest included baseline by 6.0 and 8.2 percent-age points, respectively. 6 Conclusion 

We present QEMem, a framework for addressing the relevance–sufficiency gap in long-term agent memory through query-specific evidence construc-tion. QEMem combines multi-granular evidence discovery, source-grounded ViewCards, and tar-geted refinement over existing memory stores. Ex-periments on LoCoMo show improved answer ac-curacy and evidence coverage across two LLM backbones, with strong performance on questions requiring cross-session evidence integration. These results highlight query-time evidence construction as an effective approach to improving memory uti-lization while making supporting sources explicit. 8Limitations 

Our evaluation focuses on LoCoMo and two Claude backbones; broader validation across bench-marks and model families remains future work. Ev-idence coverage relies on benchmark annotations, while grounding is assessed through automated checks and sampled LLM-based audits. QEMem also trades additional query-time computation for structured evidence and targeted refinement, moti-vating further work on reducing this overhead while preserving its benefits.

11 Appendix A Related Work 

A.1 Long-Term Memory for LLM Agents 

Recent advances in large language models have en-abled increasingly capable agentic systems that per-form complex tasks through reasoning, interaction, and collaboration (Chang et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2025, 2026c; Li et al., 2026). Multi-agent frameworks further coordinate special-ized agents to improve problem solving and deci-sion making (Yang et al., 2026a), with applications spanning code generation (Ping et al., 2026b,c), ranking and search (Chen et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026), and reinforcement learning for agentic rea-soning (Yang et al., 2026d; Zhang et al., 2026; Ping et al., 2025). As these systems evolve toward longer-horizon and more autonomous interactions, their effectiveness increasingly depends on the abil-ity to accumulate experience, preserve relevant context, and reuse previously acquired informa-tion. This growing need motivates long-term mem-ory as a fundamental component of LLM-based agents (Park et al., 2023; Hu et al., 2025). Existing research has explored diverse mecha-nisms for persistent memory storage, organization, and retrieval in LLM agents (Du, 2026; Qin et al., 2026; Ping et al., 2026a). Early approaches pri-marily address how conversational histories can be retained and accessed over time. MemGPT (Packer et al., 2024) introduces hierarchical memory man-agement inspired by operating systems, while MemoryBank (Zhong et al., 2024) maintains per-sistent user-related information for personalized interactions. MemoryOS (Kang et al., 2025) fur-ther organizes memory into multiple components to support long-term conversational continuity. Subsequent research explores how memory rep-resentations can become more compact, structured, and informative. Mem0 (Chhikara et al., 2025) extracts persistent facts from dialogue, while A-Mem (Xu et al., 2025) develops interconnected memory notes that evolve as new experiences ar-rive. Zep (Rasmussen et al., 2025) organizes conversational knowledge through temporal graph structures, supporting relationships and updates across events. LightMem (Fang et al., 2025) em-phasizes efficient memory consolidation through a lightweight architecture, whereas SimpleMem (Liu et al., 2026) constructs self-contained memo-ries with complementary retrieval views. Mem-GAS (Xu et al., 2026) further organizes memories at multiple granularities and adaptively selects re-trieval representations based on query characteris-tics. Collectively, these methods demonstrate the importance of memory organization and consolida-tion for effective long-term information access. Beyond memory representation, recent sys-tems increasingly adapt memory operations to the agent’s changing information needs. NEMORI (Nan et al., 2025) connects memory formation with predictability and prediction er-ror, while AgeMem (Yu et al., 2026) and MemA-gent (Yu et al., 2025) explore learned memory-management behaviors. These developments im-prove how agents retain, update, and retrieve past information. However, the quality of stored mem-ories and their individual relevance to a query do not necessarily determine whether the retrieved in-formation collectively supports a complete answer. QEMem addresses this complementary problem at query time, treating the construction of a suffi-cient, source-grounded evidence set as the central objective rather than introducing another memory storage architecture. 

A.2 Retrieval and Evidence Reasoning 

Effective memory utilization is closely related to retrieval-augmented generation and long-context reasoning. Retrieval-augmented generation (Lewis et al., 2020) provides external information to a language model, while dense retrieval (Karpukhin et al., 2020) and lexical retrieval (Robertson and Zaragoza, 2009) offer complementary mechanisms for identifying relevant content. Alternatively, long-context architectures allow models to process larger amounts of information directly (Beltagy et al., 2020; Zaheer et al., 2020; Dai et al., 2019). Nev-ertheless, access to additional context does not guarantee effective evidence utilization, as illus-trated by the lost-in-the-middle phenomenon (Liu et al., 2024). Methods such as LLMLingua (Jiang et al., 2023a) and ReadAgent (Lee et al., 2024) reduce context demands through compression or structured reading, while GraphRAG (Edge et al., 2024) and LightRAG (Guo et al., 2025) introduce graph-based organization to facilitate information retrieval and integration. Joren et al.Joren et al. (2025) distinguish insufficient context from failures to use available information in RAG. CRAG (Yan et al., 2024) evaluates retrieval quality to guide corrective retrieval actions. Complementary work examines source attribution: RARR (Gao et al., 12 2023a) retrieves evidence to revise unsupported generated content, while ALCE (Gao et al., 2023b) evaluates answer correctness and citation quality. QEMem brings these concerns to long-term agent memory by constructing source-linked evidence sets and refining them to address unresolved infor-mation needs before answering. These approaches improve information accessibility, but retrieving relevant content remains distinct from establishing whether the available evidence is sufficient for a particular question. A complementary direction incorporates rea-soning and feedback into retrieval decisions. Re-Act (Yao et al., 2022) interleaves reasoning with external actions, while Self-RAG (Asai et al., 2024) and active retrieval (Jiang et al., 2023b) allow re-trieval behavior to respond to evolving genera-tion needs. More recently, Search-R1 (Jin et al., 2025) explores reinforcement learning for multi-step search and reasoning. In long-term conversa-tional memory, MemR 3 (Du et al., 2025) coordi-nates retrieval, reflection, and answering through an iterative reasoning process. REMem (Shu et al., 2026) combines episodic memory graphs with agentic retrieval to support recollection and rea-soning over past events, while MRAgent (Ji et al., 2026) employs associative graph structures and ac-tive memory reconstruction. E-mem (Wang et al., 2026) further investigates episodic context recon-struction through multi-agent reasoning within re-trieved memory segments. Together, these ap-proaches move beyond single-pass retrieval toward more adaptive and context-aware memory reason-ing. Despite this progress, long-term conversational histories present a particular challenge: relevant ev-idence may be distributed across different sessions, represented at different levels of abstraction, or em-bedded within memories that contain only part of the information required to answer a query (Maha-rana et al., 2024; Jia et al., 2025). Consequently, retrieving individually relevant memories does not necessarily produce a collectively sufficient evi-dence set. Existing approaches emphasize mem-ory representation, retrieval control, or iterative reasoning, whereas QEMem explicitly centers on 

query-specific evidence-set construction . It com-bines query-guided evidence discovery with source-grounded evidence reification, making supporting claims and their provenance explicit, and uses suf-ficiency feedback to refine the evidence set when necessary. This perspective shifts the objective from retrieving relevant memory units to assem-bling complementary, traceable evidence that col-lectively supports the information requirements of the query. 

B Experiment 

B.1 Experimental Setup Dataset. We evaluate QEMem on LoCoMo , a benchmark for long-term conversational memory comprising 10 extended, multi-session conversa-tions. Following the standard evaluation protocol, we consider 1,540 questions across four categories: Multi-hop (282), Temporal (321), Open-domain (96), and Single-hop (841), excluding adversarial questions. LoCoMo also provides annotated sup-porting dialogue turns, which we use exclusively for post-hoc evidence analysis and never expose during inference. 

Baselines. We compare QEMem against seven representative memory systems: NEMORI ,

Mem0 , A-Mem , LightMem , SimpleMem ,

MemR 3, and MemGAS . These methods cover episodic and semantic memory organization, mem-ory extraction and consolidation, compression-based memory management, and iterative or struc-tured memory retrieval. We use their official imple-mentations or adaptations of the released code, fol-lowing the recommended LoCoMo configurations whenever applicable. All baselines are evaluated on the same questions under consistent backbone and evaluation settings. In particular, QEMem and NEMORI share an identical preconstructed mem-ory store, enabling a controlled comparison of their query-time memory utilization mechanisms. 

Implementation Details. We evaluate two LLM backbones, Claude Haiku 4.5 and Claude Opus 4.5 , accessed through Amazon Bedrock. Memory construction is performed once using Opus 4.5, and the resulting memories are reused across both back-bone settings. QEMem operates on NEMORI’s episodic, semantic, and original dialogue memo-ries, employing dense retrieval and BM25 with reciprocal rank fusion for multi-granular evidence discovery. We use Qwen3-Embedding-4B (Zhang et al., 2025) as the embedding model for QEMem and compatible baselines, retaining the original em-bedding models when required by baseline-specific mechanisms. All LLM inference uses a tempera-ture of zero. Following the NEMORI evaluation protocol, we report LLM-judge accuracy as the pri-13 mary metric, using a fixed Opus 4.5 judge and the same evaluation prompt across all methods. We additionally conduct paired statistical comparisons and evidence-level analyses using existing predic-tions and execution traces, without exposing refer-ence answers or supporting-evidence annotations during inference. 

B.2 Additional Evidence Sufficiency Analysis 

To examine whether the relevance–sufficiency gap persists across answer models, we extend the RQ2 analysis to Claude Haiku 4.5, as summarized in Ta-ble 5. The results closely reproduce the evidence-recovery patterns observed with Opus 4.5. QE-Mem increases complete retrieval coverage from 68.8% to 93.5%, while complete source visibility rises from 16.2% to 91.6%. Moreover, ViewCard sources cover all annotated supporting turns for 83.3% of questions, including direct turn citations and provenance links through cited episodes. The improvement is particularly substantial for multi-hop questions, where complete source visibility increases from 1.8% to 72.8%. More importantly, the relationship between evidence availability and answer accuracy remains consistent across back-bones. When NEMORI retrieves records contain-ing all supporting evidence, QEMem provides a modest 2.1-point improvement. In contrast, the gains increase to 22.0 and 47.5 percentage points when baseline retrieval coverage is partial or miss-ing, respectively. Although these two groups rep-resent only 31.2% of questions, they account for 89.1% of QEMem’s net accuracy improvement. To-gether with the Opus results, these findings suggest that incomplete evidence recovery is a persistent bottleneck across model capabilities, and that con-structing query-specific evidence sets is especially beneficial when conventional retrieval fails to re-cover the necessary supporting information. 

Coverage computation. Visibility is computed from the final turn IDs used to assemble the answer prompt, including card-derived source context. Card-source coverage uses resolvable turn and episode citations regardless of quotation-verification status. Semantic-memory provenance is excluded from these coverage metrics. Turn IDs sharing the same timestamp, speaker role, and con-tent are treated as aliases. 

B.3 Additional Analysis of Source Grounding and Evidential Reliability Claim–Source Semantic Consistency. While quotation fidelity establishes whether cited text can be recovered from the referenced memory records, it does not establish whether the corresponding evidence claims are logically supported. To eval-uate this distinction, we conduct an independent claim–source consistency audit over 288 stratified samples (144 ViewCards per backbone), sampled across question categories and provenance types. An Opus 4.5 judge evaluates each claim using only its cited source content and timestamps, without access to the question, gold answer, or answer-correctness labels. As shown in Table 6, 97.2% of Haiku-generated claims and 98.6% of Opus-generated claims are fully or partially supported by their cited evidence. Under the stricter criterion of complete semantic entailment, the correspond-ing population-weighted estimates are 59.6% (95% CI: [49.5, 70.2]) and 67.0% (95% CI: [57.3, 76.5]). Unsupported or contradicted claims account for only 1.1% and 1.3%, respectively, under population weighting. The distinction between full and par-tial support is important: many partially supported claims correctly recover the central fact but intro-duce additional details not explicitly established by the source, particularly temporal or entity-level attributions. Thus, high quotation fidelity should be interpreted as evidence traceability rather than a guarantee of complete factual entailment. 

Temporal Grounding Reliability. We further au-dit temporal grounding by comparing ViewCard time fields against mechanically checkable tempo-ral relations derived from their cited sources and conversation timestamps. As summarized in Ta-ble 7, 93.9% of Haiku-generated and 96.1% of Opus-generated time fields agree with the corre-sponding deterministic verification rules. Impor-tantly, these results are not solely attributable to the pipeline’s deterministic time normalizer: when restricting the evaluation to time fields originally produced by the LLM, consistency remains 93.3% and 95.7%, respectively. However, temporal nor-malization remains less reliable for particular rel-ative expressions. For instance, consistency on “yesterday” or “last night” is 62.4% for Haiku and 80.7% for Opus, while references to “last year” achieve 60.9% and 75.4%. Failure Modes and Limitations. The audit re-veals several limitations of source-grounded evi-dence construction. First, a small fraction of Haiku-generated cards (1.6%) contain unresolved prove-nance references, primarily because the model refers to evidence from previous refinement rounds without providing valid source identifiers; no such failures are observed for Opus. Second, citations to episodic memories frequently reproduce text from the episode summaries rather than verbatim orig-inal dialogue. Such citations remain traceable to memory records, but their fidelity to the underly-ing conversations cannot be established through quotation matching alone. Third, semantic incon-sistencies most commonly arise from over-specific temporal claims, incorrect attribution of informa-tion to speakers or events, and the merging of facts from different sources. Finally, the seman-tic audit relies on a limited stratified sample and a single LLM judge, while the temporal audit cov-ers only mechanically interpretable expressions. Together, these observations clarify the scope of QEMem’s grounding guarantees: its evidence rep-resentations provide explicit and verifiable prove-nance, but source traceability and quotation fidelity do not imply complete semantic correctness.

B.7 Answer-Level Groundedness and Faithfulness Evaluation Protocol. While RQ4 evaluates the provenance and fidelity of intermediate evidence representations, we further investigate whether these properties extend to the final generated an-swers. We conduct an answer-level grounding au-dit using 240 stratified LoCoMo questions and the existing predictions from NEMORI, Simple-Mem, MemR 3, and QEMem under Claude Opus 4.5. Questions are sampled across reasoning cat-egories, evidence complexity, and answer correct-ness, with sampling weights applied to estimate population-level results. For each question, we construct a shared verification context from the original conversations, including annotated sup-porting turns, neighboring dialogue context, and additional passages retrieved through BM25. All methods are evaluated against the same source evi-dence. An independent Opus 4.5 judge examines each answer without access to method identity, ref-erence answers, or existing correctness labels. Fac-tual claims are classified as supported, unsupported, contradicted, or unverifiable. Claims initially lack-ing evidence undergo an additional retrieval pass over the original conversation before final classi-fication. We distinguish claims constituting the final answer from auxiliary explanatory claims and report abstentions separately. 

Answer-Level Groundedness. Figure 7 and Ta-ble 12 summarize the results. QEMem achieves a fully grounded answer rate of 77.7%, outper-forming NEMORI (62.2%) and MemR 3 (66.4%) by 15.5 and 11.3 percentage points, respectively. Both improvements are statistically significant un-der paired bootstrap analysis, with 95% confidence intervals of [7.2, 24.2] and [2.7, 20.1] percent-age points. Moreover, the proportion of answers that are both correct and fully grounded increases to 73.2%, compared with 55.4% for NEMORI and 58.9% for MemR 3. QEMem also produces substantially fewer answers containing source-contradicting assertions (4.6%) than NEMORI (14.3%) and MemR 3 (17.7%), while maintaining an abstention rate of only 0.7%. SimpleMem ex-hibits a slightly higher fully grounded rate (82.3%), although its difference from QEMem is not sta-tistically significant, and achieves a comparable correct-and-grounded rate (73.6%). These findings suggest that QEMem maintains strong answer-level grounding alongside its accuracy improvements, particularly relative to methods that generate more extensive explanatory responses. 

The Role of Answer Verbosity. A closer exam-ination reveals that the observed improvements in full-output groundedness are partly associated with differences in answer verbosity. NEMORI and MemR 3 generate an average of 4.90 and 5.75 factual claims per answer, respectively, compared with 2.73 for QEMem and 2.38 for SimpleMem. Their additional explanatory claims frequently in-troduce temporal inaccuracies, incorrect entity at-tributions, or unsupported details. Consequently, when evaluation is restricted to core claims consti-tuting the final answer, the groundedness gap nar-rows substantially: QEMem achieves 79.3%, com-pared with 76.7% for NEMORI, 75.8% for MemR 3,and 82.3% for SimpleMem. These differences are not statistically significant. Similarly, claim-level support rates are broadly comparable across meth-ods, ranging from 89.6% to 93.3%. Therefore, the evidence does not establish that individual QEMem claims are intrinsically more reliable. Instead, QE-Mem achieves stronger full-output grounding than the more verbose baselines by limiting unsupported auxiliary assertions while preserving high answer accuracy.

Cross-Backbone Evidence. We additionally evaluate existing Haiku 4.5 answers on a smaller stratified subset of 68 questions. QEMem achieves a fully grounded rate of 74.4%, compared with 54.0% for NEMORI, 59.9% for SimpleMem, and 51.4% for MemR 3. Relative to NEMORI, the im-provement is 20.4 percentage points (95% CI: [8.4, 34.5]). The corresponding point estimates against SimpleMem and MemR 3 are also positive, but their question-level confidence intervals include zero. Furthermore, QEMem obtains a correct-and-fully-grounded rate of 74.0%, compared with 44.2%, 56.2%, and 47.7%, respectively. These results provide directional support for the findings under Opus 4.5, although the limited sample size prevents strong conclusions about cross-backbone consis-tency. 

Reliability and Limitations. The grounding au-dit has several limitations. First, although all meth-ods share the same original-dialogue verification context, the evidence packs may still omit relevant passages. Additional claim-specific retrieval mit-igates this issue but cannot guarantee exhaustive source verification. Second, grounding judgments are produced by a single LLM judge and are sen-23 sitive to the interpretation of temporal relations, inferred facts, and question premises. In a stabil-ity check, 35 of 40 answer-level classifications re-mained unchanged after reordering the verification context. Manual inspection of 29 disputed cases identified nine erroneous judgments, indicating that the resulting estimates should be interpreted cau-tiously. Third, full-output groundedness depends on the number of factual claims: longer responses are more likely to contain at least one unsupported assertion. We therefore report core-only grounding, claim-level support, and answer length alongside the primary metrics. Finally, the main audit covers 240 stratified questions, while the Haiku analysis is limited to 68 questions. These results provide supplementary evidence that QEMem can produce well-grounded final answers, but do not establish a causal improvement in claim-level faithfulness attributable to evidence reification alone. 

B.8 Prompt Templates 

We provide the prompt templates used by QEMem for query planning, evidence construction, and an-swer generation. All prompts correspond to the final QEMem-v2.3-clean implementation and are issued as a single user message with temperature zero. Variables enclosed in braces are populated dy-namically at inference time. The illustrative dates are fictional and do not overlap with the evaluation data. 

Query-Guided Evidence Discovery. The query planner identifies the information requirements of the question and generates complementary re-trieval queries. Its structured output also deter-mines whether aggregation or temporal reasoning is required. 

> Query Planning Prompt
> You are the query planner of a long-term conversational memory system. The memory stores a long multi-session conversation between {speakers}. Sessions span {span}. Question: {question} Analyse what information is needed to answer the question and plan retrieval. Think about: - which person(s), objects, events and attributes the question is about (resolve "he/she/they" to names when obvious), - whether the answer needs several facts combined (multi-hop), facts aggregated over many sessions (lists, counts, "what activities/places/books..."), a date or time computation, or an inference about someone's preferences, personality or likely behaviour, - which intermediate facts must be found first (e.g. to answer "What did X do after Y?" first find when Y happened). Return ONLY a JSON object: {"need": "<one sentence describing the information need>", "answer_type": "<one of: date, duration, count, list, yes_no, entity, description, reason, inference>", "aggregate": <true if the answer may be spread over multiple sessions, else false>, "temporal": <true if a date/time/order must be determined, else false>, "queries": ["<4-6 short, diverse search queries: sub-questions for intermediate facts, paraphrases with likely conversational wording, and key entity/keyword queries>"] }

The original question is combined with up to six planned queries for retrieval across episodic memories, semantic memories, and original con-versation turns. The planner’s information need is subsequently provided to the evidence constructor. 

Source-Grounded Evidence Reification. The evidence constructor transforms retrieved memo-ries into source-grounded ViewCards and jointly assesses whether the current evidence is sufficient to answer the question. Importantly, the sufficiency assessment and follow-up queries are generated within the same evidence-construction call. 

> Evidence Construction Prompt
> You are the evidence constructor of a long-term conversational memory system. Your job is NOT to answer the question but to build compact, source-grounded evidence cards that make the facts needed to answer it explicit. Question: {question} Information need: {need} Candidate memories retrieved from the memory store (each has an ID; [E*] episode summaries, [S*] semantic facts, [T*] raw conversation turns with their session date): {pool} {previous}

24 Instructions: 1. Find every memory relevant to the question. For list/count/"what activities" questions collect ALL distinct instances across sessions; for multi-hop questions collect each intermediate fact. 2. For each relevant fact write a card. Prefer raw turns [T*] as sources because they are the original words; use [E*]/[S*] when no turn is available. 3. "quote" must be copied VERBATIM from the cited source (a short span, max ~30 words) - never paraphrase inside "quote". 4. "time": when the fact happened, resolved against the session date of the source: "yesterday" -> "11 March 2031" (absolute date); "last Friday"/"last weekend" -> "the Friday/weekend before 12 March 2031" (keep weekday-relative expressions relative to the session date, do not compute the calendar day); "last week" -> "the week before 12 March 2031"; "last year" -> "2030"; no relative expression -> the session date. Use "" if not time-relevant. 5. "relation": in a few words, how this card bears on the question (e.g. "answer candidate", "intermediate fact: date of Y", "supports inference: likes outdoors", "contradicts card 2"). 6. Do not invent facts. If the memories conflict, make cards for both and note it in "relation". 7. Then judge whether the cards suffice. If key information is missing, give up to 3 follow-up search queries aimed precisely at the missing facts. Return ONLY a JSON object: {"cards": [{"claim": "<fact relevant to the question, self-contained, with names>", "sources": ["T3"], "quote": "<verbatim span>", "time": "<absolute time or empty>", "relation": "<role>"}], "sufficient": <true/false>, "missing": "<what is still missing, or empty>", "follow_up_queries": ["..."] }

The candidate pool is formatted with explicit memory identifiers and source timestamps. Dur-ing refinement, only newly retrieved memories are supplied, together with a summary of previously constructed evidence cards. 

Candidate Memory Pool Format 

## Episodes [E1] (<session date>) <episode title>: <episode content> ... ## Semantic facts [S1] (learned in session of <session date>) <semantic fact> ... ## Conversation turns (grouped by session, chronological) -- session <session date> --[T1] <Speaker>: <original conversation turn> [T2] <Speaker>: <original conversation turn> ... -- session <session date> --[T3] <Speaker>: <original conversation turn> ... 

When the constructor reports insufficient evi-dence, up to three follow-up queries are used for another retrieval round. Previously retrieved items are excluded, and the newly constructed cards are merged into the existing evidence set. The addi-tional text supplied through the {previous} place-holder has the form: Evidence cards already constructed in the previous round followed by the existing claims and their source identifiers. 

Evidence-Grounded Answer Generation. The answer model receives the constructed ViewCards, their associated original source context, and the remaining retrieved memories. Unverified cards are explicitly marked, while temporal and aggregation instructions guide the final response. 

Answer Generation Prompt 

You are an intelligent memory assistant answering questions about a long-term conversation between {speakers}, using evidence retrieved from memory. # QUESTION {question} # EVIDENCE CARDS (constructed for this question; each claim is grounded in a verbatim quote from the cited source; cards marked UNVERIFIED could not be matched to their source and must be treated with caution) {cards} # CITED SOURCE CONTEXT (original conversation turns around the cited evidence, with session dates) {sources} # OTHER RETRIEVED MEMORIES (background; use them if the cards miss something) {pool} # INSTRUCTIONS 

25 1. Base the answer on the evidence. Prefer verified cards and original turns; use episode summaries and semantic facts to fill gaps. 2. Time. Dates shown are session dates (the day the conversation took place). Express event times the way people resolve them against the session date: - "yesterday", "today", "this morning", "two days ago" -> the absolute calendar date (e.g. said on 12 March 2031 "yesterday" -> 11 March 2031); - "last Friday", "last Sunday", "on the weekend" (a weekday-relative expression) -> "the Friday before 12 March 2031" (relative to the session date; do NOT convert it into a calendar day yourself); - "last week", "last month", "a few days ago" -> "the week before 4 April 2031", "the month before ..." , "a few days before ..."; - "last year", "next month" -> the year / month (e.g. said in 2031 "last year" -> 2030). If the event is reported without a relative expression, its time is the session date itself. When a card's time is of the form "the <weekday/week/weekend> before <date>", that is the resolved time: give it in exactly that form (episode summaries may contain mis-computed calendar days). 3. Lists / "what activities", "what books", "how many": aggregate ALL distinct items found across all sessions; count carefully and de-duplicate. 4. Questions about likely preferences, personality, opinions or hypotheticals ("Would X ...", "What might X ...", "Is X likely ..."): give the most plausible answer inferred from the evidence (start with "Probably yes"/"Probably no" for yes/no questions) rather than refusing. For questions about someone's traits or attributes, answer with a few short descriptive traits. 5. Always give the answer first. If the question's details (e.g. a date or name) do not exactly match the memories, answer with the memory that best matches the rest of the question -do not start with "there is no evidence". 6. Answer exactly what is asked; do not add extra facts, dates or explanations that are not asked for. 7. The final answer must be short and direct: a phrase, name, date, list or short sentence (ideally under 15 words). First think briefly step by step (max ~120 words) under "REASONING:", then give the answer on the last line exactly as: FINAL ANSWER: <answer>

Abstention Retry. If the initial answer matches the predefined abstention patterns, QEMem per-forms at most one additional answering call using the same evidence context, with the following in-struction appended to the original answer prompt. 

Abstention Retry Prompt 

# NOTE Your previous final answer was: "{prev}". A question usually refers to something that is in the conversation even when a detail in it (e.g. the year, month or a name) may be slightly wrong. Do not abstain: answer with the memory that best matches the question, as a short direct answer.
