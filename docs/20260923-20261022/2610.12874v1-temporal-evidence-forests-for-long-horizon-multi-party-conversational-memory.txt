## ABSTRACT 

Long-term memory has been widely adopted in LLM-based agents to support persistent interaction and personalized assistance. Long-term multi-party interac-tions, however, accumulate substantially more detailed information, with decisions repeatedly updated and states evolving across participants and interaction stages. Temporal structure is therefore crucial for tracing such memories: relevant in-formation often appears densely within short intervals, while transitions among different states are anchored by a strict temporal order. Existing temporally aware memory frameworks primarily store time as metadata or use temporal signals to guide retrieval over preconstructed memory structures, without using tempo-ral structure to both recover locally concentrated evidence and reconstruct its complete event progression. To address these limitations, we propose T-M EM ,a temporal-structure-based memory framework that preserves interactions and organizes query-relevant evidence through two temporal mechanisms. Temporal Evidence Discovery exploits the local concentration of related messages to recover overlooked evidence, while Temporal Memory Structuring models state transitions and supporting relations over time and compresses low-information spans within the resulting forest. Evidence Forest Linearization then serializes the structured evidence for answer generation. Experiments on two long-term multi-party mem-ory benchmarks demonstrate that T-M EM consistently outperforms competitive memory frameworks across LLMs, parameter settings, domains, and query types. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

An LLM agent operating over a long interaction history cannot keep every prior message in its active context. External-memory systems therefore preserve past interactions as passages, summaries, or structured records and retrieve a compact subset before generation (Lewis et al., 2020; Packer et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2026b; Latimer et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2026a). In long-term multi-party interactions, however, the challenge is not only scale. Parallel discussions are interleaved across speakers and stages, while the same issue may be repeatedly proposed, revised, rejected, or confirmed. Semantically similar messages can therefore describe incompatible states, whereas a decisive update may share few words with the eventual query. A useful memory context must identify the temporally coherent episode and expose the complete state evolution that determines the current answer. We identify two complementary temporal properties that remain poorly represented by existing memory frameworks. The first is temporal locality . Memories contributing to the same event often concentrate within a discussion episode, even when an individual message has little surface overlap with a later query. A retrieved message can thus serve not only as evidence but also as a temporal anchor for locating its surrounding episode. Simply increasing Top-k may recover additional evidence, but it also introduces unrelated episodes and competing topics. The question is therefore not only which messages are relevant, but also where the relevant interaction is temporally concentrated. The second property is temporal evolution , which determines how recovered evidence should be interpreted and presented. Within an episode, messages are not interchangeable observations: a proposal may be questioned, refined, approved, implemented, or replaced, while another message 1arXiv:2610.12874v1 [cs.CL] 9 Oct 2026 may explain or support a later decision. A similarity-ranked list can retrieve several messages about the same subject without revealing which one represents the current state or how they form an event trajectory. Correct answering therefore requires reconstructing the progression connecting these messages rather than treating them as independent evidence items. Evidence completeness alone is therefore insufficient. When provisional proposals, later revisions, and supporting explanations are presented as an unordered set, the answer model must reconstruct both their chronology and their functional roles from scratch. Earlier and superseded states may then compete with the final decision despite all required messages being present. Explicitly distinguishing state-changing updates from supporting elaborations instead converts isolated matches into an interpretable trajectory and makes the evolution leading to the answer directly observable. Existing agent-memory systems improve access through graph-based organization, persistent mem-ory networks, multi-granularity association, and selective consolidation (Gutiérrez et al., 2025; Rasmussen et al., 2025; Chhikara et al., 2025; Jiang et al., 2026). These approaches are generally centered on semantic association, entity structure, or pre-query consolidation. Although temporal information may be retained, it does not jointly serve as the primary basis for locating a relevant episode and reconstructing its internal state transitions. Consequently, even a relevant memory set may be presented as disconnected records or transformed facts, leaving the answer model to infer which messages belong to the same event and which state supersedes another. This limitation is particularly consequential in long-term group conversations, where sophisticated memory systems do not consistently outperform simple retrieval (Yang et al., 2026). Temporal locality and temporal evolution address complementary failure modes: the former determines where coherent evidence is concentrated, while the latter determines how the recovered messages should be interpreted. Both are needed to recover an interaction episode and expose its complete evolution for generation. We introduce T-M EM , a temporal-structure-aware memory framework for long-term multi-party interactions, built around these two objectives. Temporal Evidence Discovery exploits temporal locality: Seed Retrieval first obtains anchors, whose temporal distribution identifies concentrated, topic-specific interaction episodes. T-M EM then searches the original messages within these episodes to recover answer-critical evidence that global similarity ranking may overlook. Temporal Memory Structuring models temporal evolution by organizing the recovered messages into a query-conditioned evidence forest with chronologically constrained U PDATE and E XPAND relations. U PDATE captures state progression, whereas E XPAND captures answers, explanations, and supporting evidence. After these relations are established, low-information spans are compressed, and Evidence Forest Lineariza-tion presents the ordered messages and their relations to the answer model. The resulting context exposes the complete event progression rather than a flat collection of memories. We evaluate T-M EM on GroupMemBench (Yang et al., 2026) and EverMemBench (Hu et al., 2026). T-M EM achieves 63.09% and 62.68% overall accuracy on GroupMemBench with GPT-5.5 and Deepseek-V4-Flash, and 79.46% and 77.63% on EverMemBench, respectively. Across both benchmarks, T-M EM consistently outperforms flat retrieval and strong agent-memory baselines. Controlled comparisons support the importance of recovering temporally coherent episodes and explicitly presenting their state evolution. Our contributions are fourfold: • We introduce T-M EM , an agent-memory framework that treats temporal structure as a core representation for retrieving and organizing evidence in long-term multi-party interactions. • We propose Temporal Evidence Discovery, which exploits temporal locality to locate concentrated interaction episodes around retrieved anchors and recover answer-critical messages overlooked by global similarity ranking. • We develop Temporal Memory Structuring and Evidence Forest Linearization, which recon-struct chronologically grounded relations and present the complete query-conditioned event evolution to the answer model. • We conduct extensive experiments on GroupMemBench and EverMemBench, where T-MEM achieves the highest overall accuracy across two LLM backbones and outperforms the strongest evaluated baseline by 6.50–8.59 percentage points. 22 RELATED WORK 

Structured and Retrieval-Augmented Memories Retrieval-augmented memory systems select information from external stores to supplement the limited context of language models. Lexical and dense retrieval provide efficient access to relevant records, while structured approaches expose broader associations and support multi-step reasoning. RAPTOR (Sarthi et al., 2024) recursively summarizes documents into a retrieval tree, whereas GraphRAG (Edge et al., 2024) constructs entity graphs and community summaries for query-focused generation. HippoRAG (Gutiérrez et al., 2024) and HippoRAG2 (Gutiérrez et al., 2025) combine knowledge graphs with associative retrieval to connect related facts. Agent-memory systems further transform interaction histories into reusable units: MemGPT (Packer et al., 2023) manages information through hierarchical memory tiers and context swapping, while MemGAS (Xu et al., 2026b) constructs multi-granularity associations and adaptively selects the retrieval granularity. These approaches broaden the information and relations available at inference time, but mainly determine which memory units are relevant. Long-term conversations also require determining when information applies and how it changes across interactions, motivating temporally aware memory. 

Temporally Aware Memories Temporally aware memory systems incorporate temporal cues across the memory lifecycle. A-Mem (Xu et al., 2025) incrementally evolves interconnected notes as new experiences arrive, allowing its memory representation to adapt over time. Zep (Rasmussen et al., 2025) represents facts and their validity intervals in a temporal knowledge graph for time-aware retrieval. Hindsight (Latimer et al., 2026) organizes memories through temporal and entity-aware networks and combines semantic, lexical, graph-based, and temporal retrieval signals. StructMem (Xu et al., 2026a) temporally anchors event-level memories before periodically consolidating semantically related events into cross-event representations. PRISM (Peng et al., 2026) performs intent-aware retrieval and compression over a preconstructed memory graph, routing different query intents, including temporal queries, through typed relation paths. Although these systems incorporate time at different stages, it generally serves as an auxiliary memory attribute or a signal for navigating structures organized by other relations. Consequently, they do not explicitly organize retrieved memories into the complete progression of an event. Furthermore, they do not exploit temporal locality to recover answer-critical evidence concentrated around relevant interaction episodes. 

Memory in Long-Term Multi-Party Conversations GroupMemBench (Yang et al., 2026) and EverMemBench (Hu et al., 2026) extend long-term memory evaluation to multi-party and collabora-tive conversations. Unlike conventional long-term dialogues, these conversations interleave multiple speakers, roles, topics, and concurrently evolving decisions. Relevant evidence may therefore be distributed across participants and interaction stages, while lexically similar messages can describe different episodes or conflicting states. Addressing this setting requires more than retrieving indi-vidually relevant memories: the system must identify when the relevant interaction occurred and organize its messages according to their temporal and evidential relationships. T-M EM addresses these challenges by discovering temporally localized evidence and structuring the original messages into query-conditioned evidence forests, providing the answer model with a coherent representation of multi-party state evolution.

## 4 T-M EM 

4.1 OVERVIEW 

T-M EM leaves the stored conversation unchanged and builds a compact evidence view when a query arrives. The three stages in Figure 1 discover candidate evidence, structure state changes and support, and linearize the resulting forest. For memory M = {mi}Ni=1 and query q, the output is a forest 

Fq = ( Vq , Eq ) whose nodes are original messages. The answer model receives q and a serialization of this forest. It never sees the reference answer. Metadata-expanded Seed Retrieval first supplies coarse anchors, and Temporal Evidence Discovery searches the corresponding episodes to recover memories that exhibit temporal locality with these anchors. Temporal Memory Structuring then links state-changing and supporting relations under strict chronological constraints and compresses low-information spans within the resulting forest. Evidence Forest Linearization converts this structure into the answer context. 4.2 TEMPORAL EVIDENCE DISCOVERY 

Group-chat events often unfold within temporally concentrated episodes containing proposals, confir-mations, and subsequent updates. We first retrieve query-aligned seed messages using a lightweight retriever (e.g., BM25), representing each message by its text together with available channel, phase, topic, author, and role metadata. The top-k messages form the coarse anchor set Rk(q). Beyond serving as direct evidence, these seeds provide temporal anchors for locating answer-critical messages that may have low global relevance. We therefore use their temporal distribution to identify candidate evidence intervals within each topic and inspect the original conversations in those intervals. 

Topic-wise temporal clustering. For each topic θ represented in Rk(q), we define the topic-specific seed set as Aθ = {mi ∈ Rk(q) : topic i = θ}.. We group the messages in Aθ into temporal clusters according to the proximity of their timestamps (e.g., DBSCAN or agglomerative clustering). ## 5 EXPERIMENTS 

5.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Datasets and Metrics. We evaluate on GroupMemBench (Yang et al., 2026) and EverMem-Bench (Hu et al., 2026), following their official data and evaluation protocols. Both benchmarks test long-term memory in multi-party conversations. We report answer accuracy as the primary metric. 

Baselines. We compare T-M EM with a diverse set of strong baselines, including BM25, Qwen3.7-text-embedding (Zhang et al., 2025), A-Mem (Xu et al., 2025), HippoRAG2 (Gutiérrez et al., 2025), Hindsight (Latimer et al., 2026), StructMem (Xu et al., 2026a), and MemGAS (Xu et al., 2026b). These baselines span conventional lexical and dense retrieval, retrieval-augmented memory, and recent structured agent-memory systems, providing broad coverage of representative approaches to long-term memory. Detailed baseline configurations are provided in Appendices B and C. 

Implementation Details. For Temporal Evidence Discovery, we use BM25 to retrieve k = 36 

coarse anchors. We instantiate the temporal clustering module with DBSCAN, clustering topic-specific anchor timestamps using a temporal radius of ϵ = 12 hours and MinPts = 2 . In Eq. 2, we set the span floor to δ = 2 hours and the density threshold to μ = 0 .25 anchors per hour; each accepted interval is padded by 12 hours on both sides. Messages within these intervals are ranked using qwen3.7-text-embedding with 2,048-dimensional vectors, and the Top-100 candidates are passed to Evidence Utility Selection. Evidence Utility Selection and Temporal Memory Structuring both use Deepseek-V4-Flash with thinking disabled, with temperatures of 0 and 0.2, respectively. The evidence context is capped at B = 8192 tokens. We separately evaluate GPT-5.5 and Deepseek-V4-Flash as both the answer and judge models. GPT-5.5 uses reasoning_effort=medium with temperature unset, whereas Deepseek-V4-Flash uses thinking enabled and temperature 0.2. The answer and judge output limits are 8,192 and 4,096 tokens, respectively. Within each backbone, all methods use the same questions, answer and judge prompts, and decoding settings. Additional configurations and retry policies are provided in Appendix C. 5.2 EXP -1: M AIN EVALUATION

Fig-ure 5 also includes HippoRAG2, the strongest baseline under this backbone, as a reference. Restricting metadata produces a gradual rather than abrupt decline. Removing topic from evidence dis-covery reduces overall accuracy by only 0.80 points, while withholding topic and phase from structuring lowers it by 1.20 points. The larger combined reduction indicates that topic and phase provide useful cues for separating interleaved episodes and interpreting their stages, but they are not the basis of the framework’s advantage. Tempo-ral proximity still localizes candidate evidence, while message content and chronological constraints retain enough information to reconstruct most state transitions. For each of the 107 Knowledge Update questions, we anchor a synthetic chain near the original answer-bearing message. The chain begins from the original state, introduces one to three successive revisions, and ends with a rollback that restores the original state. GPT-5.6 Sol generates each chain from the question-specific original state. We require the injected event content to remain independent of and non-duplicative with all other information in the source conversation while preserving a logically consistent progression from revision to rollback. All injected messages follow the source metadata schema and remain close to the original evidence in time. ## B COMPARISON BASELINES 

We select baselines to cover distinct memory mechanisms rather than enumerate every available implementation. BM25 provides a transparent lexical reference, while Embedding provides a dense semantic retrieval baseline. We additionally include representative agent-memory systems: 

A-Mem for dynamically linked and evolved memory notes, HippoRAG2 for graph-based associative retrieval, Hindsight for memory organization through temporal- and entity-aware networks, com-bining semantic, lexical, graph-based, and temporal retrieval signals, StructMem for temporally anchored event-level memories that are periodically consolidated into semantically related cross-event representations, and MemGAS for multi-granularity memory association and selection (Xu et al., 2025; Gutiérrez et al., 2025; Latimer et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2026a;b). Together, these systems cover sparse and dense retrieval, query-independent memory transformation, graph organization, temporal-and entity-aware memory retrieval, temporally anchored event consolidation, and multi-granularity selection. This selection follows the diagnostic findings of both benchmarks. In GroupMemBench, sophisticated memory architectures do not consistently dominate simple lexical retrieval in long-term group conversations (Yang et al., 2026). EverMemBench likewise reports substantial gaps for existing memory services on multi-party attribution, temporal revision, and implicitly relevant memories (Hu et al., 2026). We therefore keep strong simple retrieval controls rather than treating system complexity as a proxy for quality. We do not include every service or near-duplicate memory stack evaluated by the benchmark papers. In particular, systems whose public interface depends on a fixed user-profile schema, omits reliable group or timestamp propagation, or changes the official evidence interface cannot be compared under the same retrieval–answer contract without confounding the memory mechanism with service-specific behavior. The retained set is intended to represent the principal design families while preserving a common question, answer-model, and judging protocol.

## D REPRODUCIBILITY 

D.1 STAGE -WISE EXECUTION 

The implementation separates coarse retrieval, temporal expansion, forest organization, compression, answer generation, and judging into explicit stages. All questions finish one stage before the next stage begins. Each stage writes append-only JSONL records containing the stable question key, configuration, selected message identifiers, model outputs, status, and errors. This design allows intermediate evidence sets and forests to be inspected independently of downstream accuracy. D.2 RESUME AND FAILURE HANDLING 

Each runner reconstructs the latest record for the tuple (domain, question_type, question_id) . Successful records are skipped on restart, while failed or incomplete records are retried. Document embeddings, query embeddings, temporal-window selections, and completed forest records are cached separately so that a transient model-service or cluster failure does not require repeating earlier stages. Structured LLM calls use bounded retries for timeouts, empty responses, and malformed JSON; persistent failures remain visible as error records rather than being silently omitted. ## E ADDITIONAL RESULTS 

E.1 EFFICIENCY MEASUREMENTS 

Table 6 evaluates efficiency primarily through total token consumption, which directly reflects the amount of model processing required by each method. Monetary cost is reported as a complementary indicator, since it also depends on the prices of the models used at different stages. Memory-processing tokens cover corpus construction or retrieval, whereas answer-and-judge tokens cover the downstream evaluation. For Embedding, the token count includes one encoding pass over the 120,000-message corpus and all 745 queries; because the encoder can be deployed locally, this processing does not incur an embedding API charge. BM25 requires no model-based memory processing. Compared with conventional retrieval-augmented memory methods, T-M EM consumes moderately more tokens: 31.58M in total, compared with 14.87M for Embedding and 5.66M for BM25. This additional computation supports temporal evidence recovery and structured memory organization. Nevertheless, these memory-processing stages use a comparatively inexpensive model, so the mone-tary difference remains limited: T-M EM costs USD 44.04, only USD 4.40 more than Embedding and USD 5.36 more than BM25. The contrast is larger for methods that use LLMs to construct or select global memory representations. MemGAS, StructMem, HippoRAG2, A-Mem, and Hindsight consume 41.10M, 133.49M, 135.04M, 393.19M, and 487.35M tokens, respectively, whereas T-M EM requires only 31.58M. Thus, T-M EM 

reduces total token consumption by 23.2%, 76.3%, 76.6%, 92.0%, and 93.5% relative to these five baselines. Its total cost is also lower, at USD 44.04 versus USD 46.84, USD 148.67, USD 65.50, USD 76.70, and USD 122.59, respectively. These results show that query-conditioned temporal processing avoids the high cost of globally constructing and repeatedly consolidating LLM-derived memories. Overall, T-M EM offers a more efficient balance: it adds only moderate processing over traditional retrieval, yet requires far fewer tokens than existing agent memory frameworks while retaining their structured-memory capability. Method limitations. First, Temporal Evidence Discovery relies on the initial Top-k retrieved messages as anchors for identifying temporally dense regions and triggering local evidence search. When the initial retrieval budget is small, the anchor set may contain too few relevant messages to form a reliable cluster, or may fail to cover the relevant episode altogether. In such cases, temporal expansion has limited ability to recover the missing evidence. Therefore, although temporal locality can improve retrieval beyond the initial ranking, its effectiveness still depends partly on the quality and coverage of the coarse anchors. Second, Temporal Memory Structuring uses two broad edge types, UPDATE and EXPAND, to approximately reconstruct the evolution of an event. This abstraction intentionally keeps the resulting memory structure simple, but the two relations may conflate semantically distinct discourse functions. For example, questioning, clarification, justification, and elaboration may all be represented as EXPAND, even though they play different roles in the underlying interaction. Similarly, different forms of revision or correction may be grouped under UPDATE. Consequently, the resulting forest captures the coarse progression and supporting structure of an event rather than a complete, fine-grained discourse representation. Finally, both benchmarks provide cleaner metadata and more controlled interaction histories than many deployed group-chat systems, so generalization to noisy real-world conversations requires further study. 

Analysis limitations. First, Exp-2 evaluates evidence recovery against manually audited canonical evidence and identified alternatives, but the set of valid alternatives may still be incomplete. Multi-hop questions can admit multiple valid reasoning chains, and not every semantically equivalent message or paraphrase can be annotated. Moreover, an LLM may answer correctly using a semantically similar message, parametric knowledge, or an unannotated paraphrase even when the canonical evidence is absent. The alternative-evidence audit reduces this mismatch but cannot guarantee exhaustive coverage of valid evidence. Thus, the evidence-recall results in Exp-2 should be interpreted as a diagnostic estimate of recoverable answer-supporting evidence rather than an exhaustive measure of all possible valid evidence. Second, the final evaluation on both benchmarks depends on LLM-based answer generation and judging. LLM outputs can exhibit stochastic variation, and the judge may also respond differently to semantically equivalent, partially correct, or underspecified answers. This variability in generation and evaluation can introduce noise into the reported accuracy and may cause small variations across repeated runs. Although we use consistent answer requirements, judging protocols, and decoding settings within controlled comparisons, these controls cannot completely eliminate model-induced evaluation variance. 29 I ANSWER AND JUDGE PROMPTS
