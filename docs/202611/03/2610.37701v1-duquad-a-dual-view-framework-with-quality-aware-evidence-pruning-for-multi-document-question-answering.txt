Abstract 

Large language models with retrieval-augmented generation systematically underuti-lize evidence in the middle of multi-document contexts, a phenomenon known as Lost in the Middle. Existing mitigation strategies, such as document reordering and attention steering, can reduce boundary bias but remain brittle in noisy settings, where inaccurate relevance estimation and spurious documents still obscure useful evidence in central positions. We propose DuQUAD, a multi-document QA framework that mitigates positional bias through dual-view reasoning and quality-aware evidence pruning. DuQUAD combines a Local Agent guided by a Structural Fusion Score with a Global Agent that reasons over the full document set, providing both targeted evidence recovery and broad contextual understanding. The framework further filters candidate evidence using explicit quality scoring and refines it at the sentence level to reduce noise. DuQUAD consistently outperforms strong Lost-in-the-Middle mitigation baselines, including hybrid retrieval and reranking, multi-agent reasoning, and context optimization methods, improving accuracy by 3.0% on average over the strongest competing baseline and gold evidence recall by 9.4%, while substantially reducing computational cost compared to existing multi-agent frameworks.

2 Related Work 

Positional Bias in Large Language Models 

LLMs exhibit strong positional biases on multi-document inputs, tending to over-attend to tokens near the input boundaries while under-utilizing information in intermediate positions (Liu et al., 2024; Hutter et al., 2025). This phenomenon, known as Lost in the Middle (LiTM), is attributed to attention dynamics such as attention sinks and recency effects (Xiao et al., 2024; Gu et al., 2025). To mitigate these effects, prior work has proposed attention calibration and sorting tech-niques (Peysakhovich and Lerer, 2023; Hsieh et al., 2024), as well as positional scaling and dimension-level bias correction (Yu et al., 2024). How-ever, these methods primarily target attention-level symptoms rather than how positional bias degrades evidence utilization in multi-document reasoning. 

Training-based Approaches to LiTM Mitigation 

Training-based methods address positional bias through fine-tuning or additional supervision (Liu et al., 2024; Han et al., 2024). Position-agnostic training and multi-step QA supervision encourage models to prioritize content relevance over abso-lute position (He et al., 2024; Pan et al., 2024), while full-context utilization training directly op-timizes for mid-context evidence use (An et al., 2024). Although effective, these approaches re-quire substantial model adaptation and cannot be applied to off-the-shelf models at inference time. 

Training-free Approaches to LiTM Mitigation 

Training-free methods mitigate positional bias at inference time without modifying model parame-ters (Wang et al., 2024). Document reordering and reranking place relevant passages at salient posi-tions prior to reasoning (Peysakhovich and Lerer, 2023; Wang et al., 2025), but do not address sup-pression during reasoning itself. Context compres-sion reduces input noise (Jiang et al., 2024; Verma, 2024), but risks discarding weak signals necessary for multi-hop reasoning. These methods do not explicitly model how positional bias suppresses evidence during reasoning, leaving mid-context ev-idence vulnerable to being overlooked. Multi-agent Reasoning for Multi-document QA Multi-agent reasoning extends single-agent LLMs by decomposing complex tasks into co-ordinated sub-problems, enabling role special-ization and iterative refinement through agent collaboration. Prior work has explored various paradigms, including debate-based reasoning (Du et al., 2024) and role-based decomposition (e.g., planner–solver–verifier) (Chen et al., 2025). Build-ing on these ideas, structured approaches such as Tree of Agents (Yu et al., 2025) and MAIN-RAG (Chang et al., 2025) organize reasoning and retrieval into hierarchical or multi-stage pipelines. However, these methods primarily focus on task decomposition and document selection, and do not explicitly account for positional bias, leaving mid-context evidence still under-utilized. 

3 Methodology 

We address reasoning failures in multi-document QA: despite accurate retrieval, standard RAG mod-els suffer from positional bias and the LiTM effect, where mid-context evidence is suppressed by noise. To reliably isolate gold evidence, we propose a hierarchical framework that integrates document prioritization, dual-view agent collaboration, and quality-aware evidence pruning, as illustrated in Figure 2. 

3.1 Structural Fusion Score (SFS) 

To identify gold documents under positional noise, we propose the Structural Fusion Score (SFS). While attention over multi-document inputs typi-cally exhibits a U-shaped bias, gold evidence forms a characteristic W-shaped pattern with sharp local spikes (Hsieh et al., 2024). SFS combines (1) se-mantic relevance, (2) structural inference, and (3) attention contrast. The first two capture query– document alignment, while attention contrast em-phasizes localized spikes robust to positional bias.

3.2 Dual-View Agent Collaboration 

Although SFS prioritizes relevant evidence, using only a filtered subset may lose global context and cross-document dependencies. To address this, we introduce a Dual-View Agent Collaboration frame-work that reasons from two complementary views. The Global Agent reasons over the complete retrieved document set, while the Local Agent focuses on a compact, noise-reduced subset re-ranked by SFS. This design provides mutual ro-bustness: the Local Agent recovers evidence sup-pressed by positional bias or noise, and the Global Agent compensates for information lost through filtering. Both agents generate cited answers in-dependently, which are aggregated into a unified reasoning log for position-resilient multi-document reasoning. We use k = 6 local documents based on a recall–accuracy trade-off analysis in Appendix D. 

3.3 Quality-Aware Evidence Pruning 

While Dual-View reasoning maximizes recall, ag-gregating verbose reasoning logs and long-form documents can cause context overload, increasing the risk of hallucination. To address this issue, we introduce a two-stage quality-aware pruning strat-egy that separates evidence selection from quality verification. 

Important Document Selection Each agent in-dependently identifies a set of important documents based on its own reasoning process, directly from its generated output. Specifically, each agent re-turns up to three document IDs as important docu-ments, together with its reasoning results, follow-ing a predefined JSON output format (Appendix F, Figure 7). Let DG and DL denote the sets of important documents selected by the Global Agent and the Local Agent, respectively. These sets are merged to form a candidate document pool as follows: 

Dimp = DG ∪ D L (4) As a result, up to six candidate documents are ob-tained. This stage serves as a coarse, recall-oriented filtering step that broadly preserves answer-relevant evidence which might otherwise be missed due to positional bias or contextual noise. 

LLM-Based Quality Scoring Each candidate document Di ∈ D imp is evaluated using DocScore, an LLM-based document reliability score defined in this work, where pi ∈ [0 , 1] estimates whether the question can be answered from the given docu-ment alone. DocScore is produced by a dedicated Score Agent with a fixed prompt, which returns a JSON-formatted confidence score together with an answer span (Appendix F, Figure 9). We retain only high-confidence documents using a threshold-based rule: 

Dkeep = {Di ∈ D imp | pi ≥ τ }. (5) We fix the threshold at τ = 0 .4 for all experi-ments. This value is selected based on the threshold sensitivity analysis in Appendix C (Table 5), where 

τ = 0 .4 is the highest threshold that preserves re-call while still enabling meaningful pruning. Lower thresholds admit more irrelevant or weakly relevant documents, whereas higher thresholds risk discard-ing gold evidence too early. For each retained document, we generate a document-specific provisional answer and select the top two sentences as supporting evidence based on word or keyword overlap between the provi-sional answer and each sentence in the document. This sentence-level evidence compression reduces the computational burden on the Judge Agent while preserving the key information that directly sup-ports final reasoning. 

3.4 Final Decision via Judge Agent 

The Judge Agent produces the final answer with a fixed prompt (Appendix F, Figure 8), jointly con-sidering (i) the reasoning histories of the Global and Local Agents, (ii) document-level confidence scores (DocScore), and (iii) sentence-level evi-dence spans extracted from retained documents. Rather than relying on a single score or isolated evidence, the Judge integrates these heterogeneous signals to make a consolidated decision. Formally, given the question Q, the reasoning traces HG and HL, the sentence-level evidence set 

S, and the associated DocScores p for documents in Dkeep , the final answer ˆy is generated as: 

ˆy = JudgeAgent( Q, HG, HL, S, p) (6) By aggregating complementary agent outputs and evidence signals, the Judge Agent avoids over-reliance on any single source and supports ro-bust answer generation. The complete DuQUAD pipeline is summarized in Algorithm 1.

5 Results 

5.1 Overall Performance 

Table 1 reports accuracy on NQ, MuSiQue, and HotpotQA, with EM and F1 scores additionally reported for multi-hop tasks. On NQ, the Vanilla LLM shows strong posi-tional sensitivity, with accuracy varying substan-tially across gold-document positions. Reordering and reranking baselines reduce this positional vari-ation, with the hybrid variants showing particularly stable performance across positions. Nevertheless, DuQUAD consistently achieves the highest accu-racy across all NQ settings under both backbones. Context optimization and multi-agent baselines also remain challenged by LiTM: FiM degrades substantially at intermediate positions, while ToA and MAIN-RAG remain below DuQUAD despite their more complex reasoning pipelines. These results suggest that stronger document ranking, repositioning, or reasoning decomposition alone does not fully address positional underutilization in multi-document reasoning. Overall, DuQUAD achieves the best accuracy across all 14 accuracy settings in Table 1. It improves over the strongest competing baseline in each setting by 3.0 percentage points on aver-age. The same trend holds for both Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, suggesting that the gains are not specific to a single backbone. On MuSiQue and HotpotQA, DuQUAD also achieves the best accuracy under both backbones. With Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, it improves over the strongest competing baseline by 2.5 percentage points on MuSiQue and 2.9 percentage points on HotpotQA. DuQUAD further achieves the highest F1 on both multi-hop benchmarks. The only metric-level exception under the Llama back-bone is HotpotQA EM, where Hybrid (RRF) + Rerank achieves 54.4%, compared with 52.6% for DuQUAD. This narrow EM advantage is con-sistent with HotpotQA’s relatively compact evi-dence setting, where strong document ordering can place the two gold paragraphs at salient po-sitions, while strict exact-match scoring may pe-nalize answers that are semantically correct but differ in surface form. With Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, DuQUAD achieves the highest accuracy, EM, and F1 on both multi-hop benchmarks. These results demonstrate that DuQUAD maintains robust per-formance across both controlled positional settings and multi-hop reasoning tasks. Paired bootstrap re-sampling further shows that DuQUAD’s gains are statistically significant in 35 out of 36 comparisons (p < 0.05 ), with detailed results in Appendix G.1. 5.2 Gold Evidence Recall and DocScore Stability 

We evaluate local document selection using gold evidence recall, defined as whether the gold docu-ment is included in the candidate set selected for the Local Agent. As shown in Figure 3, Atten-tion Contrast achieves the highest recall among single-signal approaches at 81.4%, surpassing Se-mantic Relevance with 69.0% and Structural Infer-ence with 75.4%. In contrast, the proposed Struc-tural Fusion Score (SFS) attains the highest overall recall of 90.8%, yielding a 4.0% improvement over the strongest multi-signal baseline that combines Structural Inference and Attention Contrast, and a 9.4% gain over the best single-signal method. These results indicate that integrating complemen-tary signals enables more robust identification of gold evidence across different document positions. DocScore further provides a stable quality sig-nal for evidence pruning. Across NQ-1/5/10/15/20, gold documents consistently receive higher Doc-Scores than non-gold documents and are selected as Important Documents more frequently under both Llama and Qwen backbones. Cross-model anal-ysis further shows 90.4% agreement on gold evi-dence selection, suggesting that DocScore captures a stable answerability signal rather than backbone-specific behavior. Detailed statistics are provided in Appendix L.

We analyze the cost-efficiency of DuQUAD with respect to end-to-end latency on NQ-15. Figure 5 shows the accuracy–latency trade-off across repre-sentative baselines. and 3.9 ×, respectively. 6 Conclusion 

We investigated the limitations of multi-document QA under positional bias, specifically the Lost in the Middle phenomenon. To address it, we pro-pose DuQUAD, a dual-view reasoning framework that pairs a Local Agent operating on a priori-tized evidence subset with a Global Agent that reasons over the full document set. Local se-lection is guided by the Structural Fusion Score, which integrates semantic relevance, structural in-ference, and attention contrast to robustly identify gold documents despite positional noise. Quality-aware pruning further removes low-confidence ev-idence before final reasoning, and together these components raise gold evidence recall to 90.8%. As a result, DuQUAD mitigates positional bias and achieves the best accuracy among the evalu-ated baselines across all benchmarks, while requir-ing substantially less computation than existing multi-agent frameworks. These results demonstrate that DuQUAD offers a favorable balance between accuracy and cost for robust reasoning in multi-document QA under positional bias. 

7 Limitations 

While DuQUAD effectively mitigates positional bias in multi-document QA settings, it also has several limitations. First, DuQUAD employs an explicit evidence pruning mechanism to suppress noisy or low-confidence documents. Although this improves robustness against positional noise, it may reduce contextual coverage when relevant ev-idence is weakly expressed or distributed across multiple documents, particularly for complex multi-hop questions. Second, DuQUAD relies on the availability of answer-bearing documents within the initial re-trieved set. Similar to other retrieval-augmented frameworks, its performance is bounded by re-trieval quality: if relevant documents are not re-trieved, the framework cannot recover missing evi-dence through internal reasoning alone. Third, even when answer-bearing evidence is correctly retrieved and scored, the final synthesis stage may still fail in complex multi-hop questions where the reasoning chain must track closely re-lated intermediate entities, as reflected in the quan-titative and qualitative analyses in Appendix K. Finally, our evaluation is limited to Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct; broader evaluation across model families, capabilities, and sizes would further assess the generalizability of DuQUAD. These limitations reflect inherent trade-offs in multi-document QA systems that balance contex-tual coverage, noise suppression, and reasoning stability.

(1) Vanilla LLM. Constructs the input context by directly concatenating one ground-truth doc-ument with 19 distractors without additional post-processing. 

(2) Reordering and Reranking. (i) SBERT Re-ordering (Reimers and Gurevych, 2019) is a dense retrieval baseline using Sentence-BERT. Docu-ments are rearranged in descending order of co-sine similarity between the query and document embeddings. (ii) BM25 Reordering (Crestani et al., 1998) is a sparse retrieval baseline utilizing lexi-cal overlap. Documents are re-ranked based on BM25 scores to prioritize exact term matches. (iii) OpenAI Reordering (OpenAI, 2024) reorders doc-uments based on semantic similarity using pro-prietary embeddings. (iv) Jina-Reranker (Wang et al., 2025) is a reranking baseline based on cross-encoder-style query–document interaction. Documents are rearranged based on cosine sim-ilarity between query and document embeddings. Natural Questions (NQ). The original NQ cor-pus (Kwiatkowski et al., 2019) contains 307,373 training examples derived from real Google search queries, where annotators mark long answers and short answers on Wikipedia pages. In our evalu-ation, we use the controlled positional bias pro-tocol of (Liu et al., 2024) rather than the origi-nal reading comprehension task. Each instance places one gold document at a designated posi-tion among 19 distractors within a 20-document sequence. Five positional splits are constructed for n ∈ { 1, 5, 10 , 15 , 20 }, with 1,000 examples per split, and all questions are single-hop. The five splits share the same document sets and differ only in the position of the gold document. 

MuSiQue. MuSiQue (Trivedi et al., 2022) is con-structed via bottom-up composition of single-hop questions drawn from five seed datasets into con-nected multi-hop chains. The dataset contains approximately 25K questions spanning 2–4 hops across six reasoning graph types. Each question en-forces that one reasoning step critically depends on another, and no single paragraph suffices for an in-dependent answer, preventing the disconnected rea-soning shortcuts observed in prior benchmarks. We use instances from the answerable subset, each with its original supporting and distractor paragraphs. 

HotpotQA. HotpotQA (Yang et al., 2018) con-tains 112,779 Wikipedia-based question–answer pairs annotated with sentence-level supporting facts. Each question requires 2-hop reasoning and is categorized as either bridge, where an intermedi-ate entity from one paragraph is needed to locate the answer in another, or comparison, which re-quires contrasting properties across two paragraphs. We evaluate in the distractor setting, where two gold paragraphs are presented alongside eight dis-tractors. F Reproducibility 

To ensure reproducibility, we release the exact prompts used for all LLM-based components. Rea-soning agent prompts, Judge prompts, and score estimation prompts are provided in Figures 7, 8, and 9, respectively, without modification. In par-ticular, the reasoning agent prompt in Figure 7 in-structs each agent to answer the question using only the provided documents and to return the top 3 important document IDs in a fixed JSON for-mat, which is later used to construct the candidate document pool. The score estimation prompt in Figure 9 defines the DocScore procedure: for each selected candidate document, the Score Agent esti-mates whether the question can be answered from that document alone and returns a JSON object containing a confidence score p ∈ [0 , 1] together with the best answer span or phrase. All experiments were conducted on a computing system equipped with NVIDIA L40S GPUs, an AMD EPYC 9654 CPU (96 cores), and 1 TB of system memory. We fix all decoding parameters across experiments and use deterministic decoding with a temperature of 0.0 unless otherwise spec-ified, enabling faithful replication of our results. All baseline experiments were conducted under the same generation setting with the temperature fixed to 0.0 to ensure reproducibility and fair comparison. The basic prompt templates (e.g., Vanilla LLM and reordering-based methods) strictly follow the con-figurations used in the Lost in the Middle paper 5.For baselines requiring additional prompt control or specialized templates, we prioritized the officially released GitHub implementations provided by the original authors. When an official implementation was not available, we faithfully reimplemented the prompts and inference procedures as described in the corresponding papers. 

Artifact use. All existing artifacts used in this work are used solely for research and evaluation purposes, consistent with their intended use. Structural inference and attention score computa-tion are performed using the same backbone model as the main reasoning process. This avoids intro-ducing separate scoring models and helps mitigate bias arising from reliance on specific model archi-tectures.

I Boundary Handling for Attention Contrast Computation 

When computing attention contrast, special han-dling is required at document boundary positions where symmetric neighbors do not exist. Instead of constructing a second-order central difference op-erator that assumes symmetric neighbors through padding or reflection, we adopt a one-sided fi-nite difference scheme at the document boundaries. Specifically, for the first and last documents, at-tention contrast is computed using the difference between the document and its nearest neighbor. This design reduces artificial smoothing effects in-troduced by padding-based boundary conditions while more faithfully preserving relative attention salience at the document edges. The one-sided difference scheme is widely used across numeri-cal analysis and signal processing, and in our ex-periments, it exhibits consistent and interpretable behavior for document-level contrast estimation.

To complement this aggregate analysis, we present three representative cases that illustrate how the Local and Global Agents interact: two cases in which one agent fails while the other recovers the correct answer, and one failure case in which both agents converge on the same incorrect reasoning. 

Case 1: Global Recovery under Local Error. 

For the question “who won the mens figure skat-ing in 2018 olympics” , the gold document is DOC 14, and the correct answer is Yuzuru Hanyu. The Global Agent correctly identifies the answer from the full document set by citing DOC 14. In contrast, the Local Agent predicts Nathan Chen, even though DOC 14 is included in its selected local subset. In-stead of relying on the answer-bearing statement, the Local branch is distracted by another document describing Nathan Chen’s technical achievements, which are salient but not sufficient to answer the question. During quality-aware pruning, DOC 14 receives a DocScore of 1.0, while the other candi-date documents receive 0.0. The Judge Agent there-fore rejects the Local Agent’s incorrect claim and produces the correct final answer, Yuzuru Hanyu. This case shows that even when the Local branch fails, the Global branch and subsequent pruning can prevent the error from propagating to the final decision. 

Case 2: Local Recovery under Global Uncer-tainty. We also observe cases in which the Global branch follows a more indirect reasoning path, whereas the Local branch recovers the answer more directly. For the question “who performed the half-time show at super bowl 51” , the Global Agent ini-tially states that the performer is unclear and refers to surrounding information that Adele had been discussed as a possible candidate. Although it even-tually reaches the correct answer by citing DOC 14, its reasoning does not immediately converge on the core answer-bearing evidence. In contrast, the Local Agent explicitly states that “Lady Gaga per-formed the halftime show at Super Bowl LI (which is Super Bowl 51) [DOC 14],” directly identifying the correct entity and clarifying the equivalence between Super Bowl LI and Super Bowl 51. At the final integration stage, this complementary be-havior allows the system to converge stably to the correct answer, Lady Gaga. This case shows that even when one branch becomes temporarily un-stable, the other branch can preserve the correct answer signal and support a stable final prediction.

L DocScore Statistics and Cross-Model Evidence Stability 

Table 11 summarizes the DocScore and Important Document statistics used in the main analysis. For both backbones, gold documents receive substan-tially higher DocScores than non-gold documents and are selected as Important Documents much more frequently. Under Llama, gold and non-gold documents receive mean DocScores of 0.647 and 0.324, respectively, with Important Document se-lection rates of 85.7% and 14.3%. Under Qwen, the same pattern holds: gold and non-gold docu-ments receive mean DocScores of 0.722 and 0.348, with selection rates of 83.0% and 17.0%, respec-tively. This consistent separation across backbones suggests that DocScore captures document-level answerability rather than simply reflecting posi-tional artifacts or model-specific scoring behavior. Since DocScore is produced by an LLM-based Score Agent, its evidence selection could in princi-ple reflect backbone-specific behavior rather than an intrinsic notion of evidence quality. To test this, we compare DocScore-based evidence selection across Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct under identical settings on Natural Ques-tions, averaged over NQ-1/5/10/15/20. The cross-model selection results are reported in Table 12. Our goal here is to measure cross-model agree-ment , not the absolute ability of either backbone to recover the gold document. Accordingly, when measuring gold evidence stability, we restrict the denominator to cases where at least one backbone selects the gold document, and report how often both do. Cases where neither backbone selects the gold document are excluded, as they reflect re-trieval difficulty rather than disagreement between backbones, and would otherwise conflate selection quality with cross-model consistency. 

Disagreement is concentrated on distractors. 

The selected document sets are not identical across backbones, but the disagreement is concentrated on distractors rather than gold evidence. When at least one backbone selects the gold document, both se-lect it in 90.4% of cases. In contrast, among cases where both backbones select the gold document, their additionally selected non-gold documents dif-fer 90.1% of the time. 

Implication. High agreement on gold evidence alongside divergence on distractors shows that cross-model consistency is not an artifact of identi-cal candidate pools: the two backbones converge on gold evidence while diverging on irrelevant con-text. DocScore therefore captures a stable answer-ability signal across the two evaluated backbones, supporting its use as a reliable quality signal for evidence pruning.
