Abstract 

Existing robot navigation methods primarily focus on iso-lated tasks, overlooking the need to accumulate knowledge and experience across repeated navigation episodes. Al-though recent lifelong navigation methods have explored cross-task knowledge accumulation, they mainly focus on scene knowledge while neglecting historical navigation ex-perience and its connection to scene evidence, limiting the ability to reflect on past mistakes and improve future de-cisions. To address this limitation, this paper presents 

RetroNav , a training-free self-evolving navigation frame-work that integrates navigation, memory accumulation, and failure reflection. Specifically, we develop a unified memory system that connects geometric and semantic scene knowl-edge with historical navigation experience, distinguishing objective observations from task-dependent interpretations. After each navigation attempt, an independent evaluator determines whether a retry is necessary. If so, a Retro-spection Agent revisits historical decisions using accumu-lated scene knowledge and navigation records, generating an evidence-grounded retry brief to guide the next attempt. RetroNav achieves state-of-the-art performance across five navigation benchmarks. Our method will be open-sourced at https://github.com/3dv-casia/RetroNav .

1. Introduction 

Most existing robot navigation research focuses on isolated navigation tasks. In practical applications, however, robots often operate in the same environment over long periods, re-peatedly performing navigation tasks and encountering fa-miliar places and objects. Each navigation episode, whether

Therefore, navigation systems should not only complete individual tasks but also accumu-late knowledge and experience to reduce repeated explo-ration and improve subsequent decisions. Recent studies have begun to investigate lifelong Navi-gation, exploring how robots can leverage historical infor-mation across tasks [11, 16, 19, 39, 46]. Existing meth-ods primarily focus on accumulating scene knowledge, such as geometric structures, semantic information, and visual observations, while largely overlooking navigation experi-ence, including how tasks were interpreted and decisions were made. However, navigation failures may arise not only from insufficient environmental knowledge but also from incorrect interpretations or decisions. For example, a robot may have already observed the correct target location but choose a wrong direction due to misinterpreting the instruc-tion. Simply retaining scene knowledge cannot reveal such decision errors, while reusing previous decisions may re-peat them. Therefore, effective lifelong navigation requires associating historical decisions with their underlying scene evidence, enabling navigation experience to be revisited and reused. We argue that effective lifelong navigation requires inte-grating scene knowledge with historical navigation experi-ence. Scene knowledge captures the geometric and seman-tic structure of the environment, providing reusable infor-mation for future navigation. Navigation experience records how previous tasks were interpreted and executed, enabling robots to revisit historical decisions and reflect on poten-tial errors. By associating navigation experience with scene evidence, robots can continuously improve their navigation behavior through knowledge accumulation and failure re-flection. 2. Related Work 

Lifelong navigation in persistent environments. Life-long navigation studies ordered tasks executed in a per-sistent scene, where later tasks can reuse information collected during earlier episodes. While R2R [2] and RxR [20] established visually grounded instruction follow-ing, IVLN/IR2R [19] explicitly evaluates navigation over ordered episodes. GOAT-Bench [16] extends this setting to multimodal goals. It also studies explicit and implicit scene memory. OVAL [30] uses past observations to find succes-sive open-vocabulary object goals. It guides exploration us-ing semantic cues, distance, and previous visits. EvoMem-Nav [11] reflects after each subtask to update its environ-mental priors. NavHarness [46] retains maps and task expe-rience for later tasks and recovery attempts. These methods improve navigation through persistent scene knowledge, continual policy learning, and experience reuse. RetroNav separates environmental evidence from task-dependent in-terpretations and decisions, while explicitly linking them 2for later inspection. This design supports evidence-based failure diagnosis and targeted retries through independent post-attempt evaluation and a structured Retry Brief. 

Scene memory construction. Scene-memory methods differ in the representations they retain and the mecha-nisms used to consolidate them. UniGoal [42] incremen-tally constructs a scene graph of objects and their spatial or semantic relations, and matches a goal graph against it to guide navigation, while structured representations con-nect open-vocabulary observations with navigable scene elements [45]. 3D-Mem [39] combines incremental 3D scene memory with informative visual observations, and hierarchical semantic–geometric maps explicitly bridge vi-sual and spatial representations [22]. Other systems retain fine-grained visual hierarchies and update memory through reflection [11], or compress long visual histories for effi-cient reuse [14]. These methods emphasize spatial orga-nization, visual fidelity, and selective retrieval. RetroNav complements them by separating environmental evidence from task-dependent judgment and linking observations, poses, transitions, and views to regions, places, and ob-jects. Historical interpretations and actions remain experi-ence records, so reflection can inspect past decisions with-out treating them as scene facts. 

Training-free agent navigation. Training-free naviga-tion uses frozen foundation models together with prompts, tools, or pretrained controllers, avoiding additional opti-mization of navigation-task parameters. TANGO [48] com-poses language-model reasoning with reusable embodied primitives for zero-shot open-world tasks. Open-Nav [31], AgenticNav [24], and HarnessVLN [5] organize percep-tion, planning, tool use, grounding, and recovery around multimodal language models. These approaches demon-strate strong zero-shot execution, but their primary focus is single-task control or the organization of local history. RetroNav targets their intersection with lifelong navigation: fixed model parameters, persistent scene-memory consoli-dation, independent post-attempt evaluation, and evidence-grounded retry reflection are combined into one closed loop.

3.1. Problem Setting and Overview 

We consider an agent performing a sequence of navigation tasks {T i}Ni=1 in a persistent scene. Each task Ti = ( gi, s 0

specifies a goal and an initial pose. The goal may be a lan-guage instruction, an object category or description, or a reference image. At each decision step, the agent receives RGB-D observations and pose information, and selects a movement action or STOP. A navigation attempt ends upon STOP or budget exhaustion. A retry addresses the same goal from its original starting pose, while a subsequent task introduces a new goal and its specified start. Memory per-sists within the scene and task-sequence boundaries of the evaluation protocol. RetroNav couples online navigation with memory accu-mulation and scene-grounded reflection (Fig. 1). During navigation, a View Selection Agent chooses a direction and a Waypoint Selection Agent selects a geometrically feasi-ble destination. After each attempt, the Memory Builder update the unified memory and an independent evaluator accesses whether the attempt succeeded. If the attempt is judged unsuccessful, a Retrospection Agent reviews histor-ical decisions against the accumulated scene evidence and generates a retry brief. The robot then restarts navigation from its initial pose, guided by the brief. For a single attempt, we omit task and attempt indices. 3.2. Unified Memory 

A single trajectory yields both physical evidence and task reasoning. RetroNav structures this history into a unified memory: 

M = ( S, H, L), (2) where S represents scene knowledge across space and enti-ties, H preserves chronological navigation experiences, and

Core representations. The anchor of unified memory is the observation o, defined by a 6-DoF pose, multi-view RGB-D images, and its attempt identifier. Observations serve as immutable empirical evidence: semantic classifica-tions may be revised over time, but raw observations remain uncorrupted. (b) Regions de-note concrete architectural enclosures (e.g., a specific bed-room), organizing confirmed places while decoupling view-ing vantage from observed entities. Hierarchical scene consolidation : Scene knowledge 

S is updated in two stages. First, a vision-language model proposes object bounding boxes and semantic descriptions from RGB images, and SAM [17] produces segmentation masks from these boxes (see Sec. 7.2). The masks and depth observations are used to back-project object evidence into 3D metric coordinates and fuse it into cumulative 3D object instances via spatial clustering and overlap association [12]. Then, the Memory Builder receives the cumulative Bird’s-Eye-View (BEV) map and observations from the current at-tempt. It clusters observations into places, assigns places to regional instances, and refines object semantic summaries while holding object geometries invariant. Both successful and failed attempts contribute to scene consolidation. 

Observation-centric dual association. The association layer L connects experiences H with scene knowledge S

via provenance binding and contextual linking: 1. Deterministic provenance chain : Each decision event is anchored at its observation, which connects it to the corre-sponding place, supported region membership, and associ-ated object evidence. This establishes the provenance chain 

decision → observation → scene entities .2. Bidirectional contextual linking : Rather than storing passive logs, L enables active cross-track querying. From decision to scene, reviewing a decision at ot retrieves cu-mulative multi-perspective evidence accumulated across at-tempts at that place. From scene to experience, any place or object can be queried to retrieve historical attempts and fail-ures in its vicinity, enabling the agent to contextualize past choices against cumulative spatial facts during reflection. 

3.3. Memory-Assisted Navigation 

At each navigation step t, we split the decision into two agents. The View Selection Agent dynamically retrieves relevant scene context and cross-task experiences and out-puts a moving direction and motion intent. Next, the Way-point Selection Agent selects an executable waypoint. 

View Selection. At each decision step t, the View Se-4lection Agent serves as the semantic planner. It conditions on the navigation goal g, four-directional panoramic RGB observations ot, the local metric BEV map Gt, the ongoing within-attempt history ht, and the retry brief b (where b = ∅

for the initial attempt). To facilitate the unified memory, the agent dynamically queries M to construct a task-relevant context mt. First, us-ing the current robot pose, it indexes historical observation positions in S to maintain a local context, which contains the region summary, adjacent place descriptions, and visi-ble objects. When this local context is insufficient to select a view, the agent calls search memory to retrieve relevant scene entities and uses read memory to obtain detailed descriptions or source RGB images for visual verification. When encountering route branching, landmark ambigu-ity, or conflicting sensory cues, the agent queries L using the spatial entities as anchors and accesses relevant deci-sion fragments from other completed tasks. Retrieval prior-itizes decision events that occurred at that place or explic-itly referenced the target entity, filtered by relevance to the ongoing unresolved question. Each retrieved fragment en-capsulates the historical goal, task interpretation, observa-tion pose, chosen action, explicit rationale, and subsequent execution consequences. This observation-centric retrieval provides synthesized memory context mt.Conditioned on this context, the agent outputs either

Operating independently of long-term mem-ory M, it focuses strictly on local collision avoidance and physical traversability. 3.4. Evaluation and Retrospection 

When a navigation attempt ends (either the agent calls 

STOP or exhausts its step budget), we use an independent Evaluator Agent to check whether the task is succeeded. If the attempt is judged unsuccessful, the system reflects on the failure to identify mistakes and provide guidance for the next retry. 

Evaluator. After an attempt finishes, we first update the scene memory with τ . During navigation, the robot may stop too early or go to the wrong way due to misjudgments. Asking the navigator to judge itself usually fails to catch these mistakes. Therefore, we use an independent Evalua-tor to decide whether the attempt actually succeeded. The Evaluator takes the goal g, the full attempt trace τ , and the updated scene memory S as input. It evaluates the attempt from two aspects. First, it checks whether the executed path followed the key landmarks and steps described in the instruction. Second, it compares the final stopping posi-tion with the updated scene memory to confirm whether the robot truly stopped near the correct target, rather than near a confusing or similar object. Finally, the evaluator outputs a final judgment of success or failure. A retry is triggered only when the attempt is judged as a failure, which prevents blind and unnecessary retries. 

Retrospection Agent. Once a retry is triggered, the Ret-rospection Agent reviews the failed trajectory τ to find the reasons behind the failure. In many cases, navigation fails not because the environment lacks information, but because the agent misinterpreted its visual observations. Based on the accumulated scene memory S, the agent examines three common types of decision errors: (a) Goal or landmark misidentification: checking if the agent mistook a similar room or an incorrect object for the target; (b) Route selec-tion mistakes: checking if the agent turned in the wrong di-rection at corridor intersections or doorways, leading into dead ends; (c) Experience misapplication: checking the cross-task experiences cited in the decision rationales zt

through the association layer L, to verify if past experiences were incorrectly applied. During this review, the agent can use the full scene mem-ory M to clarify confusing visual details. Finally, the Ret-rospection Agent summarizes these diagnostic results into a structured retry brief b:

Retry. With the retry brief b, the robot resets to its ini-tial pose s0 and starts a new attempt. In the new attempt, the View Selection Agent takes both the brief b and the reference images as part of its input (Sec. 3.3). The brief acts like a checklist: when the robot approaches a loca-tion where it previously failed, the brief reminds it not to repeat the past mistake. Meanwhile, the robot still relies on real-time camera views to make step-by-step movement and collision-avoidance decisions. In this way, accumulated scene memory not only benefits future tasks, but also di-rectly helps fix failures in the current task.

Evaluation metrics. Across all benchmarks, we report standard embodied navigation metrics: Success Rate (SR) measuring goal reach criteria, and Success weighted by Path Length (SPL) penalizing trajectory inefficiency. For con-tinuous instruction following (R2R-CE, RxR-CE, HM3D-OVON), we also report Oracle Success Rate (OSR), indi-cating whether the agent visited the goal threshold at any step. For multi-tour IR2R-CE, we report tour-level normal-ized Dynamic Time Warping (t-nDTW) to assess fidelity to the demonstration paths across successive episodes. For GOAT-Bench, we report subtask-level SR and SPL, as well as Episode Success Rate (e-SR), which strictly requires ev-ery subtask in an episode sequence to succeed. SPL in the main tables is computed from each task’s final attempt. We additionally evaluate path efficiency using the total distance traveled across all attempts in Sec. 8.2. 

4.2. Episodic Navigation 

We evaluate navigation performance under standard episodic configurations (R2R-CE, RxR-CE, and HM3D-OVON) where environments reset between tasks and cross-task memory is disabled. In this setting, the agent relies solely on online perception, planning, and our failure re-flection and retry mechanism. As shown in Tabs. 1 and 2, RetroNav consis-tently outperforms existing training-free methods across both instruction-following and open-vocabulary object-goal search. Notably, equipped with GPT-6-Astra, RetroNav es-tablishes new state-of-the-art results on R2R-CE (84.0% SR) and RxR-CE (84.0% SR), substantially surpassing the training-free AgenticNav (76.0% SR on R2R-CE) and the training-based ReflectVLN (66.0% SR on RxR-CE). Simi-larly, in open-vocabulary object search on HM3D-OVON, RetroNav achieves a leading 66.9% SR and 50.2% SPL, outperforming existing approaches. Crucially, this strong performance generalizes across model families: equipped with GPT-5.6-Sol, RetroNav achieves 76.3% SR on R2R-CE, ranking second and outperforming AgenticNav (76.0% SR), while open-source Qwen3.8-27B also achieves solid success rates and efficiency on R2R-CE and RxR-CE, demonstrating that our reflection and retry mechanism ef-fectively recovers from execution errors regardless of the underlying foundation model. 

4.3. Navigation in Persistent Environments 

We next evaluate lifelong navigation in persistent environ-ments (IR2R-CE and GOAT-Bench), where our full Unified Memory module is active to accumulate spatial knowledge and past navigation experiences across successive episodes. As summarized in Tabs. 3 and 4, retaining and grounding decisions on cumulative scene memory yields marked per-formance gains. On multi-tour IR2R-CE, RetroNav with GPT-6-Astra achieves a leading 89.2% SR and an unprece-dented 78.8% t-nDTW, demonstrating that cumulative spa-tial awareness not only helps the agent reach destination goals but also substantially improves adherence to human demonstration paths across repeated visits. Furthermore, on GOAT-Bench’s demanding multimodal sequential navigation, RetroNav with GPT-6-Astra main-tains a high subtask SR of 84.1% while achieving a re-markable 40.3% episode success rate (e-SR), surpassing NavHarness (36.9% e-SR). Notably, open-source Qwen3.8-27B achieves a solid 74.4% subtask SR and 17.1% e-SR, outperforming established open-source baselines such as EvoMemNav (59.6% SR) and SSMG-Nav (8.6% e-SR). These results confirm that our Unified Memory architecture effectively transforms historical observations and reflec-tion outcomes into reusable environmental assets, signifi-cantly reducing compounding errors in long-horizon em-bodied tasks across diverse model families. 5. Conclusion 

In this paper, we presented RetroNav , a training-free self-evolving navigation framework that integrates navigation, memory accumulation, and failure reflection. RetroNav maintains a unified memory that links raw environmental observations with historical navigation experiences, allow-ing the agent to re-examine prior choices while keeping factual observations distinct from subjective interpretations. After each navigation attempt, an independent evaluator as-sesses task completion. If the attempt is judged unsuc-cessful, a reflection module reviews prior decisions against accumulated scene evidence and generates a retry brief to guide the next attempt. By bridging cross-task knowledge reuse with within-task decision revision, RetroNav achieves state-of-the-art performance across five navigation bench-marks. Despite its effectiveness, several limitations remain. First, the accuracy of memory construction and failure reflection depends on the quality of upstream perception, spatial association, and model judgment. Perceptual ambiguities can occasionally misguide the diagnosis. Second, while the retry mechanism substantially improves overall task success, additional attempts inevitably in-cur extra travel steps and inference costs. Future work could investigate adaptive retry policies that balance recovery benefits against execution budgets, as well as more robust cross-task association under sensor noise.

7.1. Record Layout and Evidence References 

A fundamental principle of RetroNav is that an agent’s ex-perience contains two complementary streams: objective evidence about the physical environment, and subjective in-terpretations made toward specific goals. Tab. 9 specifies the database schemas concretizing this separation across S,

H, and L.

Stable observation anchors. Every perceptual input im-ported into memory is anchored by an immutable observa-tion record o, indexed by a scene-global integer ID, source attempt ID, local step, and 6-DoF sensor pose. Sensor pay-loads (RGB, depth, instance masks, and local point clouds) are retained as referenced filesystem assets, preventing data duplication across semantic records. Because raw sensor observations are immutable, downstream semantic interpre-tations can be iteratively revised without corrupting under-lying empirical evidence. 1Physical and semantic scene knowledge ( S). Scene knowledge maintains the persistent spatial representation: • Places group observations within shared local vantages, retaining 1–4 representative multi-view images and syn-thesizing view-specific spatial descriptions. • Regions cluster confirmed places into architectural enclo-sures (e.g., bedrooms, corridors), decoupling viewpoint vantages from observed room bounds. • Objects store 3D metric bounding boxes, fused point clouds, categories, and bounding-box image crops. • Transitions record verified traversability exclusively from physically executed displacements, guaranteeing collision-free path connectivity. 

Episodic decision traces ( H). Complementary to scene knowledge, episodic history preserves the temporal se-quence of agent reasoning. Each decision event stores the task goal g, current observation ID, chosen view/action, ex-plicit task interpretation and decision rationale, execution feedback, and next observation ID. When failure reflection is conducted (Sec. 3.4), retrospective diagnoses and sug-gested corrections are appended as linked reflection records, preserving historical decision traces in their original context while augmenting them with diagnostic insights. 

Dual associative indexes ( L). The association layer connects S and H through two complementary links: • Provenance index : links each decision event to its imme-diate observation, resolving its place and region member-ship. If topological places merge during map refinement, events automatically re-resolve their associations through their persistent observation anchors. • Contextual attribution index : distinguishes topological colocation (an event occurred at a place) from causal at-tribution (a decision explicitly cited a specific object or past experience), enabling selective retrieval during sub-sequent reasoning.

Transitions are added from the actual execu-tion log. The resulting scene knowledge, navigation experi-ences, and associations are saved together with the updated ObjectMap for subsequent navigation and reflection. 

7.3. Query Interfaces and Context Budgets 

During deployment, the agent queries unified memory for two distinct cognitive purposes: online spatial grounding during forward navigation (Sec. 3.3), and causal mistake di-agnosis during post-attempt reflection (Sec. 3.4). 

Online spatial context. To support forward viewpoint selection without exceeding prompt limits, the system dy-namically maintains a local context window encompass-ing places within a horizontal radius of 3.0 m and vertical threshold of 1.5 m (capped at 8 places). A 0.5 m hystere-sis margin prevents rapid context oscillations across topo-logical boundaries. Within this local window, objects are grouped into at most 20 semantic categories. If local in-formation is insufficient to resolve movement direction, the View Selection Agent invokes search memory (query-ing 1–4 phrases, returning up to 6 ranked entity summaries via lexical and exact-token matching) and read memory 

(fetching detailed descriptions and a representative high-resolution RGB image for up to 4 selected Place/Object IDs). 

Cross-task experience retrieval. When facing ambigu-ous intersections or misleading visual landmarks, the agent queries the associative layer L using candidate spatial enti-ties and current subgoals as anchors. The retrieval engine identifies relevant historical decision events located at the same place or citing the same entities. The interface returns up to three concise event summaries and extracts up to two detailed decision fragments, providing the historical goal, action choice, execution consequence, and linked reflection without congesting context windows. 

Reflection query protocol. During post-attempt re-flection, the Retrospection Agent operates under dis-tinct query bounds tailored for forensic diagnosis:

8.3. Joint View and Waypoint Selection 

We evaluate joint view and waypoint selection with Qwen on R2R-CE. The two-stage scheme first selects a view from four RGB images and then selects a waypoint within that view (Sec. 3.3). The joint scheme receives the four im-ages and their waypoint candidates, and directly outputs both a view ID and a waypoint ID, removing one model call per decision. We report SR, SPL, OSR, model calls, to-kens, and cumulative model-call time averaged per episode; robot execution time is excluded. Tab. 12 compares the two schemes. As shown in Tab. 12, the joint scheme performs worse than the two-stage scheme across the navigation metrics. It requires a longer trajectory with approximately one addi-tional decision step per episode. Although joint selection re-duces overall latency by eliminating one round-trip API call per step, this latency reduction comes at the expense of sub-stantially higher cumulative token consumption (390.92 K vs. 265.41 K). The main reason is that the reported token count is ac-cumulated over all decision steps, including both input and output tokens. As navigation proceeds, the input context becomes longer because it contains an expanding history of observations, actions, and execution feedback. There-fore, an additional step near the end of an episode carries a larger token cost than an early step. Even though the joint scheme uses only about one more step on average, this extra step introduces a complete additional context window and substantially increases the cumulative input tokens. Con-sequently, the joint scheme consumes more tokens overall despite using fewer model calls per decision. These results suggest that separating view selection and waypoint selec-tion provides more reliable navigation decisions and better controls the accumulation of context over long trajectories. 6
