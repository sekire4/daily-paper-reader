# CoLink: Global Compression and Knowledge Linking for Multi-Turn Dialogue Modeling 

Lian Yan, Zhenbo Xia, Haotian Wang, Chen Tang, Yi Guan, Jie Liu, Fellow, IEEE, Jingchi Jiang 

Abstract —Extracting salient information from long-horizon dialogue histories remains a significant challenge for Large Language Models (LLMs) due to the restricted context windows and efficient retrieval challenges. Current methods typically rely on history organization or local token-level optimization, suf-fering from information loss, retrieval redundancy, and myopic attention. Inspired by the view in cognitive science that dialogue constitutes sense-making and memory association, we propose CoLink, a framework that enhances LLMs’ global context awareness in domain-specific multi-turn dialogues through infor-mation linking and compression. CoLink first clusters historical contexts into topic graphs to establish cross-semantic associations with external knowledge. Guided by the adaptive control of thought, it then retrieves and activates knowledge via synergizing semantic relevance and graph-structural topology. Crucially, to achieve effective compression of history and knowledge, we ground multi-turn dialogues in information theory and derive a principled optimization objective for extracting the core sense-making information. Coupled with a competitive slot mechanism, CoLink condenses the global context into dedicated memory and knowledge slots, efficiently distilling lengthy, redundant inputs into an extremely compact latent representation. Extensive empirical results on both public and our MMD datasets validate CoLink’s superiority and generalizability in knowledge linking and sense modeling. 

Index Terms —Multi-turn dialogue, global information com-pression, knowledge adaptive activation, information bottleneck.

A. History Organization and Utilization 

Hierarchical structures and retrieval mechanisms mitigate redundancy by selectively retaining crucial history and en-abling localization of relevant context. MemoryBank [6] par-titions history into chunks and retrieves relevant blocks via similarity search. RAPTOR [8] and MemTree [7] organize dia-logue into hierarchical trees through recursive clustering or se-mantic aggregation, enabling retrieval at multiple granularities. Graph-based approaches, such as GraphRAG [10], HippoRAG [9], ArchRAG [17], KG2RAG [18], and HippoRAG2 [11], extract entity–relation graphs and leverage graph traversal to identify relevant substructures. While these methods improve scalability and retrieval efficiency, they primarily rely on sparse entity or structural cues, often discarding fine-grained contextual information. Moreover, they lack explicit modeling of inter-turn semantic dependencies, which limits holistic integration and global compression of the dialogue history. 

B. Exploiting Potential Gains from Local Tokens 

These Local Token Modeling methods are fundamentally predicated on the phenomenon of attention sparsity. Empirical observations indicate that LLMs consistently allocate dispro-portionate attention weights to a minimal subset of tokens, par-ticularly the initial and the last few tokens. This phenomenon is known as the ”Attention Sink” [19], [20]. Leveraging this insight, methods such as StreamLLM [12], StreamingDialogue [4], and LM-Infinite [21] persistently cache initial tokens, turn delimiters, or recent tokens to approximate full-history attention. Similarly, H2O [22] dynamically retains only a fraction of tokens with the highest accumulated attention scores. Chen et al. [5] introduce core context-aware attention, which partitions long contexts into groups and preserves only a single representative token per group. However, recent studies [23], [24] reveal that these sinks predominantly serve as structural biases to maintain attention stability rather than as repositories of core semantics. By con-fining the receptive field to local windows or sparse anchors, these methods inevitably fragment the historical continuity. Consequently, they inherently lack global perception, leading to degraded performance in tasks that necessitate deep, long-horizon understanding. 

C. Cognitive Memory Mechanisms 

Cognitive neuroscience has extensively investigated the mechanisms of memory retention, retrieval, and knowledge representation. Advanced cognitive abilities rely not merely on the synergy among various memory systems, but fundamen-tally on the high-level abstraction of information [14], [25]. Regarding memory architecture, Squire’s taxonomy classically distinguishes declarative memory (comprising semantic facts and episodic events) from non-declarative memory (e.g., pro-cedural skills) [26]. Furthermore, research posits that seman-tic networks serve as the critical interface between sensory inputs and consolidated long-term knowledge [27]. During dialogue processing, cognitive theories suggest that novel linguistic stimuli trigger a complex complementary interaction [14]: the hippocampus acts as a rapid episodic index, which subsequently activates long-term semantic knowledge in the neocortex via associative retrieval. These activated memories are then integrated within short-term working memory to facilitate coherent language generation. Motivated by these insights, recent studies have explored brain-inspired architectures for dialogue systems. C. Training Objectives and Optimization 

To effectively optimize the compressors, we operationalize the objective derived in Section III via a composite loss function comprising three terms, as defined in (20). 

KL Divergence Loss (LKL ): Constrains the posterior 

qϕ(z|h, q ) to approximate the prior rφ(z|q), enforcing aggres-sive compression of the history relative to the query.

Existing medical dialogue datasets often suffer from knowl-edge leakage, role confusion, poor coherence, and factual hallucinations [35]–[37]. To mitigate these issues and en-hance granularity, we construct the MMD dataset using a novel knowledge-augmented multi-agent debate framework. This framework simulates expert consultations involving three roles: User, Domain Experts ( N ≥ 2), and Judge.  

> •

User Agent: Initiates queries related to specific diseases.  

> •

Expert Agent Group: Each expert performs reasoning based on a shared domain knowledge base, retrieves relevant facts, reflects on retrieved content, and filters out irrelevant knowledge.  

> •

Judge Agent: Evaluates and aggregates expert opinions, and outputs a final diagnosis or recommendation only when a consensus is reached. Dialogue scenarios follow real medical settings (e.g., diagno-sis, examination recommendation, medication). Each conver-sation is grounded in an anonymized electronic health record and a corresponding knowledge base. For each user query, experts, augmented by a private, expert-only knowledge base, engage in iterative debate to correct errors. The final response is emitted only upon consensus verified by the Judge agent (see Fig. 4). D. Main Analysis 1) Comparison Results: Table III presents the compara-tive performance across all datasets. All experiments utilize Qwen2.5-1.5B [42] as the backbone model. Based on these results, we summarize the key findings as follows: 

CoLink attains effective global information compression. 

CoLink consistently outperforms both structured retrieval and local token attention baselines, achieving an average gain of 

2.59% even under a 32 × compression constraint across two datasets. This indicates its superior ability to compress long-context information. Notably, CoLink matches or even exceeds the performance of Full History. This validates that, governed by the Information Bottleneck principle, CoLink effectively distills sufficient statistics from the global context, filtering out redundancy and noise that may distract the full-context baseline while retaining decision-critical semantics. 

Rigid structuring compromises contextual integrity. Al-though chunk-based retrieval methods, such as Memory-Bank and NV-Embed, offer moderate improvements over the base model, rigid graph and tree-based approaches (e.g., GraphRAG, MemTree) often degrade performance. Our anal-ysis reveals that while structured extraction preserves discrete entities, it fails to capture the holistic semantic flow of the text. Furthermore, entity retention is highly sensitive to extraction quality and provides limited benefits for long-range dependen-cies (see Section V-F2). Similarly, Summary History, despite reducing redundancy, suffers from inevitable information loss during condensation and obstructs performance. In contrast, CoLink resolves this dilemma via gradient-optimized slots, proving that soft, learnable compression is a more robust strategy for retaining holistic semantics than rigid structuring. 

CoLink achieves superior compression efficiency. Beyond generation quality, we evaluate the compression ratio of vari-ous approaches, as summarized in Table IV. CoLink reaches optimal performance utilizing only 3.1% of the original con-text length. This evidence reaffirms the high efficiency and effectiveness of our cognitive-inspired cross-semantic linking and IB-driven abstraction. Unlike traditional structured ap-proaches, CoLink directly compresses the dialogue history in the latent space, circumventing costly entity extraction and graph construction to achieve O(1) retrieval complexity. Furthermore, let T denote the number of dialogue turns and L

the average length per turn, yielding a total historical context length of N = T ·L. Standard dense attention mechanisms suf-fer from quadratic time complexity O(( T L )2) and linear space complexity O(T L ) due to the continuous growth of the KV cache. In contrast, CoLink compresses the unbounded history into M fixed-size slots (where M ≪ T L ). This reduces the storage complexity for history representation to O(M ) and the attention complexity during generation to O(M 2), ensuring constant-time scalability regardless of dialogue duration.

Architectural Transferability. To assess whether CoLink is model-agnostic, we apply the framework to Llama-3.2-3B [43] by training the memory slots on the MMD dataset. As presented in Table V, CoLink yields consistent performance gains over the vanilla Llama-3.2 architecture. This result un-derscores its strong architectural transferability, indicating that our cognitive-inspired compression mechanism can effectively augment various LLM backbones. 

Cross-Domain Adaptability. We further evaluate CoLink on UltraData [44], an English open-domain multi-turn dia-logue dataset, to verify its performance beyond the medical domain. Notably, since external knowledge is not natively required for this task, we deploy only the memory slots for history compression. Ai,j = exp( qi · kj )

F. Further Analysis 1) Impact of Slot Number: The number of memory slots (M ) controls the granularity of global history compression. To investigate the trade-off between compression ratio and generation fidelity, we conduct sensitivity analysis by varying 

M on MMD, as visualized in Fig. 6(b). Performance exhibits a non-monotonic trend: both overly small and excessively large M lead to degradation. This behavior is theoretically intuitive. With too few slots, long dialogue histories are over-compressed, forcing each slot to encode excessive informa-tion and causing semantic loss. We revisit multi-turn dialogue through the lens of cognitive neuroscience and propose CoLink, a framework that innova-tively integrates the IB principle of global abstraction with adaptive control of thought to activate associative knowledge. To address the scarcity of multi-turn dialogue data, we design a knowledge-augmented multi-agent debate framework and construct a dataset, MMD. Comprehensive evaluations demon-strate CoLink’s significant superiority over strong baselines while utilizing only 3.125% of the original context length. Driven by the IB principle, CoLink matches or even exceeds the performance of the full visible history. Furthermore, its soft, learnable slot mechanism preserves continuous semantic flow, avoiding the structural fragmentation inherent in rigid approaches. Rigorous validation through ablations and human evaluation confirms the robustness, model-agnostic nature, and generalization capability of our approach. While theoretical derivations and extensive experiments val-idate CoLink’s efficiency in history compression, our analysis reveals a trade-off: the method excels at capturing abstract semantic representations but struggles with tasks that require precise lexical retrieval. We evaluated this using the MemDaily dataset [49], which features concise histories but demands high-precision recall of specific entities (e.g., phone numbers, job titles). On this benchmark, CoLink underperforms com-pared to text-based prompt baselines (such as Full History and Summary History). This suggests that the compression process may filter out fine-grained details necessary for exact factual reconstruction. Consequently, enhancing fidelity in precise information retrieval remains a pivotal direction for future work. REFERENCES [1] J. Achiam, S. Adler, S. Agarwal, L. Ahmad, I. Akkaya, F. L. Aleman, D.
