# Abstract 

While language models have become dramatically more capable, retrieval still largely relies on lex-ical matching or similarity in fixed embedding spaces. This creates a capability asymmetry: what the retriever fails to find, even the strongest gen-erator cannot recover. We propose DecisionRAG, which brings the capabilities of pretrained lan-guage models to retrieval using decision models that jointly evaluate candidate branches in a single forward pass, without generating any tokens, and show that judging candidates in a shared context makes their relevance estimates comparable. Like a person browsing a library, DecisionRAG navi-gates the corpus’s inherent structure from broad topics down to individual passages, deciding at each level which branches are worth exploring. Without task-specific training or a first-stage re-triever, DecisionRAG outperforms dense retrieval with an 8B reranker by 13.8 R@1 points on Mul-tiDoc2Dial and 17.4 Hit@1 points on Natural Questions. Across four benchmarks, including a corpus of 2.68 million Wikipedia paragraphs, retrieval takes just 0.7–1.8 seconds per query. 

# 1. Introduction 

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) has become a core component of modern language-model pipelines (Lewis et al., 2020; Guu et al., 2020; Gao et al., 2023b). By fetching evidence from an external corpus at inference time, RAG gives a model access to up-to-date information it never saw during training (Lewis et al., 2020; Vu et al., 2024) and grounds its responses in verifiable sources (Shuster et al., 2021; Gao et al., 2023a). Yet, while the language generation models have become dra-matically more capable (OpenAI, 2026; Anthropic, 2026), the retrievers that decide what they see have not kept pace.

Most retrieval systems still score each passage via heuris-tics based on term frequencies like BM25 (Robertson & Zaragoza, 2009), or by nearest neighbors in a fixed embed-ding space (Karpukhin et al., 2020; Izacard et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2025). This creates a striking capability asym-metry between the retriever and the generation model (Fig-ure 1): whatever the retriever misses, no amount of generator capability can recover (Barnett et al., 2024). Ideally, a model as capable as the generator would decide what to retrieve. Agentic approaches let an LLM navigate the corpus step by step (Yao et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023; Jin et al., 2025), but generating many tokens at every step in an agentic loop makes them too slow and costly for many ap-plications (Jeong et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2026). Rerankers offer a cheaper middle ground (Nogueira & Cho, 2019; Sun et al., 2023; Reddy et al., 2024), but they can only reorder what a first-stage retriever has already returned. Decision models have emerged as a new paradigm to lever-age the power of pretrained language models quickly with-out sampling any tokens (TypeSafe AI, 2026; OpenAI, 2026; Li & Wagle, 2026). Given a context and a set of questions, a decision model such as Jev (TypeSafe AI, 2026) returns, for each question, a probability distribution over its answer options (for a yes/no question, P (yes) ), answering many questions in parallel against a shared context in a single forward pass. This makes them a natural fit for retrieval, bringing the capability of LLMs to the choice of what to retrieve. Furthermore, scoring the candidates jointly makes their decisions comparable with one another (Chen et al., 2025; Gupta et al., 2025). Using decision models to choose what to retrieve thus promises to level the playing field 1arXiv:2610.35868v1 [cs.AI] 26 Oct 2026 Retriever-Free Retrieval: Navigating Documents with Decision Models 

between the retriever and generator. We propose DecisionRAG, which retrieves by navigating instead of searching (Figure 2). Most corpora have an in-herent structure of topics, documents, sections, and pas-sages, which humans exploit to find information efficiently (Pirolli & Card, 1999). DecisionRAG leverages this struc-ture through hierarchical decisions (Geva & Berant, 2018; Liu et al., 2021): at each level, a decision model jointly eval-uates candidates to identify the most promising branches, progressively narrowing the search from broad categories (e.g. topics or document titles) down to individual pas-sages. Each decision requires only a single forward pass without generating any tokens, keeping retrieval fast even for large corpora. DecisionRAG thus brings the capabilities of pretrained language models directly to retrieval, without a first-stage retriever at query time, a precomputed index, or task-specific training. We evaluate DecisionRAG on four benchmarks spanning multi-turn dialogue (Feng et al., 2021; Katsis et al., 2025), scenario-based questions (Sun et al., 2022), and open-domain search over 2.68 million Wikipedia paragraphs (Kwiatkowski et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2024). Without task-specific training, DecisionRAG outperforms dense re-trieval with an 8B reranker by 13.8 R@1 points on Mul-tiDoc2Dial and 17.4 Hit@1 points on Natural Questions. Judging candidates in a shared context contributes to these gains, improving accuracy by up to 7.7 points over inde-pendent judgments (Section 6.1). Despite harnessing the capabilities of a language model at every stage, Decision-RAG retrieves in just 0.7–1.8 seconds at a cost of 0.2–2.3 cents per query. Compared with an LLM that generates nav-igation decisions over the same hierarchy token-by-token, it achieves comparable accuracy with 34–145 × lower latency (Section 6.3). Training a dedicated 8B decision model on in-domain data further raises R@1 on MultiDoc2Dial to 60.7, surpassing the best compared published task-trained retriever by 5.7 points and suggesting substantial headroom as decision models improve. 

# 2. Related Work 

Traditional retrieval methods, including BM25 (Robertson & Zaragoza, 2009) and dense retrievers (Karpukhin et al., 2020), rank passages using lexical matching or learned embedding similarity. Neural reranking methods refine these rankings using language-model-based relevance esti-mates. Cross-encoders evaluate individual query–passage pairs (Nogueira et al., 2020), while LLM-based rerankers perform listwise ranking by generating permutations (Sun et al., 2023) or iteratively selecting candidates from subsets (Zhuang et al., 2024; Li et al., 2026). Although rerank-ing can improve retrieval quality, it remains limited by the candidate recall of the initial retrieval stage. An alternative line of work integrates language models di-rectly into the retrieval process. Agentic approaches in-terleave reasoning with the use of search-engines to itera-tively identify relevant evidence (Yao et al., 2023; Jin et al., 2025). Other methods use hierarchical representations to guide retrieval. MemWalker navigates a hierarchy of sum-maries constructed from long documents (Chen et al., 2023), while PageIndex uses document tables of contents for LLM-guided navigation (VectifyAI, 2025). LATTICE extends hi-erarchical navigation to corpus-level retrieval, constructing a semantic tree and using LLM-generated relevance assess-ments to select branches at each level (Gupta et al., 2026). These approaches require generating tokens sequentially at each retrieval step, which can incur substantial latency when navigation involves multiple rounds of reasoning. Recent work has explored more efficient approaches to language-model-based ranking. FIRST derives listwise rankings from the logits of a single generated identifier (Reddy et al., 2024), while ICR and BlockRank extract rank-ing signals from attention patterns over jointly presented candidates (Chen et al., 2025; Gupta et al., 2025). Deci-sion models offer another approach to evaluating multiple candidates without generating tokens (TypeSafe AI, 2026; OpenAI, 2026). Given a shared context and a set of ques-tions, these models produce probability distributions over the answer options of each question in a single forward pass. This enables parallel relevance judgments over mul-tiple candidates, with each candidate evaluated against the same contextual information. Hierarchical retrieval has also been studied as a means of reducing the search space in large document collections. DocQN learns to navigate document trees using reinforce-ment learning (Geva & Berant, 2018), while dense hierarchi-cal retrieval progressively selects documents and passages using trained encoders (Liu et al., 2021). RAPTOR con-structs a hierarchy of recursively generated summaries to support retrieval at different levels of abstraction (Sarthi et al., 2024). Related work has also highlighted the impor-tance of document-level context for passage retrieval (Wang et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026). DecisionRAG combines hierarchical retrieval with non-generative relevance judgments. It uses existing document structure where available and constructs document groups otherwise (Section 4.1). Unlike conventional reranking pipelines, DecisionRAG does not require a first-stage re-triever to construct a candidate pool. # 3. Method 

3.1. Problem Formulation 

We consider a corpus D = {D1, . . . , D N }, where each document Di consists of passages di1, . . . , d iM i . Given a query q, which may include the preceding dialogue turns, our goal is to retrieve the passages containing the evidence needed to answer it. (Figure 2) More generally, we view the corpus as a tree whose leaves are passages and whose internal nodes group them at increasing granularity, such as help centers, topic clusters, documents, or section headings. Each node has a short textual description: the text itself for a passage, the title for a document, and a one-sentence summary for a cluster. We call each depth of the tree a level 

and each round of requests to the decision model a stage ;most levels take one stage, and the final reranking is a stage of its own. As a running example, we use the query 

Who is the main character in Christopher Nolan’s film from the year 2000? (1) which can be answered by the Wikipedia corpus.

Judging candidates in a shared context. Because every question sees the same context, each probability is formed in view of the alternatives: a candidate that would look relevant on its own receives a lower P (Yes) when a clearly better one is present. The shared context thus puts the individ-ual probabilities into perspective, making them comparable across candidates. Our ablations support this: scoring can-didates together rather than separately improves R@1 on MultiDoc2Dial by 7.7 points and Hit@1 on Natural Ques-tions by 6.5 points (Sections 6.1 and 6.2). They also show where the comparison matters: splitting a pool into smaller requests lowers Hit@1 but leaves R@10 unchanged. Scores from separate requests therefore suffice for screening, where recall counts, whereas the final ranking benefits from one shared request.

Candidates not selected are pruned, and their descendants are never evaluated. We illustrate this process in Figure 2 (c) using the running example in Equation (1).

Budgets and pruning. Pruning is irreversible: a branch dropped at one level is not revisited for this query. The budgets kℓ therefore lean towards recall, trading coverage for cost (Section 6.6). A budget can also be a probability threshold, e.g., keeping a runner-up help center whenever the top score is below 0.8 (Appendix B.2). 

3.4. Hierarchy Construction and Scaling Higher-order hierarchies. The document–passage hier-archy is only the simplest instance of DecisionRAG. For larger corpora, documents can be organized into higher-order groups, such as topics, categories, or products, using existing corpus structure or grouping related documents. The same procedure then applies recursively: at each level, parallel binary judgments identify promising groups, and only their children are evaluated at the next level. For exam-ple, when searching the full Wikipedia corpus, one could traverse categories related to films and Christopher Nolan before reaching the individual page for Memento . Thus, De-cisionRAG extends to deeper hierarchies without requiring all documents to be evaluated at once. When a corpus lacks such structure, a hierarchy can be built offline by clustering document embeddings, e.g., with recursive k-means (Nist´ er & Stew´ enius, 2006) or scalable agglomerative clustering (Bateni et al., 2017; Monath et al., 2021; Dhulipala et al., 2023), as we do for Natural Questions (Section 4.1). The tree need not be uniform: on MTRAG, a heading level is inserted only below pages with more than 60 passages. 

Levels too large for one request. A level may hold more candidates than fit into one request; on Natural Questions, the 150 retained clusters contain about 17,500 page titles. We then split the level into parallel requests, each with its own shared context. This costs little: the benefit of the shared context levels off at about 50 candidates per re-quest (Section 6.2), and pooled scores suffice for screening. Where the top candidates of a split level should be compared more closely, we rescore them in one request, as for the top 50 pages on MTRAG (Appendix B.4). 

Combining with a retriever. DecisionRAG needs no first-stage retriever, but it can use one: adding the top passages of a dense retriever to the final candidates recovers evidence that pruning discarded ( + dense candidates ; Section 5.1). 

Scaling with corpus size. Because all candidates of a level are judged in parallel, latency depends on how many levels the hierarchy has, not on how many passages the corpus holds. Assume a roughly balanced hierarchy in which every node groups at most b children, e.g., topics of 100 documents with 100 passages each. Every additional level then multiplies the number of reachable passages by up to b, so a corpus of N passages needs about ⌈log b N ⌉ levels. Unless stated otherwise, we use the pretrained Jev decision model (TypeSafe’s Jev 1.13, typesafe/jev-1.13 ) (TypeSafe AI, 2026), without task-specific fine-tuning or a conven-tional first-stage retriever. We also evaluate task-trained, open-weight decision models in Section 5.3.

4.2. Retrieval Configurations 

We select dataset-specific navigation hierarchies and reten-tion budgets using separate design sets (Appendix A.5). For conversational benchmarks, we include the last six dialogue turns and maintain the notepad described in Appendix B. On MultiDoc2Dial , we navigate a help center → page →

passage hierarchy, retaining the top ten pages and scoring their passages alongside those from up to two notepad pages. On ConditionalQA , we use a page → paragraph hierar-chy, retaining the top three pages and jointly scoring their paragraphs. On MTRAG , we use a page → heading → passage hierar-chy, scoring all page titles in parallel, jointly rescoring the top 50 pages, and retaining the top ten. For long pages, we select relevant headings before scoring their passages. On Natural Questions , we navigate a cluster → page →

paragraph hierarchy, retaining 150 of the 1,000 clusters, evaluating paragraphs from the top 30 pages. All DecisionRAG (Jev) configurations finish with multiple-choice ranking of the top 20 passages (top five for Natural Questions); on MTRAG, only the selected passage is pro-moted to rank one. The + dense candidates variant adds the dense top 20 passages (top ten on ConditionalQA) to the final candidate pool. Additional routing rules and imple-mentation details are provided in Appendix B. We also evaluate task-trained decision models based on Qwen3-Base at 1.7B, 4B, and 8B parameters (Section 5.3). Architecture and training details are provided in Ap-pendix G. 

4.3. Baselines 

We compare DecisionRAG with retrieval methods used with-out task-specific fine-tuning, as well as task-trained methods where available. The baselines are grouped as follows: 

First-stage retrieval. We compare against BM25 with stemming and stopword removal, SPLADE++ (Formal et al., 2022), and Qwen3-Embedding-8B (Zhang et al., 2025). On ConditionalQA and MTRAG, the appendix also reports SPLADE-v3 with a DistilBERT encoder (Lassance et al., 2024) (Tables 11 and 12). 

Reranking. We evaluate bge-reranker-v2-m3 (Chen et al., 2024; BAAI, 2024) and Qwen3-Reranker-8B on the top 100 passages retrieved by Qwen3-Embedding-8B. We also evaluate GPT-5 (OpenAI, 2026) with low reasoning effort, reranking the top 20 dense candidates using a RankGPT-style prompt (Sun et al., 2023). 

Published reference results. We include the Multi-Doc2Dial authors’ fine-tuned DPR (Karpukhin et al., 2020; Feng et al., 2021) and DPR with cross-encoder (Zhang et al., 2026), as well as the ELSER retrieval results released with MTRAG (Katsis et al., 2025). These results are included as references rather than rerun under our evaluation setup.

# 5. Results 

We evaluate DecisionRAG across four benchmarks, com-paring its retrieval quality against conventional retrieval and reranking pipelines. We further assess downstream answer quality, the effects of task-specific training, and online la-tency and cost. 

5.1. Retrieval Effectiveness 

Without task-specific training or a conventional retrieval index, DecisionRAG substantially outperforms dense re-trieval with an 8B reranker (Table 2): On MultiDoc2Dial, it improves R@1 by 13.8 percentage points, and on Natural Questions, it improves Hit@1 by 17.4 points. Natural Questions also shows that DecisionRAG needs no ready-made hierarchy: Wikipedia’s 108,626 pages, far too many for one top level, are simply clustered into 1,000 groups (Section 4.1). DecisionRAG then reads only 16% of the page titles. Any corpus can be given a hierarchy this way, with a number of sequential stages that grows only logarithmically with its size (Equation (4) and Section 6.6). Even when using a strong language model such as GPT-5 to rerank the dense retrieval results, DecisionRAG achieves comparable or higher rank-one accuracy on MultiDoc2Dial and Natural Questions (65.4 versus 60.8). It also improves ConditionalQA R@10 (71.9 versus 64.0) and MTRAG R@5 (34.2 versus 31.8), although GPT-5 achieves higher Condi-tionalQA Hit@1 (73.4 versus 69.7). On MTRAG, whose annotated evidence was collected with ELSER, we judge the top five passages of every system, and DecisionRAG ranks first among all eighteen, including last-turn and rewritten-query baselines (R@5 34.2 versus 32.5 for the best baseline; Appendix A.3 and Table 12). DecisionRAG can also complement dense retrieval by in-corporating dense-retrieved passages into its final ranking stage. This combination improves R@10 from 83.6 to 84.7 on MultiDoc2Dial, from 71.9 to 76.3 on ConditionalQA, and from 86.7 to 88.9 on Natural Questions. These gains suggest that the two approaches recover complementary evidence and can be effectively combined.

# 6. Understanding DecisionRAG 

What makes decision-based retrieval effective? We examine how shared context sharpens relevance judgments, when multiple-choice ranking helps, and how navigation strate-gies and budgets trade off retrieval quality against efficiency. 

6.1. Scoring Candidates in a Shared Context

Shared-context binary judgments consistently outperform scoring each candidate in a separate request, and multiple-choice ranking performs best within its 255-option limit. 6.3. Decisions versus Generation

6.6. Navigation Budget and Hierarchy 

Finally, we examine how the navigation budget affects re-trieval. Pruning at the top level is what keeps navigation cheap on large corpora, but it is also where evidence can be lost: a page in a discarded cluster can never be retrieved. We inves-tigate how many clusters must be kept to match the recall of scoring every page directly, varying the share of the 1,000 Natural Questions clusters that DecisionRAG keeps up to flat scoring of all 108,626 page titles (Figure 7). Recall@10 rises steeply up to about 10% of the clusters and then stays level: keeping 15% matches flat scoring (86.2% versus 84.5%) at a fifth of the cost (2.3¢ versus 12.1¢ per query) and a third of the latency (1.7 versus 4.5 seconds). Below 5%, the cluster holding the gold page is often discarded, and recall@10 falls to 63.7% at 1%. The same trade-off appears one level down, in the page bud-get. On MultiDoc2Dial, we increase the number of newly retained pages from three to ten, keeping the remaining configuration fixed. R@10 improves from 81.0% to 83.6%, while R@1 increases only from 46.9% to 47.4% (Table 9). The larger budget increases API cost by 83% and median latency by 28%. # 7. Limitations 

DecisionRAG relies on a corpus hierarchy whose structure and node descriptions allow the decision model to identify relevant branches. Poorly organized documents or unin-formative descriptions can therefore hurt retrieval quality. Moreover, navigation prunes branches irreversibly, so rele-vant passages may be missed if their ancestors are discarded; we show that larger exploration budgets and complementary dense retrieval can mitigate this. 

# 8. Conclusion 

We introduced DecisionRAG, which brings the capabilities of large language models into retrieval by navigating corpus hierarchies with non-generative decisions. At each level, candidates are evaluated jointly in a shared context, allow-ing their relevance to be judged in view of one another. By progressively narrowing the search space, DecisionRAG retrieves relevant evidence with a number of sequential deci-sions that grows only logarithmically with corpus size under a balanced hierarchy. As decision models improve, retrieval can inherit their capabilities directly.

# A. Dataset and Corpus Details 

A.1. MultiDoc2Dial 

MultiDoc2Dial (Feng et al., 2021) contains goal-oriented dialogues grounded in 488 documents from four US government help centers. We build a help-center–page–passage hierarchy from the dataset’s metadata. Help centers are given by the domain field, and documents sharing a title after removing the #1/#2 part suffix form one page, giving 413 pages. We use the benchmark’s official structure-based segmentation into 4,110 passages. Passages are represented by their full text and their page and parent-heading path, pages by their titles and section headings, and the four help centers by one-line descriptions written manually from their names. We evaluate on the official validation split (661 dialogues, 4,201 turns). Each turn has one gold passage containing the grounding span of the agent’s response. The released test split provides no usable evaluation examples. 

A.2. ConditionalQA 

ConditionalQA (Sun et al., 2022) contains scenario-based questions about UK government guidance whose answers may depend on several conditions and paragraphs. Its corpus contains 652 guides. We recover their structure from the HTML headings ( h1 , h2 ) and extract 58,483 paragraph-level units from p, li and tr elements. Pages are represented by their titles and top-level part headings, and paragraphs are grouped under their heading paths. Retrieval searches the full corpus rather than assuming the relevant guide is known. We evaluate on the 285 questions of the labeled development split, 271 of which have annotated evidence (about four paragraphs on average). 

A.3. MTRAG–IBM Cloud 

We use the IBM Cloud subset of MTRAG (Katsis et al., 2025), with 8,578 documentation pages and 72,442 passages, keeping the benchmark’s document files and passage chunks. Pages are grouped into 172 products by the first path segment after /docs/ in their URLs. Because the corpus does not preserve HTML markup, we recover section headings from the text layout (short lines separated by blank lines). Pages are represented by their titles and recovered headings, and passages by their text and heading. Product descriptions are built automatically from the product name, the opening of its landing page and three page titles. The held-out evaluation set contains 26 human-written conversations with 205 turns, 188 of which have annotated evidence (one to seven gold passages). 

Expanded relevance judgments. MTRAG’s gold passages were selected from the annotators’ ELSER retrieval sessions, so relevant passages that ELSER did not surface cannot be gold. We therefore add LLM-judged relevant passages to the gold passages. For each turn, we pool the top five passages of all 18 systems evaluated on MTRAG, including released ELSER and the last-turn and rewritten-query baselines, together with the gold passages, representing identical texts once. GPT-5 receives the conversation, the reference reply and the shuffled candidates, without knowing which system retrieved them, and grades each passage as needed for the answer (2), related but insufficient (1) or unrelated (0). Grade-2 passages are added to the relevance set, and all original gold passages are kept. GPT-5 grades 87.9% of the gold passages as needed. The expanded labels reduce the dependence on ELSER. Table 12 compares all systems. 

A.4. Natural Questions 

We use the DAPR version (Wang et al., 2024) of Natural Questions (Kwiatkowski et al., 2019), whose corpus splits 108,626 Wikipedia pages into 2,682,017 paragraphs. We evaluate on a fixed random sample of 500 of the 3,610 questions in DAPR’s test split, which is drawn from NQ’s development set. A question’s gold paragraphs (one to three) lie on a single page. Since Wikipedia has no structure above the page, we cluster the pages into 1,000 groups with k-means over e5-base-v2 embeddings (Wang et al., 2022) of each page’s title and first sentence. Groups hold 15 to 350 pages (median 106). Each group is described by one sentence that Qwen3-8B writes from its page titles (Appendix B.5). A.5. Design Sets and Configuration Selection 

Retrieval configurations (candidate representations, routing rules and retention budgets) are selected on design sets that are disjoint from the evaluation sets. Selection does not update the parameters of Jev. MultiDoc2Dial uses samples of its training split and ConditionalQA 300 training questions. MTRAG has no training split, so we use 50 synthetic IBM Cloud conversations (292 turns) and 131 IBM Cloud turns from MTRAG-UN (Rosenthal et al., 2026), whose conversations are disjoint from the evaluation set. 

# B. Retrieval Configuration Details 

B.1. Candidate Representations and Questions 

At each stage, the judge receives a context containing the query and the numbered candidate descriptions, followed by one yes/no question per candidate. Candidates are listed in document order and grouped under their parent pages where applicable. Each stage builds a new context; probabilities from earlier stages are not included. Conversational queries contain the six most recent dialogue turns. For conversational retrieval, the page and passage questions are “Is the information the agent needs for its next reply on Page n?” and “Should the agent’s next reply be based on Passage n?” For ConditionalQA and Natural Questions, they are “Is the information needed to answer the user’s question on Page n?” and “Does Paragraph n contain information needed to answer the user’s question?” 

B.2. MultiDoc2Dial and the Conversational Notepad 

The routing context describes the four help centers, with one question per center. We keep the highest-scoring center, the runner-up if the top probability is below 0.8, and the center of the most recently retrieved page. The page stage scores the pages of the kept centers and keeps ten. All passages of these pages, plus those of the two most recent notepad pages, are scored in one shared context. The notepad, used on MultiDoc2Dial and MTRAG, lists distinct page identifiers by most recent retrieval. After each turn, the page of the top-ranked passage moves to the front. The page-stage context lists the notepad pages. The notepad is built only from the system’s own retrieval decisions, never from gold evidence, and previously retrieved passages are neither marked nor excluded. 

B.3. ConditionalQA 

The page stage scores all 652 guides in one request and keeps three, whose paragraphs are scored together under their heading paths. 

B.4. MTRAG 

The main configuration scores pages directly instead of routing through products. In a first pass, all 8,578 page titles are split into parallel requests under the input-size limit, keeping each product’s pages together, and the top 50 pages by pooled score are kept. Because these scores come from different contexts, a second pass rescores the 50 together with their product names and headings, and keeps ten. Pages with more than 60 passages pass through an extra stage that scores their headings and keeps the top eight. At most 60 passages of these headings, in document order, continue to the passage stage. 

B.5. Natural Questions 

Each cluster is described by Qwen3-8B, given its page titles (most typical first, at most 300) and the prompt: “Write one sentence (at most 30 words) describing what the pages in this group are about. Name the main kinds of subjects it covers, including the less typical ones, so that a reader can tell whether a given Wikipedia page would belong to this group.” The judge scores the 1,000 cluster descriptions in eight parallel requests, asking for each whether “the page with the information needed to answer the user’s question [is] likely in Topic n”, and keeps 150 clusters. Their page titles, about 17,500 per question, are scored in parallel requests of at most 20k tokens. The paragraph stage scores all paragraphs of the top three pages, the first ten of pages ranked 4–10 and the first three of pages ranked 11–30 in one shared context, about 230 15 Retriever-Free Retrieval: Navigating Documents with Decision Models 

paragraphs per question. Each paragraph’s probability is multiplied by its page’s. 

B.6. Multiple-Choice Ranking 

The multiple-choice question follows binary scoring. Its answer options are the top 20 candidates by P (yes) (top five on Natural Questions), and its context lists only these candidates, in reading order and formatted as in binary scoring. On MultiDoc2Dial, ConditionalQA and Natural Questions, the choice probabilities reorder the shortlist, and all other candidates keep their binary order. On MTRAG, only the highest-probability choice moves to rank one. Compared with a choice request over the full binary context, the shorter context changes retrieval by at most one point on every dataset and shortens the MTRAG choice request from 0.30–0.37 to 0.14 seconds. Retrieval without the choice step is evaluated in Section 6.4.

# E. Evaluation Sensitivity 

We rescore the retrieval results under two alternative relevance definitions. 

E.1. Identical passages under different IDs 

A retrieved passage can repeat the gold text under a different ID. We group passages by their text after whitespace normalization (and lowercasing for MTRAG), map retrieved and gold IDs to these groups, and keep only the first occurrence of each group in a ranking, so duplicate copies cannot create multiple hits. Text matching raises MultiDoc2Dial R@1 to 55.8 for DecisionRAG (57.0 with dense candidates) and to 40.3 for dense retrieval with Qwen3-Reranker; the ordering of systems is unchanged. 

E.2. ConditionalQA under DAPR relevance judgments 

DAPR provides alternative relevance judgments for ConditionalQA, evaluated with nDCG@10 (Wang et al., 2024) (last column of Table 11). On the same 271 questions, DecisionRAG’s nDCG@10 exceeds dense retrieval with Qwen3-Reranker by +5 .3 points (95% interval [1 .3, 9.2] ) and GPT-5 by +4 .0 [0 .3, 7.8] , and its DAPR R@10 exceeds GPT-5 by +8 .2[4 .0, 12 .3] . With dense candidates, nDCG@10 rises to 71.2.

# G. Open-Weight Judge Training 

Model. The open-weight judges are Qwen3-Base models (1.7B, 4B and 8B) whose language-model head is replaced by a scoring head. Each candidate is posed as its own question with the answer options “no” and “yes”, appended to the shared context. All questions start at the position right after the context and cannot attend to each other, so the judgments of different candidates depend only on the shared context, as in Equation (2). The head projects the hidden states of the question’s end token and of each option’s end token into a common space, scales their cosine similarity by a learned inverse temperature (capped at 100), and normalizes over the question’s options with a softmax; P (yes) is the probability of “yes”. Training minimizes the cross-entropy over the options. In the general and MultiDoc2Dial-only checkpoints, the end tokens of a question’s two options can also attend to each other; the LoRA checkpoint does not use this link. 20 Retriever-Free Retrieval: Navigating Documents with Decision Models 

General training. The general models are trained on a mixture of public multiple-choice and classification datasets, including reading comprehension (RACE, QuALITY), science and commonsense question answering (SciQ, ARC, Com-monsenseQA, HellaSwag), natural language inference (MNLI), intent and topic classification, and dialogue, tool-use and web-navigation decisions (MultiWOZ, xLAM, Mind2Web). The label of each example is the dataset’s gold option; the alternatives are its own distractors or, for classification, a random subset of the class labels. The mixture contains no MultiDoc2Dial or ConditionalQA data. 

Retrieval training data. MultiDoc2Dial training examples are generated from its training split with the routing, page and passage stages of the navigation; 3% of training dialogues are held out for monitoring. The generator simulates the notepad with a gold page with probability 0.65, includes the gold page among three candidates with probability 0.85, and adds a second help-center negative stage with probability 0.17; identical-text copies of the gold passage are never used as negatives. ConditionalQA examples are built by the navigation’s own page and paragraph stages, so their inputs match those at inference. A page-stage example lists all 652 pages with “yes” for the gold page; a paragraph-stage example lists every paragraph of five candidate pages with “yes” for the evidence paragraphs. The gold page’s rank among the five candidates is drawn from Jev’s page-stage ranks on the 300 ConditionalQA design questions, and the remaining candidates are the top BM25 pages; when the gold page is not drawn, all paragraphs are negatives. Paragraphs whose exact text appears as evidence on another page are not asked, and questions without evidence contribute only a page-stage example. Questions on 10% of the training pages are held out for monitoring. 

Optimization. The fine-tuning runs on retrieval data use AdamW ( β1 = 0 .9, β2 = 0 .95 , no weight decay), gradient clipping at 1.0, 50 warm-up steps and cosine decay to 10% of the peak learning rate. The MD2D-only model trains the head alone for 200 steps (learning rate 10 −3) and then the full model for one epoch; general → MD2D continues from the general 8B checkpoint for one epoch without the head-only phase. Both use learning rates of 10 −5 (backbone) and 10 −4

(head), a maximum context of 32,768 tokens and packed rows of 40,960 tokens; overlong passage-stage records (5.5%) are dropped rather than truncated. The DecisionRAG (8B trained) model reported in Table 4 instead continues the general 8B model with LoRA (rank 64, α = 128 , on the attention and MLP projections of every layer) and a fully trained head, both at learning rate 10 −4, for one epoch over 30% of the MultiDoc2Dial training dialogues (a seeded sample) and all ConditionalQA training examples, with rows of 65,536 tokens.
