ABSTRACT 

Long-context language models can increasingly process entire documents, mak-ing full-context prompting a practical alternative to passage retrieval. However, greater capacity determines how much information a model can receive, but not which information best supports a query. This distinction motivates rethinking retrieval: when the full document already fits, can selecting and organizing its evi-dence improve answering? The challenge is to retain supporting facts and relations while keeping the context focused on the question. To reconcile these goals, we introduce T REE FUSE , a training-free framework that uses semantic abstraction to guide selection while retaining original text for answering. T REE FUSE constructs a reusable semantic hierarchy from the full document in a single generation, link-ing node descriptions to source spans. Given a query, structural–semantic scoring and fine-grained lexical attribution nominate shared source nodes; their union with ancestor context preserves structural relations and complete local text. Across four frontier answer models, T REE FUSE improves average QA-F1 by 1.72 points over full-context prompting on three LongBench tasks, surpassing the evaluated base-lines on the four-model average while reducing answer-context length by 58.55%. Our code is available at the code repository. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Frontier language models can increasingly accommodate entire documents within their context win-dows, making full-context prompting a practical option for long-document question answering. Yet input capacity and context selection address different questions: how much information a model can receive, and which information it should receive for a particular query. Prior work shows that performance can depend strongly on where relevant information appears within a long context, even when that information remains available to the model (Liu et al., 2024). Prior compression methods show that selecting context can improve answering as well as shorten the input (Jiang et al., 2024a; Chirkova et al., 2025), while LaRA (Li et al., 2025) finds no universally preferable choice between retrieval-based and full-context approaches. These findings motivate rethinking retrieval beyond ac-commodating input-length constraints: When the full document already fits within the context window, can retrieval improve answering, and how should it select the supporting context? 

A relevant passage is not always sufficient to support an answer. A question may require a directly mentioned fact together with background or a relation established elsewhere in the document. Suf-ficient Context (Joren et al., 2025) explicitly distinguishes contextual support from answer correct-ness. Context construction must therefore consider both the support it retains and how selectively it presents that support. Narrow extraction can omit a necessary fact or separate a detail from its interpretation, whereas broad expansion may introduce unrelated material. Controlled studies of arithmetic reasoning show that such irrelevant information can interfere with model predictions (Shi et al., 2023), motivating attention to context concentration as well as support retention. Hierarchi-cal methods such as HiChunk (Lu et al., 2026) and LongRefiner (Jin et al., 2025) make document organization available to retrieval and refinement. The remaining design question is how to use that organization while remaining sensitive to precise source-level details.

Beyond answer scores, source-evidence analy-ses examine completeness and concentration, while component comparisons characterize semantic and lexical selection, hierarchical organization, and single-pass construction. These evaluations ad-dress both parts of our research question, establishing average gains over full-context prompting and examining how the selected contexts retain and organize supporting evidence. To summarize, our contributions are: • We empirically demonstrate that query-adaptive context construction can improve average an-swering performance even when full-document input is feasible, positioning retrieval as a com-plement to long-context capacity. • We introduce reusable semantic trees and dual-granularity selection to coordinate structural sup-port with precise source details, retaining complete local text without method-specific training. • We provide a broad evaluation across four frontier answer-model families and diverse long-document tasks, obtaining a 1.72-point average QA-F1 improvement on the three LongBench tasks with 58.55% less context. We release our code to support research on adaptive context construction: https://anonymous.4open.science/r/treefuse-ECFE .

2 RELATED WORK 

Retrieval and Compression for Long-Context Question Answering LongBench (Bai et al., 2024) and LaRA (Li et al., 2025) evaluate long-context understanding and retrieval-based answer-ing. LongRAG by Jiang et al. (2024b) groups related documents into larger retrieval units to exploit 2Preprint long-context models. A separate LongRAG by Zhao et al. (2024b) jointly addresses global infor-mation and factual details in long-context QA. Compression methods act at different granularities: LongLLMLingua (Jiang et al., 2024a) prunes tokens, Provence (Chirkova et al., 2025) selects sen-tences, and RECOMP (Xu et al., 2024) and BRIEF-Pro (Gu et al., 2026) learn or generate compact contexts. ACC-RAG (Guo et al., 2025) adapts compression rates, while SARA (Jin et al., 2026) combines textual and compressed representations. Sufficient Context (Joren et al., 2025) examines whether supplied information is sufficient for answering. 

Semantic Chunking and Hierarchical Document Indexing Semantic chunkers such as Lum-berChunker (Duarte et al., 2024) and Meta-Chunking (Zhao et al., 2024a) seek coherent boundaries, with libraries such as Chonkie 1 providing multiple implementations. Hierarchical indexes addi-tionally organize relationships among units. RAPTOR (Sarthi et al., 2024) recursively clusters and summarizes passages. TreeRAG (Tao et al., 2025) combines tree-based chunking with bidirectional traversal. SiReRAG (Zhang et al., 2025b) constructs similarity and relatedness trees and combines their nodes in a retrieval pool. Heading-aware indexing (Tinh & Phuong, 2025) exploits existing sections, while HiChunk (Lu et al., 2026) and LongRefiner (Jin et al., 2025) learn specialized com-ponents for hierarchical chunking or refinement. 

Structure-Aware and Hybrid Retrieval BM25 (Robertson & Zaragoza, 2009) and DPR (Karpukhin et al., 2020) provide lexical and learned semantic relevance signals. Contextual Retrieval (Anthropic, 2024) prepends explanatory context to chunks before embedding and BM25 indexing. MemWalker (Chen et al., 2023) interactively navigates summary trees, while PageIndex (Zhang et al., 2025a) uses reasoning-based search over document hierarchies. HiChunk (Lu et al., 2026) ex-pands retrieval hits through Auto-Merge, and LongRefiner (Jin et al., 2025) performs query-aware refinement. DISRetrieval (Chen et al., 2026) combines discourse parsing, LLM-enhanced node rep-resentations, and structure-guided hierarchical retrieval. TREE FUSE approaches retrieval as a complement to long-context capacity, not solely a remedy for limited input length. It targets answer contexts that retain supporting evidence while concentrat-ing on the query, using a reusable semantic hierarchy to guide source-preserving selection. Ap-pendix A.1 provides a detailed comparison with evaluated baselines and selected related methods. 

3 METHOD 

3.1 PROBLEM FORMULATION AND OVERVIEW 

Given a document D and query q, T REE FUSE constructs an answer context C(q, D ) by selecting source-grounded semantic units. The objective is to retain supporting information while limiting content unrelated to the query, even when the full document fits within the model’s context window. Source selection does not create new source evidence; it changes which content is visible to the an-swer model and how that content is organized. T REE FUSE therefore separates the information used to select evidence from the text delivered for answering. A compact semantic view exposes a node’s topic and structural role, while its original text retains details not captured by the description. Se-lecting complete local units preserves the surroundings of a match, and ancestor completion retains structural connections without including every neighboring branch. TREE FUSE combines a reusable hierarchy with query-dependent selection and pruning. Figure 2 outlines the pipeline. First, source-grounded semantic tree construction links node descriptions and hierarchical relations to original source spans (§3.2). Given a query, structural–semantic scoring and fine-grained lexical attribution nominate nodes from this shared representation (§3.3). Their union is completed with ancestor nodes and used to prune the original tree, yielding an answer context that preserves retained structural relations and complete local source text (§3.4). 3.2 SOURCE -G ROUNDED SEMANTIC TREE CONSTRUCTION

The two channels in the dual-granularity selection module of Figure 2 nominate nodes from the shared hierarchy using complementary views. Structural–semantic scoring assesses a node’s rel-evance and supporting role from its descriptions and structural context. Fine-grained lexical at-tribution identifies localized matches in its original text, including entities, values, or phrases not captured by the descriptions. The channels produce node sets independently rather than averaging scores with different scales and meanings. Their selections may overlap: a node can be nominated by either channel or both.

4 EXPERIMENTS 

4.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Implementation. For source-grounded tree construction (§3.2) , we use Qwen3.6-35B-A3B (Qwen Team, 2026a) to generate the semantic hierarchy and node descriptions, and a for-ward source matcher to align generated boundary clues with original document spans. The resulting trees are cached for reuse. For dual-granularity node selection (§3.3) , the semantic channel uses the same Qwen3.6-35B-A3B model to score nodes against the query, while the lexical channel uses node-level BM25 scores obtained by taking the maximum normalized score over each node’s source-text subunits. For structure-preserving pruning (§3.4) , we take the union of the two selec-tions, add ancestor nodes, and prune unselected branches while preserving parent–child links and complete local source text. LongBench receives the resulting tree-structured JSON; LaRA formats the retained nodes as numbered source chunks with ancestor-title provenance. We evaluate these contexts with Qwen3.8-Max (Qwen Team, 2026b), DeepSeek-V4-Pro (DeepSeek AI, 2026), Kimi-K2.6 (Kimi, 2026), and GLM-5.3 (GLM-5-Team et al., 2026), reusing each query’s constructed context across answer models without model-specific fine-tuning and holding it fixed across three answer-generation runs. Construction and selection prompts, selection settings, and input formats are documented in Appendices A.2, A.3, and A.4. 

Benchmarks and metrics. We evaluate context construction across complementary QA settings and document domains, with four frontier answer models testing the reuse of the same selected contexts. 5 CONCLUSION 

We revisited retrieval in the long-context era as a problem of selecting and organizing evidence, rather than merely fitting documents into a limited window. TREE FUSE operationalizes this view through a training-free, reusable semantic hierarchy: abstraction guides selection, while structure-preserving pruning retains original text for answering. Across four frontier answer mod-els, T REE FUSE improves average QA-F1 by 1.72 points over full-context prompting on three Long-Bench tasks with 58.55% less answer context, and achieves the highest four-model average accuracy on LaRA with 43.87% less context. Under the main evaluation settings, all compared retrieval and compression baselines fall below Raw in four-model average performance on each LongBench task and LaRA, whereas T REE FUSE exceeds it. This contrast validates the value of adaptive context construction while showing that input reduction alone does not ensure better answers. The central design principle is to use abstraction to decide what to retain without substituting it for the source evidence. Long-context capacity and retrieval thus address complementary decisions: how much information can be made available, and which source content and relations should be presented for a particular question. Our findings establish the latter as a consequential design choice even when the former no longer prevents full-document input. 

Limitation and Future Work. Our evaluation centers on long-document question answering. Ex-tending T REE FUSE to multimodal documents would require linking textual evidence with tables and figures. Although retained text is source-grounded, selection still depends on generated structure and descriptions. Controlled perturbation studies could quantify this sensitivity and guide more robust tree construction and selection. Future work could also explore other evidence-intensive tasks and incremental tree updates as documents evolve, extending the reusable hierarchy beyond the settings evaluated here. 9Preprint
