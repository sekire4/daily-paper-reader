Abstract 

Optimizing Information Retrieval (IR) pipelines for Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) requires jointly selecting structural components and numerical hyperparame-ters under limited evaluation budgets. We introduce the Information Retrieval Agentic 

Harness (IRAH), which combines Language Agent Tree Search for IR (LATS-IR) with offline meta-learned initialization. LATS-IR performs UCT-guided hierarchical search, first exploring macro-structural pipeline choices and then refining numerical param-eters, with natural-language reflections to guide subsequent mutations and provide inspectable search traces. We evaluate IRAH on three in-distribution benchmarks and one out-of-distribution benchmark to assess generalization under distribution shift. Under the same meta-learned initialization, LATS-IR outperforms TPE on two of three in-distribution benchmarks, with gains of up to 6.4% , and remains competitive on the out-of-distribution benchmark. Across all four benchmarks, warm-started IRAH achieves the highest average held-out nDCG@10 (0.4816 )and improves over its cold-start counterpart. These results establish IRAH as a competitive approach to retrieval pipeline optimization under constrained evaluation budgets. 

1 Introduction 

Modern retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) sys-tems rely on retrieval pipelines whose effectiveness is shaped by interdependent design choices (Lewis et al., 2020; Hsia et al., 2025). These include how documents are chunked, which dense encoder is used, whether sparse and dense scores are fused, which reranker is applied, and how numerical pa-rameters such as fusion weight and retrieval depth are set. A chunking strategy changes the distribu-tion of candidate passages seen by the embedder;

reranking can compensate for some retrieval er-rors while amplifying others; and the best retrieval depth depends on both the underlying corpus and the evaluation objective (Hsia et al., 2025). As a result, RAG pipeline optimization is better viewed as a structured configuration-search problem than as isolated tuning of individual hyperparameters. Existing automated optimization methods reduce manual tuning burden (Akiba et al., 2019), but general-purpose black-box optimization does not explicitly exploit the macro–micro structure of re-trieval pipeline decisions. Recent work on RAG hy-perparameter optimization demonstrates the value of structured search in large configuration spaces (Fu et al., 2024). However, existing approaches do not explicitly organize optimization as a hier-archical search trajectory in which structural and numerical decisions are explored at different stages and prior outcomes inform subsequent proposals. Moreover, many hyperparameter optimization al-gorithms provide limited visibility into the opti-mization process (Moosbauer et al., 2022). For IR pipelines, inefficient exploration can also incur substantial systems costs when structural changes require constructing or loading new indices. Start-ing optimization from scratch for each new cor-pus further discards potentially useful information from prior retrieval tasks. These limitations mo-tivate an approach that can exploit the structure of the configuration space, adapt its search based on previous evaluations, transfer useful experience across datasets, and retain an inspectable optimiza-tion trace. We introduce the Information Retrieval Agentic 

Harness (IRAH) (Figure 1), which combines LATS-IR, a reflection-guided hierarchical search process, with offline meta-learned initialization. 2 Related Work 

Retrieval-Augmented Generation and Pipeline Design. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) grounds generation in externally retrieved evidence (Lewis et al., 2020), with performance depending on interacting choices in preprocessing, chunking, retrieval, reranking, and generation (Gao et al., 2023). Systems such as DSPy treat language-model applications as compositional pipelines whose com-ponents can be optimized (Khattab et al., 2024). We focus on retrieval configuration, where struc-tural and numerical choices jointly affect effective-ness and systems costs such as index construction and reuse. 

Hyperparameter and Structured RAG Op-timization. General-purpose methods such as Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (TPE) (Bergstra et al., 2011; Akiba et al., 2019) and AutoML un-der the CASH formulation (Thornton et al., 2013; 2Feurer et al., 2015) automate configuration search from observed evaluations, but do not explicitly encode the retrieval-specific hierarchy considered here. Recent RAG optimization methods increasingly exploit search-space structure. Fu et al. (2024) use a two-level hierarchical bandit; Afzal et al. (2026) compare global and component-wise Bayesian op-timization; and Chen and Chen (2026) use cyclic sequential optimization of retriever and generator parameters. Language models have also been used as iterative optimizers that condition proposals on prior outcomes (Yang et al., 2024). In contrast, LATS-IR organizes structural and coarse numer-ical exploration separately from local numerical refinement while maintaining an explicit search tree over retrieval configurations. 

Tree Search and Language-Agent Reflection. 

Monte Carlo Tree Search and UCT balance ex-ploitation and exploration (Kocsis and Szepesvári, 2006; Browne et al., 2012). Language Agent Tree Search (LATS) extends tree search to language-model agents using generation and self-reflection (Zhou et al., 2023). LATS-IR adapts these ideas to retrieval configuration optimization: nodes are evaluated pipeline configurations, edges are phase-constrained mutations, UCT guides selection, and reflections condition subsequent candidate genera-tion. 

Meta-Learning for Warm Starts. Meta-learning transfers information across tasks to improve learning or optimization (Vanschoren, 2019). AutoML systems use dataset meta-features and prior evaluations to identify promising initial configurations (Feurer et al., 2015). IRAH applies this idea to retrieval optimization by using an offline meta-learner to rank shared anchor configurations and supply the highest-ranked candidates as warm starts for LATS-IR. 

3 IRAH: Information Retrieval Agentic Harness 

In this section, we introduce IRAH, a framework for retrieval configuration optimization. IRAH cen-ters on LATS-IR, a reflection-guided hierarchical search procedure, and uses offline meta-learning to provide warm-start initialization. We formalize the hierarchical configuration problem (Section 3.1), then describe LATS-IR (Section 3.2), and finally introduce the meta-learned warm-start mechanism (Section 3.3). 

3.1 Preliminaries 

We formulate information retrieval (IR) configura-tion optimization as a hierarchical configuration-search problem, extending the Combined Algo-rithm Selection and Hyperparameter Optimization (CASH) paradigm (Thornton et al., 2013; Feurer et al., 2015) to modular retrieval pipelines used in RAG systems (Gao et al., 2023; Khattab et al., 2024). Rather than treating all pipeline decisions as an undifferentiated set of parameters, we dis-tinguish between macro-structural decisions and 

micro-level numerical parameters. A candidate con-figuration is c = ( m, θ), where m ∈ M denotes categorical structural choices, such as the chunk-ing strategy or embedding model, and θ ∈ Θ( m)

denotes numerical parameters associated with that structure, such as hybrid fusion weight α.This hierarchy reflects both the dependencies and computational costs of retrieval pipeline op-timization. Numerical settings are tuned within the context of a particular pipeline structure, while changing structural choices such as the chunking strategy or embedding model can require construct-ing a new physical index. In contrast, parameters such as retrieval depth or fusion weight can often be varied while reusing an existing index. Prior RAG optimization work similarly demonstrates benefits from structured or component-wise search (Fu et al., 2024; Afzal et al., 2026; Chen and Chen, 2026). We therefore define the configuration space as 

S = {(m, θ) : m ∈ M , θ ∈ Θ( m)} .

3.2 LATS-IR: Language Agent Tree Search for Information Retrieval 

LATS-IR is our optimization engine. It adapts Language Agent Tree Search (LATS) (Zhou et al., 2023) to the hierarchical IR configuration prob-lem defined in Section 3.1. The hierarchy is im-posed through the search procedure: tree nodes represent fully specified retrieval configurations, while phase-constrained edges first permit macro-structural changes and later restrict search to lo-cal numerical refinement. LATS-IR can operate from an uninformed cold start with agent-generated initial configurations, or from configurations sup-plied by the meta-learned warm-start mechanism described in Section 3.3. For a warm start, the sup-plied seeds are evaluated and the highest-reward seed initializes the tree root. At each tree expansion, LATS-IR (1) selects a previously evaluated node, (2) generates K valid candidate mutations conditioned on the active search phase, and (3) evaluates the candidates on 

Qopt . The candidates are added as sibling children of the selected parent, and each reward is backprop-agated through its tree path to update visit counts and empirical value estimates. This process repeats until the evaluation budget Nmax is exhausted. Al-gorithm 1 summarizes LATS-IR. 

UCT-Guided Node Selection. Each evaluated configuration produces a retrieval reward rt, which is backpropagated along the selected tree path. For each node on this path, we increment its visit count and update its empirical value ¯V (c) as the mean reward backpropagated through that node. Conse-quently, ancestor nodes summarize the observed performance of configurations explored within their descendant branches. Nodes are selected for expansion using Upper Confidence Bounds for Trees (UCT) (Kocsis and Szepesvári, 2006):

Two-Phase Hierarchical Search. LATS-IR di-vides optimization into a macro phase centered on structural exploration followed by micro-level numerical refinement, exploiting the structure of the configuration space S under a limited evalua-tion budget. In Phase 1 , candidate mutations may modify structural components m, including the embedding model, chunking strategy, and reranker, together with coarse numerical settings. The goal is to explore alternative retrieval architectures and identify a promising structural region of the search space. At the phase transition, we select the Phase 1 configuration with the highest optimization reward, 

c(1) ∗ = ( m∗, θ(1) ∗), and fix its categorical struc-tural choices m∗. Phase 2 then refines the nu-merical parameters within a local neighborhood 

Θlocal (c(1) ∗) ⊆ Θ( m∗) centered on the Phase 1 configuration. This separation prevents the search 4from revisiting alternative macro-architectures dur-ing refinement and concentrates the remaining bud-get on fine-grained optimization of a promising structural configuration. 

Reflection-Guided Candidate Generation. 

Candidate mutations are generated by an LLM conditioned on the selected parent configuration, its observed reward, relevant prior reflections, the active search phase, and the valid parameter choices or numerical bounds for that phase. For each expansion, the LLM proposes K structured child configurations, which are validated against the allowable search space before evaluation. After each evaluation, the agent produces a short reflection comparing the evaluated configuration with its parent and, when available, their observed reward difference. The reflection is retained in search memory and supplied as context for subsequent candidate generation, allowing later mutations to incorporate information from earlier successes and failures. Complete prompting, validation, and output-schema details are provided in Appendix A.2.3. 

3.3 Meta-Learned Warm-Start Initialization 

To mitigate cold-start overhead, we construct an offline meta-learner fmeta from configuration eval-uations collected across multiple source retrieval datasets, following meta-learning approaches in AutoML (Feurer et al., 2015; Vanschoren, 2019). For an IR dataset D = ( C, Q), comprising corpus 

C and query set Q, we extract a dataset-level rep-resentation ϕ(D) ∈ Rd capturing corpus, query, and query-corpus characteristics. Each candidate retrieval configuration c is represented by configu-ration features ψ(c) ∈ Rp.Given a dataset-configuration pair, the meta-learner produces a score indicating the predicted utility of that configuration for the dataset: 

smeta (c, D) = fmeta ([ ϕ(D); ψ(c)]) . (3) Let A = {c1, . . . , cA} denote the set of an-chor configurations evaluated during offline meta-training. For an unseen target dataset Dtarget , we score each anchor configuration and select the top-

B ranked candidates: 

Cseed = Top B (A; smeta (·, Dtarget )) , (4) where Top B returns the B anchor configurations with the highest predicted scores. The resulting seed set Cseed provides the starting configurations for warm-started LATS-IR, transferring promising configurations from prior retrieval tasks. When 

Cseed = ∅, the agent instead generates cold-start configurations; otherwise, the meta-learned seed configurations initialize the search. Full details of the source benchmarks, anchor configuration set, meta-feature construction, and meta-learner train-ing and selection are provided in Appendix A.1. 

4 Experimental Setup 

We evaluate IRAH on four retrieval benchmarks spanning diverse domains. We compare warm- and cold-started variants of IRAH and Tree-structured Parzen Estimator TPE, together with Grid Search, using a shared retrieval engine and evaluation proto-col. We optimize all methods using nDCG@10 on an optimization-query split and report performance on a held-out query set. Additional implementation details are in Appendix A.

4.2 IRAH Implementation 

We instantiate IRAH as follows. For warm-start initialization, we use an XGBRanker meta-learner trained on 11 source retrieval bench-marks using performance measurements from a 36-configuration anchor set. Each dataset– configuration pair is represented by 13 dataset-level meta-features and 14 configuration features. At inference, XGBRanker scores the 36 anchor con-figurations for the target dataset and returns the top B = 3 configurations for warm-start initial-ization. Complete meta-training details, including the source benchmarks, feature definitions, anchor configurations, and model-selection procedure, are provided in Appendix A.1. The downstream search space spans four chunking strategies (none , token , sentence ,and recursive_character ); three dense em-bedding models ( multi-qa-mpnet-base-cos-v1 ,

bge-large-en-v1.5 , and bge-m3 ); three cross-encoder rerankers plus a no-reranking option; and numerical parameters controlling chunk size, re-trieval depth k, and hybrid lexical–dense fusion weight α. Phase 1 searches over pipeline struc-ture together with coarse numerical values k ∈{10 , 20 , 30 , 50 } and α ∈ { 0.00 , 0.33 , 0.67 , 1.00 }.Phase 2 fixes the selected categorical architecture and performs localized refinement of chunk size, 

k, and α. Complete search-space specifications and Phase 2 refinement bounds are provided in Appendix A.2.1. The fixed search budget ( Nmax = 50 ) is divided between macro-structural exploration and micro-level refinement using a phase-allocation fraction 

γ. Warm-started runs on the in-distribution bench-marks use γ = 0 .24 , allocating 12 of 50 eval-uations to Phase 1 and 38 to Phase 2, whereas cold-started runs use γ = 0 .60 (30 and 20 evalu-ations, respectively). To reduce reliance on trans-ferred configurations under substantial distribution shift, warm-started targets with dmeta > 1.5 use the cold-start allocation. We treat this threshold as a heuristic experimental decision rule rather than a calibrated OOD boundary. Thus, warm-started in-distribution search devotes more of its budget to local refinement, while cold-started and OOD search retain greater structural exploration. Node selection follows the adaptive UCT pro-cedure described in Section 3.2. Reflection and candidate mutation are performed using 

openai/gpt-oss-120b (OpenAI, 2025), and gen-erated candidates are validated against the phase-specific search-space constraints before evaluation. Additional UCT, prompting, and search-memory details are provided in Appendices A.2.2 and A.2.3.

4.4 Evaluation Metrics 

The optimization objective for all methods is 

nDCG@10 computed over Qopt . After optimiza-tion, the configuration selected by each run is eval-uated on the corresponding held-out query set, and we report held-out nDCG@10 as the retrieval per-formance metric. We additionally report optimization-loop run-time, measured as the wall-clock time attributed to each method’s search and evaluation procedure. Because structural configuration changes can re-quire distinct physical retrieval indexes, we also analyze the number of unique index collections constructed during optimization. For IRAH, we further report LLM token usage as a measure of agentic optimization overhead.

Out-of-Distribution Generalization We further evaluate transfer to CoSQA , our OOD benchmark. Although all four evaluation benchmarks are held out from meta-learner training, CoSQA lies substan-tially farther from the meta-training distribution, with dmeta = 2 .092 (Table 1). Consistent with this larger distribution shift, the meta-learner’s top-ranked anchor configuration achieves only 0.2593 

held-out nDCG@10 on CoSQA (Appendix B.2), substantially below its direct transfer performance on the in-distribution targets. Despite this weaker transfer signal, meta-learned initialization benefits downstream search. IRAH (Warm) improves from 0.3088 to 0.3336 

held-out nDCG@10 over IRAH (Cold), a gain of 

0.0248 (8.0% ). Although TPE (Warm) achieves the highest score on this benchmark ( 0.3420 ), IRAH (Warm) remains competitive and substan-tially improves over its cold-start counterpart. This suggests that LATS-IR can recover strong perfor-mance from weaker meta-learned initialization, and that transferred configuration information can re-main useful under distribution shift even when di-rect meta-learner recommendations are less reli-able.

Method runtime varies across datasets. As shown in Table 3, IRAH (Warm) is the fastest method on 

SciFact and CQADupStack-STATS , while TPE is faster on CoSQA . On SciFact , IRAH (Warm) re-duces method runtime by approximately 50% rela-tive to Grid Search. FiQA-2018 incurs substantially greater runtime for IRAH (Warm). Appendix B.4 further analyzes this behavior using physical in-dex usage and pure search/evaluation time, show-ing that the increase is not explained by collection count alone.

6 Conclusion 

We introduced IRAH, an agentic framework for IR configuration optimization that combines LATS-IR, a reflection-guided hierarchical search procedure, with meta-learned warm-start initial-ization. A key aspect is that LATS-IR orga-nizes search into macro-structural exploration fol-lowed by local numerical refinement, with natural-language reflections to inform subsequent muta-tions and provide an inspectable trace. Across all four benchmarks, warm-started IRAH achieves the highest average held-out nDCG@10 (0.4816 ) and outperforms its cold-start counterpart. These re-sults show that hierarchical agentic search with meta-learned warm starts is a competitive approach 8to retrieval pipeline optimization under constrained evaluation budgets. 

Limitations 

Our evaluation covers four target benchmarks and one retrieval backend. Although these benchmarks span scientific, financial, community-question, and code-retrieval domains, broader evaluation across retrieval systems, larger collections, and additional domains is needed to establish how consistently IRAH’s search behavior generalizes. LATS-IR relies on an LLM for reflection and candidate mutation throughout optimization. Our experiments use only gpt-oss-120b and we do not evaluate sensitivity to the choice of language model. This incurs inference overhead, consuming roughly 0.6 million tokens per 50-trial run. Future work could investigate smaller specialized models or distilled reflection policies while preserving the inspectability of the search trajectory. Finally, warm-start effectiveness depends on how well the meta-training benchmarks represent the target distribution. Our OOD experiment shows that meta-learned initialization can remain useful under distribution shift, though direct-transfer qual-ity is weaker than for the in-distribution targets. Ex-panding the diversity of meta-training datasets and incorporating uncertainty into warm-start selection are important directions for improving robustness.

A.1 Meta-Learner Instantiation 

To construct the warm-start prior, we evaluate three offline meta-learning models across 11 public IR benchmarks (Appendix A.1.1) using Group K-Fold cross-validation grouped by dataset ID to prevent cross-domain leakage. Offline training data are generated by evaluating 36 candidate re-trieval configurations on each source dataset (Ap-pendix A.1.2). Each dataset is represented by 13 corpus- and query-level meta-features (Appendix A.1.3), which are concatenated with 14 configuration-specific fea-tures to form a 27-dimensional representation for each dataset–configuration pair. The meta-learner is trained to score candidate configurations accord-ing to the target retrieval metric (i.e., nDCG @10 ), aligning predicted seed configurations with the downstream optimization objective. Among the evaluated models, XGBRanker 

achieves one of the highest out-of-fold Spearman-ρ

correlations and is selected as the final meta-learner (Appendix A.1.4). It is subsequently retrained us-ing the full 11-dataset meta-training corpus. For an unseen target dataset at inference time, the system extracts its 13-dimensional meta-feature represen-tation, scores the 36 candidate anchor configura-tions, and returns the top-3 ranked configurations for warm-start initialization.

A.2.3 Reflection and Mutation Implementation 

At each evaluated node, LATS-IR uses an LLM-based reflection and mutation procedure to inform subsequent candidate generation. After evaluat-ing a configuration, the reflection prompt provides the model with the evaluated configuration, its ob-served retrieval scores, the branch trajectory lead-ing to the node, and the rationale associated with the proposed configuration. For non-root nodes, the trajectory includes the parent configuration and reward together with the observed parent-relative outcome; root nodes are treated as initial expan-sions. The reflection prompt follows the template:          

> You are evaluating an IR retrieval configuration for a search optimization experiment.
> Config: {embedding}, {chunker}, α={α},k=
> {k}, {reranker}.
> Scores: {metric values}.
> Search branch that led here: {branch trajectory and prior critiques}.
> Rationale: {candidate-generation rationale}. Write exactly two sentences: (1) summarize what the scores suggest about the configuration, and (2) suggest what the next expansion should try or avoid.

The resulting reflection is stored with the search node and can be reused as context during later can-didate generation. Mutation prompts condition on the UCT-selected branch, previous evaluations, the active search phase, and the valid choices or bounds for that phase. The shared context includes:      

> Branch trajectory: {selected root-to-leaf path with reflections}.
> Trial history: {previously evaluated configura-tions and scores}.
> Observed patterns: {summary of prior out-comes}.
> Objective: {optimization objective}.
> Target score: {target reward}.

During Phase 1, the prompt permits both struc-tural mutations and coarse numerical changes:     

> Phase 1: Structural and Coarse Numerical Ex-ploration. Explore combinations of chunking strategy, embedding model, and reranker, together with the allowed coarse chunk-size, α, and kval-ues. Use only the valid choices provided for this phase, and do not intentionally repeat an exact configuration from the trial history.

During Phase 2, the selected categorical struc-ture is held fixed and the prompt restricts proposals to local numerical refinement:  

> Phase 2: Local Numerical Refinement. Keep the selected chunking strategy, embedding model,

15 and reranker fixed. Refine α within the active local window, vary k near the selected value, and adjust chunk size within the allowed range. Avoid previously tried Phase 2 configurations. 

Candidate configurations are emitted through a structured tool-call interface rather than free-form text:    

> propose_candidate_eve(embedding_model, reranker_model, alpha, top_k, chunk_strategy, chunk_size, chunk_overlap, reasoning)

After proposing the requested candidates, the model issues a finalization call. Each proposed configuration is validated against the enumerated model choices, chunking recipes, and numerical bounds before retrieval evaluation. Invalid tool ar-guments are rejected and may be corrected within the bounded tool-call budget. 

Implementation constants. Each expansion re-quests K = 3 candidate configurations. The pro-posal agent is allowed up to six tool-call iterations, permitting invalid tool arguments to be corrected before finalization. Invalid configurations are not passed to retrieval evaluation. Stochastic optimiza-tion runs use a maximum budget of 50 evaluated configurations and three random seeds. 

Duplicate handling. The candidate-generation prompt includes the prior trial history and explic-itly instructs the model not to repeat previously evaluated configurations. Duplicate rejection is not additionally enforced as a hard constraint by the proposal tool. All reflection and mutation calls use

A.3 Optimization Baselines 

We benchmark IRAH against two standard opti-mization baselines, a fixed non-exhaustive Grid Search and Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (TPE) (Bergstra et al., 2011) optimization implemented with Optuna (Akiba et al., 2019). The Grid Search evaluates 108 predefined pipeline configurations spanning 18 physical in-dex collections. Each collection is determined by a specific dense embedding model and chunking con-figuration, while reranker choice, retrieval depth 

k, and fusion weight α vary across configurations. The complete specification of all 108 configura-tions is provided in Table 16. TPE is evaluated under the same 50-trial bud-get as IRAH and searches over the same pipeline components, including chunking strategy, embed-ding model, reranker, retrieval depth k, and fusion weight α. Categorical choices follow those listed in Table 8, while numerical parameters are sam-pled over their allowed ranges: k ∈ [10 , 50] and 

α ∈ [0 .0, 1.0] . Cold-started TPE begins without meta-learned initialization, whereas TPE (Warm) receives the same meta-learned seed initialization used by IRAH (Warm). 

A.4 Retrieval Engine and Evaluation Protocol 

All candidate retrieval configurations are evaluated using the same managed enterprise retrieval engine. Retrieval depth k denotes the number of retrieved passage chunks rather than aggregated documents. The base search grid uses k ∈ { 10 , 20 , 30 , 50 },while optimization methods may propose addi-tional values within their method-specific numeri-cal bounds (Section A.2.1). Hybrid lexical-semantic retrieval is controlled by a fusion weight α ∈ [0 , 1] , where α = 0 corre-sponds to pure lexical BM25 retrieval and α = 1 

corresponds to pure dense vector retrieval; interme-diate values combine the two retrieval signals.
