# Variational Cognitive Memory Abstraction for Long-Horizon Vision-Language-Action Models 

Xinyu Liu, Daiying Tian, Chenwei Tang, Ruyang Han, Chengyang Zhang, Qingyu Zeng, Wei Ju, Liangli Zhen, and Jiancheng Lv 

Abstract —Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have shown remarkable success in robotic manipulation, yet their ability to execute long-horizon tasks remains limited by insufficient long-term memory modeling. Existing methods either rely on implicit temporal representations within policy networks, where historical information can be gradually diluted, or maintain explicit memory buffers that suffer from redundancy, noise accumulation, and inefficient retrieval. In this paper, we propose CogMemory, a novel variational cognitive memory framework that enables VLA systems to selectively preserve and reuse task-relevant historical information. Our approach encodes multimodal trajectories into compact latent memory representations through a selective state-space memory encoder, multi-slot temporal abstraction, and variational information compression. During action generation, dynamic cross-attention retrieval with gated fusion adaptively incorporates relevant memories into policy prediction. With merely 3.45M additional parameters, CogMemory stays lightweight and seamlessly fits both early- and intermediate-fusion VLA backbones. Extensive experiments on long-horizon robotic manipulation benchmarks demonstrate consistent improvements across diverse VLA backbones. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Recent advances in Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have significantly improved robotic manipulation through large-scale multimodal pretraining [1]–[3]. Existing VLA systems broadly follow two predominant fusion paradigms, as illustrated in Figure 1. Early-fusion architectures [4]–[6] jointly model vision, language, and actions within unified transformers, yet they lack explicit mechanisms for retaining long-range history—task-relevant cues from early steps are progressively diluted as interaction horizons grow, causing severe perfor-mance degradation in multi-stage tasks. Intermediate-fusion approaches , e.g., diffusion [7] and flow-based [8] policies [9]– [11], integrate VLM representations into continuous action generators, but they still rely on implicit temporal modeling within policy networks, where historical information is only passively aggregated through sequential updates. This implicit aggregation remains vulnerable to noise, redundancy, and the gradual decay of early critical signals, making it difficult to preserve long-term dependencies or maintain temporally consistent decisions across extended executions. VLA models have recently advanced general robotic ma-nipulation by combining large vision-language models [15]– [17] with large-scale robot datasets [18]–[20]. Existing VLA approaches formulate robot control as multimodal sequence modeling, where visual observations, language instructions, and robot states are jointly encoded for action prediction. Representative methods like RT-series models [4], [21], Open-VLA [5], Octo [6], π0 [9], CogACT [10], and FLOWER [11], have explored different architectures for representation learning and continuous robot action generation. Despite their strong performance on individual manipulation tasks, long-horizon robotic control remains challenging due to the difficulty of preserving and exploiting relevant historical information over extended interactions. Recent works have introduced explicit memory mechanisms into robotic policies. RoboFlamingo [22] propagates compressed vision-language representations through recurrent states, while TraceVLA [23] and Interleave-VLA [24] encode interaction histories into visual or multimodal sequences. UniVLA [25] incorporates previous actions for temporal reasoning, and MemoryVLA [14] introduces an external memory retrieval mechanism for cognition-guided action generation. However, existing approaches either rely on implicit temporal accumulation or directly cache historical features, which may introduce redundancy and limit scalability for long-horizon manipulation. In contrast, CogMemory ex-plicitly abstracts interaction histories into compact variational memory representations and adaptively retrieves task-relevant information for long-horizon decision making. III. M ETHOD 

As illustrated in Figure 2, given multimodal representations extracted from a VLA backbone, CogMemory introduces a two-stage cognitive memory pipeline: Memory Abstraction 

and Memory-guided Action Generation . The abstraction stage sequentially compresses long-horizon interaction histories into compact latent memory slots through three core operations, i.e., i) selective state-space modeling (SSM) for long-range temporal encoding, ii) multi-slot temporal pooling for structured memory consolidation, and iii) variational information compression (VIC) for learning informative and noise-robust latent memory distributions. The action generation stage then retrieves task-relevant memory via cross-attention and adaptively fuses it with current representations through gated fusion, enabling temporally coherent and context-aware decision making. Formally, let X = [ x1, x 2, . . . , x L] ∈ RL×D denote the multimodal representations extracted from the VLA backbone, where L is the history length and D is the feature dimension. Depending on the backbone architecture, X may correspond to multimodal token representations from VLM outputs (e.g., 

π0-style architectures) or intermediate representations extracted from early fusion layers (e.g., FLOWER-style architectures). 

CogMemory transforms X into a structured latent memory representation M ∈ RK×D , where K ≪ L denotes the number of memory slots. During policy inference, task-relevant historical information is dynamically retrieved from M and adaptively integrated into action generation for long-horizon decision making.

In this section, we conduct extensive experiments to evaluate the effectiveness of CogMemory for long-horizon robotic manipulation on two widely used long-horizon robotic ma-nipulation benchmarks: LIBERO [38] and CALVIN [39]. LIBERO contains 130 language-conditioned manipulation tasks organized into five suites covering spatial manipulation, object interaction, goal-conditioned control, and long-horizon task composition. CALVIN evaluates language-conditioned manip-ulation through sequential compositions of 34 manipulation primitives with over 380 language annotations across multi-ple environment splits. Together, these benchmarks provide complementary evaluations of long-horizon manipulation, task composition, and language-conditioned control. Our evaluation is designed to address the following three Research Questions (RQs): RQ1: Can CogMemory improve long-horizon manipulation performance while maintaining overall task execution capability? RQ2: Can CogMemory 

improve sequential task completion over extended horizons and maintain effectiveness under unseen environment transfer? RQ3: How do different design components and memory slot capacities affect the performance of CogMemory ? All models are trained following the standard benchmark protocols with trajectories sampled at 10 Hz. The proposed memory module introduces 3.45M additional parameters while enabling end-to-end optimization with the underlying VLA policy. C. Ablation Study (RQ3) 

We conduct ablation studies on the CALVIN D →D bench-mark using the PaliGemma-based backbone to analyze the contribution of individual components and the effect of memory slot count in CogMemory . Following the same protocol as the main CALVIN experiments, all variants are fine-tuned on the official training split for 30k steps and evaluated on 1,000 instruction chains. We report success rates at each chain 7

length along with Avg. Len.. Table III presents the results. For component ablations, we consider three variants: “w/o Memory” removes the entire memory abstraction and retrieval module, reverting to the original backbone for action generation; “w/o VIC” replaces variational latent memory modeling with deterministic feature encoding, omitting distribution sampling and KL regularization; “w/o Gate” removes the adaptive gating mechanism and directly injects retrieved memory features without fusion control. Removing the memory module causes substantial degradation across all chain lengths, with the gap widening as the horizon grows (e.g., LH-5 drops by over 11%), confirming that explicit memory abstraction is critical for preserving task-relevant historical information in sequential manipulation. Replacing VIC with deterministic encoding leads to the largest degradation on long-horizon chains, indicating that variational compression is essential for learning compact and noise-robust latent representations. Removing the gating mechanism also consistently reduces performance, suggesting that adaptive fusion—enabling dynamic regulation of memory utilization conditioned on current states—is important for effective integration of historical context. For the slot count ablation, we evaluate configurations with K = 4 , 8, and 16 

memory slots. The best performance is achieved with K = 8 ,suggesting that a moderate number of compact latent slots provides the optimal trade-off between memory compactness and long-horizon task execution capability. 

D. Qualitative Analysis 

As shown in Figure 3, to further understand how CogMemory improves long-horizon manipulation, we provide qualitative comparisons on representative sequential tasks. The baseline policy frequently fails in such scenarios, often completing early sub-tasks but failing on subsequent steps due to the gradual dilution of task-relevant historical context, a direct manifes-tation of the implicit temporal modeling limitation discussed earlier. In contrast, CogMemory consistently completes the full sequences, demonstrating that compact latent memory slots effectively preserve critical historical cues (e.g., “stove is on” or “drawer is open”) across extended horizons. The selective retrieval and gated fusion mechanisms enable the policy to recall past states on demand, reducing error accumulation and improving robustness under sequential manipulation settings. These qualitative results complement our quantitative findings, further validating that explicit cognitive memory abstraction supports temporally coherent decision-making in long-horizon robotic tasks. D. LIBERO Benchmark 

We evaluate CogMemory on the LIBERO benchmark [38], a large-scale benchmark designed for evaluating long-horizon and language-conditioned robotic manipulation. LIBERO is built upon the BDDL task specification framework and focuses on compositional generalization across diverse household manipulation scenarios. The benchmark is implemented in the MuJoCo simulator using a Franka Panda robot equipped with a parallel gripper. LIBERO consists of five evaluation suites with increasing levels of difficulty and compositional complexity: LIBERO-Spatial , LIBERO-Object , LIBERO-Goal , LIBERO-Long-10 , and 

LIBERO-Long-90 . The Spatial, Object, Goal, and Long-10 suites each contain 10 tasks, while Long-90 contains 90 long-horizon tasks. The benchmark includes a diverse set of tabletop manipulation behaviors such as object picking, placing, opening, closing, rearrangement, drawer interaction, and multi-stage task execution. The Spatial suite evaluates generalization across different object positions and scene layouts, requiring policies to handle spatial variation while preserving task semantics. The Object suite focuses on unseen object combinations and evaluates object-level compositional generalization. The Goal suite further increases difficulty by introducing diverse goal configurations and longer manipulation sequences. In contrast, the Long-10 and Long-90 suites emphasize persistent temporal reasoning and long-horizon sequential manipulation, where policies must maintain consistent task progress across multiple interaction stages. Each task is paired with natural language instructions describing the manipulation objective. Following the standard LIBERO protocol, demonstrations are collected using scripted policies and teleoperation trajectories. The benchmark provides RGB observations, proprioceptive robot states, and language annotations for policy training. E. CALVIN Benchmark 

We further evaluate CogMemory on the CALVIN bench-mark [39], a widely used benchmark for long-horizon language-conditioned robotic manipulation. CALVIN is designed to evaluate sequential decision making, temporal consistency, and generalization under multi-step instruction chaining. The bench-mark is built in a simulated tabletop household environment using a Franka Emika robot equipped with RGB cameras and proprioceptive sensing. CALVIN contains a large collection of manipulation tra-jectories generated from diverse interaction primitives, in-cluding opening and closing drawers, sliding doors, rotating switches, pressing buttons, moving blocks, and object relocation. The environment includes multiple articulated objects and interactive scene elements, resulting in complex long-horizon task dependencies. Policies receive RGB observations, robot proprioceptive states, and free-form language instructions as input. A key characteristic of CALVIN is its long-horizon eval-uation protocol. Instead of evaluating single-task execution, policies are required to sequentially complete multiple language instructions within a single rollout episode. Evaluation is performed over 1,000 instruction chains, where each chain contains five consecutive manipulation tasks sampled from the benchmark task set. A policy succeeds at chain length LH-n if it correctly completes the first n tasks in the instruction sequence without failure. The benchmark additionally reports the average completed chain length (Avg. Len.), which measures long-horizon task consistency and persistent temporal reasoning capability. CALVIN provides two standard evaluation settings: ABC →Dand D →D. In the ABC →D setting, policies are trained on 11 

environments A/B/C and evaluated on the unseen environment D, emphasizing cross-environment generalization and zero-shot transfer capability. In contrast, D →D evaluates policies within the same environment distribution, focusing primarily on long-horizon sequential manipulation performance. The ABC →D protocol is generally considered substantially more challenging due to distribution shift in scene layouts, object appearances, and environmental configurations.
