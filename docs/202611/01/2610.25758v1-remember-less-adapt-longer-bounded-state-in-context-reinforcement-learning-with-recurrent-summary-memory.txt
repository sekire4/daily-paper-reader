## ABSTRACT 

In-context reinforcement learning (ICRL) enables agents to adapt to new tasks without parameter updates, relying on memory to retain what they learn from ex-perience. For agents operating continually, existing approaches using memory trade off bounded cost against access to past experience. Approaches using grow-ing memories, such as full-history attention and accumulating summaries, incur storage and per-decision costs that increase with task length. Fixed-size memo-ries bound storage but restrict access: sliding windows discard older steps, while recurrent networks compress even recent interactions into a hidden state. We in-troduce Recurrent Summary Memory (RSM), a Transformer policy that attends exactly to a short working buffer of recent interactions and carries a fixed-capacity summary of earlier experience. When the buffer fills, RSM rewrites the summary from its previous contents and the buffer, then clears the buffer, so deployment memory and per-decision cost stay O(1) in task length. On long-running partially observable tasks, frozen RSM policies sustain their adapted performance for eight times the training horizon with about 1% of full-history memory, ending ahead of every evaluated baseline while growing memories deteriorate. When the task configuration changes mid-run, RSM re-adapts better than growing and recurrent memories. Clearing the summary or blocking the policy’s access to it removes its benefit, while swapping in another task’s summary steers behaviour towards that task. Together, these results show that our fixed-capacity summary can sustain in-context adaptation far beyond the training horizon. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

An agent placed in an unfamiliar environment often cannot directly observe everything it needs to act, so it must use past experience to inform its decisions and adapt to the environment (Kaelbling et al., 1998). In Dark Key-to-Door (Laskin et al., 2023), for example, an agent that searches a dark room for a hidden key and then a door should reuse the key’s location on every later attempt; in a maze with unfamiliar controls, an inferred mapping from actions to movements should support nav-igation throughout the task. In-context reinforcement learning (ICRL) trains policies to adapt in this way: a single policy is trained across tasks to condition its decisions on its history of observations, actions, and rewards, enabling adaptation without test-time parameter updates (Laskin et al., 2023; Lee et al., 2023; Grigsby et al., 2024). Because its parameters remain fixed, memory must carry the task knowledge acquired during deployment (Duan et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2016). For an agent that keeps operating, continued adaptation therefore depends on preserving useful discoveries. Existing memory architectures trade off bounded cost against access to experience. Full-history Transformers (Parisotto et al., 2020; Grigsby et al., 2024) let a policy revisit individual records, but their key/value caches and per-decision attention costs grow with task duration. Sliding windows bound these costs by discarding older records, even when their information remains useful (Xiao et al., 2024). Recurrent networks such as GRUs (Cho et al., 2014) maintain a fixed-size hidden state and provide strong baselines under partial observability, but compress even recent interactions (Heess et al., 2015; Ni et al., 2022; Morad et al., 2023). Summary memory offers a way to sep-arate immediate access from longer-term retention: recent records provide direct context, while a 1arXiv:2610.25758v1 [cs.LG] 19 Oct 2026 Preprint. Under review. compact representation carries earlier experience (Rae et al., 2020; Ge et al., 2024). Segment-level Transformers implement this separation with local attention and representations carried between segments (Dai et al., 2019; Hutchins et al., 2022): Recurrent Memory Transformers (RMTs) carry a fixed bank of memory tokens (Bulatov et al., 2022), whereas Memo appends one summary block per segment, so its state still grows (Gupta et al., 2025). A fixed-capacity summary eliminates storage growth, but bounded storage alone does not guarantee sustained adaptation. Once a segment is discarded, its useful evidence must survive not only the next summary, but every subsequent rewrite until it is needed. Re-exploring the room may recover a forgotten key location, but not a cue presented only once (Ni et al., 2023). Conversely, when the hid-den task changes, retaining obsolete evidence can obstruct adaptation. The summary must therefore learn both to preserve relevant information and to revise it. These demands grow beyond the training horizon, where memory undergoes more rewrites than in training. Success on finite training tasks need not imply reliable memory during continued operation; indeed, finite and continuing memory tasks can reverse the relative performance of recurrent and attention-based agents (Pleines et al., 2025). We therefore ask: Can a Transformer policy sustain useful in-context adaptation beyond its local context and training horizon under a fixed deployment memory budget? 

We address this question with Recurrent Summary Memory (RSM) , a Transformer policy with a recurrent-memory architecture. RSM combines exact attention over a short-term working buffer of the current segment’s interactions with a fixed-capacity long-term summary. Here, exact means that each decision attends directly to the cached representation of every record in the buffer, not that the whole history is kept. At each segment boundary, it writes a replacement summary from the previous summary and buffered steps, then clears the buffer, so only the new summary carries earlier experience into the next segment. What the summary retains is learned only through the actor–critic losses, not set by token-retention rules (StreamingLLM; Xiao et al., 2024) or auxiliary reconstruction losses (Compressive Transformers; Rae et al., 2020). With fixed capacities, RSM’s deployment state and per-decision computation are O(1) in task length: in our experiments it holds 249.9 KB per agent, whereas a full-history cache of the same backbone grows by 6 KB per step, to about 5 GB after a day of 10 Hz control. Such bounds matter for robots with fixed onboard memory, game agents acting over thousands of steps, and services running many agents in parallel. To test whether this bounded memory sustains adaptation, we evaluate RSM on four partially observ-able benchmarks: Dark Key-to-Door (location reuse; Laskin et al., 2023), CountRecall (evidence ac-cumulation; Morad et al., 2023), MazeRunner (navigation with unknown action mappings; Grigsby et al., 2024), and the passive T-Maze (one-time-cue recall; Ni et al., 2023). We compare against a full-history Transformer, a GRU, accumulating-summary Memo, and a state-matched sliding win-dow, trained with the same learner, inputs, losses, and interaction budget. On these benchmarks, a fixed-capacity summary sustains in-context adaptation far beyond the training horizon, answering our question affirmatively. Specifically, our contributions are: • Bounded summary memory for online in-context RL. We specify and evaluate RSM, combining an exact segment-local working buffer with a fixed-capacity summary pro-duced by learned write queries and a projected overwrite. RSM is trained with AMAGO’s (Grigsby et al., 2024) off-policy actor–critic losses through every carried write. Its deploy-ment state and per-decision cost remain O(1) in task length (Section 3). • Sustained adaptation beyond the training horizon. RSM remains competitive at the training horizon; frozen RSM policies maintain adapted performance over eight times that horizon and re-adapt better than growing and recurrent memories when the hidden task changes, whereas growing memories deteriorate (Section 4.2). • Evidence that decisions depend on summary content. Clearing the summary, trans-planting another task’s summary into it, and blocking the policy’s access to it show that the policy uses its content; linear probes recover hidden task variables; and write-rule ablations show greater stability with overwriting than with additive updates (Sections 4.3–4.4).

The WRITE outputs depend on Zb through the READ rows, which makes the overwrite recurrent without an additive identity path between summaries: the writer must express both the retained information and the new evidence in M output tokens. Only Zb+1 crosses a boundary: the working buffer is cleared and the slot counter restarts. During cached interaction, a write is executed only when a full segment is followed by another record. Dense training also computes a final WRITE output, but it has no downstream decision loss. RSM is trained with AMAGO’s off-policy actor–critic learner (Grigsby et al., 2024), shared by all compared policies: each update replays complete outer tasks, recomputes their summaries from 

Zinit under the current weights and backpropagates through every carried write (Werbos, 1990), with no summary target or auxiliary memory loss. Every later loss therefore reaches every earlier write through Zb+1 = Pθ (Wb); under the overwrite, the Jacobian ∂Z b+2 /∂Z b+1 passes only through attention to the READ rows, whereas RSM-R adds an identity term. Before each collection block, the parallel agents’ states are rebuilt from their consumed public prefixes under the current weights. ## 4 EXPERIMENTS 

We ask (1) whether RSM adapts as well as growing memories, at and far beyond the training horizon (Section 4.2); (2) whether its decisions use the summary (Section 4.3); and (3) which design choices matter (Section 4.4). 4.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Environments. We use four partially observable benchmarks. In Dark Key-to-Door (Key-to-Door below; Laskin et al., 2023; Grigsby et al., 2024), the agent searches an 8 × 8 room for a hidden key and door over repeated attempts in one 500-step task; CountRecall (POPGym; Morad et al., 2023) deals 104 cards from four categories and asks at every step how many of a queried category have been dealt; in MazeRunner (Grigsby et al., 2024), the agent reaches three ordered goals in a hidden 

15 × 15 maze with permuted actions in one 500-step attempt; and the passive T-Maze (Ni et al., 2023) requires recalling a cue shown only at the first step of a 128-cell corridor. 

Baselines and Ablations. All policies share the stateless timestep encoder, the public inputs and the AMAGO off-policy learner; the Transformers share width 256, three layers and eight heads. We compare RSM (Section 3) with the full-history Transformer , whose key–value cache grows with every record; AMAGO’s recurrent GRU , whose state is fixed; and Memo (Gupta et al., 2025), which appends four summary tokens per 32-record segment, both as published (jittered segment lengths) and with fixed segments . We call the full-history Transformer and Memo the growing memories .The sliding window is a summary-free Transformer attending only to the last W = 40 records at every layer, the largest window within RSM’s per-agent state. The ablations train RSM w/o memory , which resets the summary at every segment boundary; RSM-R , which adds the write to the carried summary instead of overwriting it (Equation 6); RSM with M ∈ { 1, 4, 8, 16 } summary tokens on Key-to-Door; and, on CountRecall, RSM detach , whose summary is detached at every boundary so that no later loss reaches the write. 

Evaluation Protocol. Every policy is trained three times, with seeds 42, 100 and 2026, for 8M steps each, and evaluated at its final checkpoint by greedy rollouts on 256 held-out confirmation 

tasks per benchmark, whose layouts, card streams and mazes never occur in training. The metrics are doors per 500 steps on Key-to-Door, exact accuracy on CountRecall ( CR evicted : queries whose evidence has left the buffer), the fraction of the three goals reached on MazeRunner, and success on the T-Maze, each first at the training horizon , the length of a training task. CountRecall and the T-Maze have fixed-length tasks; Key-to-Door and MazeRunner also run for 4,000 steps, eight times that horizon, counting successes per 500 steps: in the same task the layout or map stays fixed while attempts continue, and in a new task a new layout arrives every 500 steps, or a new maze with the same action mapping every 1,000, with memory carried. At the training horizon. RSM is competitive at the training horizon and pulls ahead over long tasks (below). Some baselines score higher in mean, but none is consistently better: the full-history Transformer reaches 0.998 against 0.989 accuracy on CountRecall, within the equivalence margin, and Memo reaches a goal fraction of 0.863 against 0.804 on MazeRunner, ahead on every seed but by a mean gap below δ. RSM is equivalent to the full-history Transformer on CountRecall, MazeRunner and the T-Maze and consistently better than the GRU on CountRecall and MazeRunner; on Key-to-Door the intervals are too wide to separate it from the growing memories and the GRU (Tables 1 and 7). Clearing, transplanting and blocking. We test causally whether the decisions depend on the car-ried summary (Figure 4). Resetting RSM’s summary to Zinit at every boundary, with the working buffer intact, removes the benefit of the summary on every benchmark and every seed, and leaves the policy well below RSM w/o memory on CountRecall and the long MazeRunner task and at its fixed-turn level on the T-Maze: the trained policy does not fall back on the buffer-only strategy that RSM w/o memory learns (Figure 4a–e). To transplant, we replace each task’s summary at one boundary with the summary that a different task, the donor , carried there; behaviour then follows the donor task: on Key-to-Door the next attempt visits the donor’s key cell first in most tasks (Fig-ure 4f), and on CountRecall the policy answers with the donor’s counts until the next rewrite. As attention weights alone do not explain a decision (Jain & Wallace, 2019), we also intervene on them: adding −β to every decision’s attention scores on the four summary keys leaves the summary carried but unreadable at β = ∞, which reduces RSM to the level of RSM w/o memory on Key-to-Door and below it on CountRecall (Figure 4a,b), with a loss growing with β. Carrying and training the summary. Removing the carried summary, keeping only the 32-record buffer, loses most of the adaptation (Table 1, RSM w/o memory), consistently on every seed on Key-to-Door, CountRecall and the T-Maze, and the loss widens over the 4,000-step Key-to-Door task. On MazeRunner one 500-step attempt is largely solvable from the buffer, so the summary adds less than δ at the training horizon, but several goals per window over longer tasks. How the write is trained also matters: detaching the summary at every boundary lowers RSM’s CountRecall accuracy on every seed (0.914 against 0.989), yet keeps most of the gain over RSM w/o memory (0.680). Training each write through the decisions that later read it thus improves what is kept. 

Overwrite versus additive write. RSM-R stays within δ of RSM in mean at the training horizon and over the 4,000-step Key-to-Door task, but over many MazeRunner attempts on one map it declines below RSM w/o memory and on the map-change task it collapses by the fourth map, on every seed, while after a Key-to-Door layout change it adapts again no better than RSM w/o memory (Table 1). Wiping its memory at every attempt boundary preserves its rate, so the decline comes from what it carries. Its summaries drift in one direction until the first and the last are anti-correlated, at a norm tens of times larger than RSM’s: a drift that Proposition 2 bounds only for the overwrite. 

Summary length. Varying the summary from one to sixteen tokens on Key-to-Door, with buffer, backbone and budget fixed, changes adaptation by no consistent margin (Table 3): the paired differ-ences from four tokens change sign across seeds at the training horizon, and in the last window every length completes 45 to 46 doors. Even one token shows no consistent difference from four, and a larger summary buys no further adaptation on this task. More details in Appendices F.4 and F.6.

## 5 RELATED WORK 

In-context reinforcement learning. Recurrent meta-RL first achieved in-context adaptation through hidden-state updates (Duan et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2016), and PEARL and VariBAD infer a belief over the task from context (Rakelly et al., 2019; Zintgraf et al., 2020). Sequence models extend the idea to longer histories: Algorithm Distillation imitates the learning histories of RL algorithms (Laskin et al., 2023), Decision-Pretrained Transformers predict optimal actions from interaction data (Lee et al., 2023), and AMAGO and ReLIC learn in context through off-policy and on-policy updates over long sequences (Grigsby et al., 2024; Elawady et al., 2024). How well such agents remember is harder to measure: memory is easily confounded with exploration and credit assignment (Paˇ sukonis et al., 2023; Ni et al., 2023; Cherepanov et al., 2026a), finite and end-less versions of a task can reverse the ranking of recurrent and Transformer memories (Pleines et al., 2025), and retaining information differs from rewriting it when the task changes (Shchendrigin et al., 2026). Our evaluation follows these findings: we ran frozen policies far beyond the training horizon, changed the hidden task mid-run, and intervened on the summary directly. 

Memory for sequence-based control. Like recurrent networks, state-space models and gated lin-ear attention keep a fixed-size state (Lu et al., 2023; Pramanik et al., 2024), and Stable Hadamard Memory calibrates each update to erase what is no longer needed (Le et al., 2025). Learned sum-maries compress long contexts in language models (Chevalier et al., 2023; Ge et al., 2024), and language-model and RL memories have also been trained with reconstruction or predictive losses (Rae et al., 2020; Wayne et al., 2018). In RL, RATE and ELMUR carry segment memory offline, ELMUR detaching it between segments (Cherepanov et al., 2026c;b), and Memo, the closest on-line method, compresses each segment into summary tokens and backpropagates through them, but keeps every summary block, so its state grows with the task (Gupta et al., 2025). Memo also prompts each write with learned summary embeddings and reports a fixed-size, RMT-style variant, finding that accumulating summaries better retains relevant past experience; we instead test whether a fixed-capacity summary, trained with an overwrite, sustains adaptation far beyond the training horizon. Like Recurrent Memory Transformers (Bulatov et al., 2022), RSM carries a fixed bank of memory tokens between segments. RMTs are trained with token-prediction losses on language and algorithmic tasks and place the carried memory at both their read and write positions; RSM instead writes with learned, history-independent queries, as Memo does, overwrites its summary with their projected, normalised outputs, and is trained online through actor–critic losses. 

## 6 DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION 

We introduced Recurrent Summary Memory, a Transformer policy that attends exactly to a short working buffer and carries a fixed-capacity summary, overwritten at every segment boundary and trained only through actor–critic losses. With 249.9 KB of deployment state per agent, about 1% of a full-history cache after 4,000 steps, RSM remains competitive with growing memories at the training horizon. Over tasks eight times longer, it sustains its adapted performance and re-adapts better than the growing and recurrent memories when the hidden task changes, while the growing memories deteriorate. Memories that perform alike at the training horizon can thus diverge in con-tinued operation, so finite-horizon scores alone are an incomplete test of memory for long-running agents. Clearing, transplanting and blocking the summary show that the decisions depend on what it carries, and the overwrite provably bounds the summary at any horizon, whereas an additive write drifts. These results suggest that a fixed-capacity memory can keep an agent adapting far beyond its training horizon, provided it is trained to decide what to keep at every rewrite. Three limits remain. We trained only on tasks of at most 500 steps; only the deployment memory is bounded, since each training update replays whole tasks and backpropagates through every summary write; on Key-to-Door, RSM learns more slowly than every other memory that learns the task and, after a layout change, recovers in only about a third of layouts. Future work can also bound training memory, for example by backpropagating through only the most recent summaries: on CountRecall, cutting the gradient at every boundary still kept most of the summary’s benefit (Section 4.4). Attention masks. All layers use ordinary causal content attention. READ rows attend to preceding and self READ rows; RECORD rows attend to the READ rows and to preceding and self RECORD rows; WRITE rows attend to the READ rows, all valid RECORD rows, and preceding and self WRITE rows. C RECORD slots are reserved even for a partial segment, and padded records are masked as attention keys. 

Boundaries and deferred writes. A boundary occurs after C admitted records, counting the ini-tial observation and valid reset observations. Cached execution defers the write until the next record arrives: the full segment remains in state, then the model writes, clears the cache, and loads the new READ tokens before admitting that record. Ending an outer task alone triggers no write. Attempt re-16 Preprint. Under review. sets preserve memory; an outer-task reset clears the cache and restores Zinit independently for each agent. Reset-only steps keep the interface’s reward and transition semantics and are not physical actions. 

Training and rebuild. Dense training reconstructs complete replayed tasks under current weights and propagates the actor–critic loss (Appendix C) through every carried write. Only valid recorded transitions contribute to the loss; a final write with no following segment has no downstream decision loss. Padding preserves WRITE positions and is masked as attention keys. Before collection resumes, R EBUILD runs without gradients in evaluation mode on U proc , the records consumed since the agent’s last outer-task reset. This prefix excludes the pending obser-vation. Using q = ⌊(n − 1) /C ⌋ preserves the deferred write when the last processed segment is full. The resulting state matches cached execution under current weights. Rebuilding avoids replay-ing stored recurrent states, which become stale as the weights change (Kapturowski et al., 2019). Frozen-policy evaluation uses only Algorithm 1, with no replay or reconstruction.

## E EVALUATION PROTOCOL 

Every policy observes the fields listed below together with its previous action and reward; task identities, hidden layouts and evaluator labels never reach it. Training tasks are drawn from identi-ties 0–999 ,999 , development tasks from 1,000 ,000 –1,000 ,063 and the 256 confirmation tasks from 

5,000 ,000 –5,000 ,255 , disjoint from each other. Every reported result is one greedy rollout per confirmation task at the final checkpoint of each run, and nothing is retrained or tuned for the long-running evaluations. E.1 BENCHMARKS 

Dark Key-to-Door. The start, key and door cells of an 8 × 8 room are hidden and fixed for the outer task; the agent observes its position, whether it holds the key, and the attempt clock. A task lasts 500 environment steps and holds attempts of at most 50 actions separated by reset-only steps, with memory kept across attempts. The metric is the number of doors completed in 500 steps ( δ = 1 

door). 

CountRecall. A stream of 104 cards from four categories is dealt in a hidden order; the agent observes the dealt and the queried category (one-hot) and a timer, and answers 103 scored queries for the running count of the queried category. The metric is exact accuracy ( δ = 0 .05 ). Retention is read by the number of summary rewrites since the queried category was first dealt. 

MazeRunner. The walls of a 15 × 15 maze and the permutation of its five actions are hidden; the agent observes its position, the distances to the four surrounding walls, a timer and the coordinates of three ordered goals, all scaled by the maze size. A task is one episode of at most 500 steps that ends at the third goal or at the timer. The metric is the fraction of goals reached ( δ = 0 .1; one goal per 500 steps on the long-running tasks). 

Passive T-Maze. A cue at the first step fixes the rewarded turn at the junction of a corridor of length L; the agent observes the cue only then; afterwards it observes a flag that marks the junction. Training draws L from the multiples of 32 between 32 and 256 and allows L + 1 decisions, with a −1/128 penalty for a non-forward action before the junction. The metric is success at L = 128 

(δ = 0 .25 ). E.2 LONG -RUNNING EVALUATIONS

F.3 SUMMARY TRANSPLANTS 

At one boundary, we replace every task’s written summary with the summary that another task of the same seed read at that boundary, and leave the buffer, the weights and everything else unchanged; the control writes the initial summary at the same boundary ( cleared once ). On Key-to-Door, the first attempt after the boundary visits the donor’s key cell far more often than with the task’s own summary or with the summary cleared once (Figure 9b), and the misdirected policy completes fewer doors than the cleared one for the rest of the task (Figure 9a): the transplanted summary redirects the policy towards donor-associated cells, and the effect persists across later rewrites. On CountRecall the transplanted policy answers with the counts that the donor’s summary implies rather than the true counts until the next rewrite, which moves its answers away from the donor’s counts and partly towards the task’s own (Figure 9c). F.5 SUMMARY ATTENTION 

The READ-slot attention share is the attention mass that a decision’s heads place on the READ slots, averaged over heads. We compare it with the share M/ (M + i) under uniform attention at segment position i and with RSM w/o memory, whose READ slots always hold Zinit . Because RSM w/o memory is separately trained, it controls for fixed READ-slot content and position, not for position alone. Figure 11 shows the READ-slot attention share by position in the segment and the effect of biasing decisions’ attention away from the summary or the buffer. The decisions assign more attention mass to these slots than RSM w/o memory does, in every layer on Key-to-Door and in the second and third layers on CountRecall, on every seed. Against uniform attention the excess is clear only in the first layer on Key-to-Door and the second on CountRecall; the other layers stay near or below M/ (M + i) early in the segment and only slightly above it later. The share hardly depends on where the needed evidence lives: queries whose evidence survives only in the summary, and the first steps of later Key-to-Door attempts, draw only marginally more summary attention than their position predicts.
