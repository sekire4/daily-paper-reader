ABSTRACT 

Large-scale search and recommendation systems derive much of their retrieval quality from rich, heterogeneous features, yet existing generative retrieval (GR) methods struggle to exploit such feature ecosystems efficiently. Static feature injection either encodes all available signals or places them according to prede-termined schemes, increasing serving cost and introducing irrelevant or conflict-ing evidence. More fundamentally, existing methods cannot adaptively determine when a feature is needed, which feature to select, or where it should influence hierarchical Semantic ID (SID) generation. We propose Agentic Generative Re-trieval (AGR) , a tool-augmented paradigm that interleaves SID generation with on-demand feature acquisition. Conditioned on the request and decoded SID pre-fix, AGR decides whether to invoke a tool, which feature to acquire, and where its observation should influence subsequent decoding, thereby steering generation toward feature-consistent SID branches. To supervise this behavior, we construct tool-augmented trajectories based on feature applicability and information gain along the SID hierarchy, and train the model through SID pretraining followed by tool-augmented fine-tuning. Experiments on an industrial dataset and three public benchmarks, together with an online A/B test, consistently demonstrate the effectiveness of AGR .

1 INTRODUCTION 

Generative Retrieval (GR) casts retrieval as autoregressive identifier generation (Tay et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2022; Bevilacqua et al., 2022). Following TIGER (Rajput et al., 2023), items are commonly represented by hierarchical Semantic IDs (SIDs) learned through residual quantization. This paradigm has also been successfully adopted in large-scale industrial scenarios (Deng et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025a;c; Team et al., 2026). Deploying GR in industrial systems, however, exposes a fundamental mismatch between request-conditioned SID generation and the feature-intensive nature of such systems (Zhai et al., 2024). Large-scale retrieval depends heavily on heterogeneous signals, such as user profiles, behavioral histories, and freshness cues (Hou et al., 2026). Yet existing GR methods scale poorly with the num-ber of features (Figure 1), typically appending all signals to the input or injecting them according to a predetermined scheme, making it difficult to jointly maintain retrieval quality and serving effi-ciency (Zhou et al., 2026). Specifically, encoding all features indiscriminately inflates serving cost and introduces irrelevant or conflicting evidence, as feature utility is inherently request-dependent: a feature indispensable for one request may be pure noise for another (Zhang et al., 2026a). This raises the question of when a feature should be incorporated . Meanwhile, injecting features at a fixed scheme cannot adapt to the decoding state, but residual quantization induces a hierarchical SID space where different depths resolve distinctions at different granularities. Therefore, a feature highly informative at one level may be irrelevant at others. This raises the question of where a fea-ture should influence generation . Addressing both questions is crucial for efficient and scalable GR deployment in industrial search, yet effective feature injection remains largely overlooked.

AGR encapsulates het-erogeneous features as tools, each uniquely identified by a tool token. Conditioned on the current decoding state, the model decides whether to emit a tool token and, if so, which tool to invoke. The acquired feature values are then represented as observation tokens that reshape the probability distri-bution over subsequent SID tokens, steering generation toward feature-consistent paths. As features are introduced only when the decoding state warrants them, AGR reduces feature noise and naturally aligns feature granularity with the coarse-to-fine progression of SID generation. Through this adap-tive tool use, AGR shifts generative retrieval from passive SID generation to agentic decision-making over both items and features. 

Contributions. Our contributions are shown as follows: • Driven by the industrial need to exploit rich, heterogeneous feature ecosystems in GR, we sys-tematically reveal and quantify two fundamental limitations of static feature injection in GR: it is neither request-adaptive ( when to inject) nor level-adaptive ( where to inject). We further argue that features should be acquired on demand and injected at the right SID depth. • We propose Agentic Generative Retrieval (AGR) , which encapsulates feature injection as tool invocation and thereby unifies adaptive feature selection with dynamic placement into a single decoding process, paving the way for exploiting rich features in real-world retrieval systems. • We derive principled criteria for choosing which tool to invoke and where it is most informative along the SID hierarchy, so that each tool call steers decoding toward feature-consistent branches, and instantiate them in a two-stage pipeline of SID pretraining and tool-augmented fine-tuning. • We evaluate AGR on Kuaishou Search system and three widely used public benchmarks. Generative Retrieval. Given a request containing query q and model-visible user context uvis ,generative retrieval aims to retrieve K items from a corpus X by directly generating item identifiers. Each item x ∈ X is represented by a hierarchical SID sequence s = [ s1, . . . , s L], where si ∈ S i

denotes the token at level i and s⩽i = [ s1, . . . , s i] denotes its prefix. The retriever generates the sequence autoregressively by modeling Pθ (si+1 | q, u vis , s⩽i), and scores each prefix by accumu-lating token-level log-probabilities πθ (s⩽i) = Pij=1 log Pθ (sj | q, u vis , s⩽j−1). During inference, a prefix trie Tr over valid SIDs constrains beam search to valid paths only. After L decoding steps, the top-K completed sequences are mapped to the corresponding items as results (Cao et al., 2021).

AGR Framework. To provide a flexible and extensible interface for feature interaction, we model tool calls and their results as tokens. Specifically, each tool token t ∈ T triggers a function call 

ft(q, u, s⩽i), whose result is represented as an observation token ot ∈ O . By extending the vo-cabulary to V = S ∪ T ∪ O , the model naturally unifies SID generation and tool invocation into a single autoregressive process. As shown in Figure 3, given the request q, user uvis , decoded prefix 

s⩽i, and prior observations, the model predicts either the next SID token si+1 ∈ S or a tool token 

t ∈ T (e.g. tI or tG). Specifically, tool tokens serve solely as decision triggers and are not fed back into the autoregressive context; only the returned observation token (e.g., oI or oG) is injected, with distinct positional encodings to differentiate it from S. As a result, the decoder consumes a single token per step, yet still allowing observations to prune irrelevant branches during generation (e.g., the “Movie” branch in Figure 3). For more details, refer to Algorithm 2 and Example 1. 

Tool Implementations. Based on the type of observation returned, tools fall into two categories: 

structured feature tools , and sequential feature tools :(a) Structured feature tools return categorical values and can be directly encoded into a single learnable observation token — e.g., a game intent maps to oI : Game , a Beijing location to 

oL : Beijing , and a high freshness requirement to oF : High . Due to the hierarchical structure of RQ, items with similar features naturally share SID prefixes; for example, videos about games tend to cluster under the same prefixes (Rajput et al., 2023). Thus, these learnable observation tokens could serve as informative signals during decoding. (b) Sequential feature tools return user behavioral sequences (e.g., General Search Unit, GSU (Zhou et al., 2026)), which need to be encoded into a single observation token oG. Various methods could accomplish this (Cao et al., 2026); we adopt two complementary approaches: 

Vote , a parameter-free approach that is efficient for training and inference, and Attn , which of-fers stronger user preference modeling capacity. As shown in Figure 4, Vote performs prefix matching: it selects items whose SID prefix matches the decoded one, then picks the most frequent next SID token (i.e., s3 7 ) via majority voting. Attn instead operates in the embed-ding space: it computes the accumulated residual representation of the decoded prefix (e.g., the sum of the embeddings of s1 1 and s2 3 ), retrieves the Top-E (E = 5 for evaluation) most similar items from the sequence, and encodes their next SID token representations into a single embedding via multi-head attention. Two-Stage Training. To decouple semantic learning from tool integration for better extensibility, 

AGR training consists of two stages. The first stage leverages GR (SID-only) data to learn SID generation following traditional GR methods (Chen et al., 2025c). The second stage then introduces 

AGR data, where the model learns to integrate tool calls into generation. The loss now covers both SID tokens and tool tokens. In practice, since tool tokens are unseen during the first stage, their token-level losses are initially much higher than those of SID tokens, risking destabilization of already-learned representations. 5.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Datasets. We evaluate AGR on four datasets: Industry, Qilin, JDSearch, and KuaiSAR (large). Industry is derived from real-world user behavior logs collected from Kuaishou Search, with ap-proximately 300 million records per day; we use June 14–20 for the first training stage, June 21–22 for the second training stage, and June 23 for testing. Qilin is a multimodal retrieval benchmark with app-level user sessions (Chen et al., 2025b); JDSearch focuses on personalized product-search benchmark with real queries and user–item interactions (Liu et al., 2023); and KuaiSAR provides a unified search-and-recommendation benchmark (Sun et al., 2023b). Further details are provided in Table 8. 

AGR Implementations. We adopt a 12-layer encoder-decoder BART (Lewis et al., 2020) as the backbone ( ≈ 574M parameters, including embeddings for approximately 19,000 predefined SID codewords) and construct hierarchical SIDs via RQ-KMeans (Rajput et al., 2023). Dataset-specific SID configurations and tool sets are listed in Table 9; inference follows Algorithm 2. On the large-scale Industry dataset, we train the model sequentially in two stages, with each stage running for one epoch over its corresponding aggregated training data. For the smaller public datasets, we fine-tune with each method for 10 epochs until convergence. For fair comparison, within each dataset, all methods share the same text-pretrained backbone, SID codebooks, training data, learning rate, and hardware configuration. Additional details are provided in Appendix A.

We compare AGR with five generative retrieval baselines: TIGER (Rajput et al., 2023) for standard GR baseline; TIGER Distill Teacher, which first trains a teacher model with up to 400 GSU tokens (Table 4) and then distills its predictions into TIGER using an auxiliary distilla-tion loss with a weight of 0.1; UniSearch (Chen et al., 2025c) for unified short-video retrieval; and GLASS (Cao et al., 2026) and GEMs (Zhou et al., 2026) for long-history modeling. 6 LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK 

AGR interleaves tool invocation with SID generation, but has three limitations. First, its training data are constructed using predefined rules rather than learned policies; future work could apply agentic reinforcement learning to optimize tool use under the sparse rewards common in search and recommendation. Second, the current tool ecosystem is limited and should be extended to more tools and retrieval domains. Third, trajectories are constructed along golden SID prefixes using target-side agreement, creating a gap from free-running inference. Optimizing and evaluating tool decisions under model-generated prefixes is therefore an important future direction. 

7 CONCLUSION 

We presented Agentic Generative Retrieval (AGR) , a tool-augmented paradigm that selectively ac-quires and incorporates heterogeneous features during hierarchical SID generation. AGR determines when, and where features should be introduced based on the request and decoded SID prefix. We train this capability using tool-augmented data constructed from feature applicability and discrim-inability. Evaluations on an industrial dataset and public benchmarks, together with an online A/B test, demonstrate the effectiveness and efficiency of AGR , highlighting tool-augmented generation as a promising direction for large-scale generative retrieval. 9REFERENCES

A AGR IMPLEMENTATIONS 

This section provides further implementation details of AGR . For datasets with textual features (In-dustry and Qilin (Chen et al., 2025b)), we adopt Qwen2.5 (Yang et al., 2024) as a parameter-shared dual-encoder model and train it with Query-to-Item (Q2I) supervision. For anonymous datasets (JD-Search (Liu et al., 2023) and KuaiSAR (Sun et al., 2023b)), we instead use TwinBERT (Lu et al., 2020) to obtain item embeddings. We then apply hierarchical RQ-KMeans to construct their Se-mantic ID (SID) codebooks. Specifically, for the Industry dataset, which contains billions of recall samples, we use codebook sizes of (8192 , 4096 , 2048 , 2048 , 2048) , whereas for the comparatively smaller public datasets, we use (512 , 256 , 128) . We next describe the implementation of AGR in terms of tools, model architecture, inference, training-data construction, and training procedure. A.1 DATASETS AND SETTINGS

As Figure 3 illustrates, its input and output spaces are asymmetric: the output contains generated SIDs and tool-call decisions, whereas the decoder input contains SIDs and externally re-turned observations. A generated SID is fed back autoregressively; a generated tool token instead triggers external execution, and the returned observation is fed into the next decoding step. This interface represents every tool call and observation with one decoding position and uses distinct positional encodings to separate observations from SID decisions. The quota M is maintained sep-arately for every beam-decoding path. A quota of M calls therefore adds at most M decoding steps 15 to each path, so an L-token SID requires at most L + M + 1 steps including eos . Tool calls are neither fixed to predetermined positions nor required to alternate with SIDs. At each incomplete depth, the model may select either a valid SID successor or an available tool. The method described in Section 4.2 determines an admissible position at which to invoke each tool so as to maximize the discriminability of the feature. Theorem 3 further characterizes the position at which the feature is most informative w.r.t. sequential features. 

Example 1 (AGR Workflow) . Figure 3 illustrates how AGR interleaves SID generation with feature acquisition for the query “Fighting Zombies”. After the first SID token is generated, the prefix may lead to either the movie or game branch, making the next prediction uncertain. The model there-fore emits the intent-tool token tI and receives the game-intent observation oI : Game , which steers decoding toward games while suppressing irrelevant movie candidates. Further along the game branch, query relevance alone cannot distinguish among candidates such as Minecraft and Plants vs. Zombies . The model thus emits the GSU-tool token tG to acquire user preferences from histor-ical behaviors. As shown in Figure 4, Vote retains items whose SIDs exactly match the decoded prefix (A, B, and D) and returns the most frequent successor SID, s3 7 , indicating a preference for Minecraft . In contrast, Attn exploits the residual clustering structure of SIDs: summing the embeddings of the decoded SID tokens reconstructs the prefix representation at the current decod-ing depth. It then retrieves the Top-E historical behaviors whose item representations are closest to this reconstructed prefix representation, thereby including approximate matches such as C, and aggregates their successor SIDs via lightweight multi-head attention. Both implementations provide fine-grained preference signals that steer decoding toward the user’s preferred Minecraft branch while suppressing irrelevant candidates.

B INTERPRETIVE ANALYSIS 

B.1 AN INTERPRETIVE VIEW OF DYNAMIC FEATURE INTERACTION 

This section provides an interpretive account of dynamic feature interaction. It is not intended as a general performance guarantee for AGR . Instead, it explains a simple design intuition: as the decoded SID prefix becomes more fine-grained, the information that is useful for predicting the next SID token may also change. Theorem 1 (Contextual Incremental Information) . A feature observation ot is useful w.r.t. model 

Pθ only if it adds information beyond the current context (i.e., q, u vis and s⩽i). 17 Proof. Consider the conditional mutual information at the current context by definition: 

Iθ (si+1 ; ot | q, u vis , s⩽i)= E [DKL (Pθ (si+1 | q, u vis , s⩽i, o t) ∥ Pθ (si+1 | q, u vis , s⩽i))] . (12) If this quantity is zero, the nonnegativity of the KL divergence implies that 

Pθ (si+1 | q, u vis , s⩽i, o t) = Pθ (si+1 | q, u vis , s⩽i) almost surely . (13) Hence, ot provides no additional predictive information for the next SID token at the current context (i.e., q, u vis , s⩽i). The contrapositive proves the theorem. Although conditional mutual information is nonnegative in expectation, the predictive gain of a real-ized observation can vary across contexts. This consideration is particularly important in industrial search and recommendation systems, where numerous features are available to characterize com-plex and evolving user preferences, yet generative retrieval models can incorporate only limited additional information during decoding because of efficiency and scalability constraints. Feature injection must therefore be selective, retaining observations that improve prediction at the current prefix while avoiding those that provide no gain or reduce the probability of the correct next SID token. Our method implements this context-dependent selection through tool invocation and obser-vation injection (Section 4); next, we analyze this mechanism through experiments on tool selection and invocation.

D RELATED WORKS 

Generative retrieval and semantic identifiers. Distinct from traditional discriminative re-trieval (Xie et al., 2026; Zheng et al., 2024), generative retrieval reformulates retrieval as condi-tional sequence generation over discrete identifiers (Chen et al., 2025c; Tay et al., 2022). Early work focused on document retrieval, where DSI stores query–document associations in model pa-rameters (Tay et al., 2022), NCI introduces structured identifiers and prefix-aware decoding (Wang et al., 2022), and SEAL uses document-derived textual access paths (Bevilacqua et al., 2022). These studies established that the identifier space is learnable and that errors at different decoding posi-tions have different retrieval consequences (Sun et al., 2023a; Zeng et al., 2024). Generative rec-ommendation extends this idea to multi-token item semantic identifiers (SIDs). TIGER quantizes item representations with RQ-VAE and generates hierarchical SIDs (Rajput et al., 2023), making coarse-to-fine constrained decoding a standard formulation. Subsequent work shows that SID qual-ity depends on semantic consistency, discrimination, codebook utilization, and tree balance (Ju et al., 2026), while autoregressive SID trees may also restrict ranking expressiveness (Hou et al., 2026). 

AGR builds on this SID-based generative retrieval paradigm, but shifts the focus from how identifiers are constructed to how external evidence should interact with SID generation. 

Feature conditioning in generative retrieval. Real-world GR systems rely on heterogeneous auxiliary information, including user profiles, behavioral histories, scenario context, freshness sig-nals, and intent features. Existing methods usually incorporate such information before decoding or through a fixed injection pattern (Wang et al., 2025). DiffusionGS performs query-conditioned denoising over candidate items (Li et al., 2026b); DualGR separates long- and short-term user inter-ests (Yi et al., 2026); GLASS retrieves user histories conditioned on coarse SIDs (Cao et al., 2026); and OneReason partitions behavioral histories into temporal streams (Team et al., 2026). These methods show that request-aware evidence selection is important, and they move beyond globally encoding all context indiscriminately. However, their injection mechanisms are still largely pre-determined: evidence is selected before generation, kept fixed after a coarse decision, or injected at predefined network locations. They do not formulate feature acquisition itself as an explicit decoding-time action. AGR differs by expanding the generative action space with tool tokens, al-lowing the model to decide whether a feature is needed, which feature to acquire, and where in the SID hierarchy the acquired evidence should affect subsequent generation. 

Industrial GR systems and optimization. Industrial systems pursue GR for unification, cross-domain reuse, and serving efficiency. UniSearch jointly trains generators and encoders for video and live search (Chen et al., 2025c); OneSearch develops task-aware SID construction for e-commerce (Chen et al., 2025a); IntSR shares models across search and recommendation (Yan et al., 2025); OneRec unifies retrieval and ranking (Deng et al., 2025); and OneMall extends GR across multiple scenarios (Zhang et al., 2026b). Complementary work improves SID construction, colli-sion control, and long-term maintenance (Wang et al., 2026c; Fu et al., 2026b; Baikalov et al., 2026; Fang et al., 2026), and generative interfaces have also been extended to reranking and query recom-mendation (Yang et al., 2026; Shao et al., 2025). Beyond maximum-likelihood training, preference-oriented methods align GR with ranking quality or business value: UniSearch uses ranking sig-nals (Chen et al., 2025c), while related methods apply preference optimization to e-commerce (Li et al., 2024), adapt DPO to hierarchical SIDs (Chen et al., 2026b), use listwise optimization (Fu et al., 2026a), generative flow networks (Wang et al., 2026a), token-level signals (Lin et al., 2026), 21 iterative preference construction (Deng et al., 2025), page-wise supervision (Zou et al., 2026), and list-level value optimization (Xue et al., 2026). Efficiency methods accelerate decoding through draft verification (Wang et al., 2026b), process reward models (Guo et al., 2026), and load-aware computation (Xue et al., 2026). These efforts optimize different components of large-scale GR pipelines, whereas AGR targets a complementary bottleneck: avoiding noisy and expensive static feature injection by acquiring useful features on demand during decoding. 

Reasoning and agentic retrieval. Recent work introduces intermediate reasoning to improve in-tent understanding in search and recommendation. OneSearch-V2 constructs keyword-based chains of thought (Chen et al., 2026a); OneRec-Think interleaves textual reasoning with generation (Liu et al., 2025); SCoTER transfers structured reasoning from LLMs (Jiang et al., 2025); DiffuReason combines latent thinking with diffusion refinement (Jiang et al., 2026); and OneReason organizes recommendation around perception and cognition (Team et al., 2026). Agentic recommendation further allows models to actively acquire external evidence. KP-Agent combines LLM agents with domain tools (Long et al., 2025), and ChainRec learns to route and terminate tool chains (Li et al., 2026a). However, general-purpose LLM planning typically introduces sequential inference, external orchestration, and tool-chain latency, which are difficult to accommodate in latency-constrained re-trieval. Unlike these agentic recommenders, AGR treats feature access as a lightweight action inside native constrained SID decoding. Tool invocation is not a separate planning loop, but an interleaved part of beam search that directly reshapes the probability distribution over subsequent SID tokens.

Example 3 (Detailed inference for “World Cup Portugal”) . The user had recently consumed several football videos and then issued the request “World Cup Portugal”. The historical behaviors suggest an interest not only in the broad topic, but specifically in recent Portugal matches and Cristiano Ronaldo. The baseline captures the coarse topic, yet mixes a recent Ronaldo video with generic highlights and an older Portugal–DR Congo match (Figure 8). It therefore misses the request’s personalized, temporal, and 

22 intent-level requirements. AGR resolves these requirements through three tool-guided decoding paths. The showed calls below occur independently on three different beam paths: • Personalization through GSU. At the prefix [s1 6421 , s2 11037 ], SID-only decoding favors 

s3 13061 , leading to the Bruno Fernandes video (a). AGR instead invokes the GSU tool. Match-ing the prefix against the user’s history yields five votes for s3 13842 versus one for s3 13061 . The returned observation oG : s3 13842 redirects generation to the consumed Ronaldo video (d). • Temporal correction through Freshness. At [s1 6421 , s2 11480 , s3 13706 ], the original branch leads to an older July 3 match against Croatia (b). The Freshness tool classifies the query as high-freshness and returns oF : High , shifting generation toward s4 15244 . This retrieves the most recent report—Portugal’s July 7 elimination by Spain (e). • Semantic disambiguation through Intent. At [s1 6421 , s2 11826 , s3 14155 ], the competing branch leads to a Portugal away-jersey video (c). The Intent tool recognizes that the user seeks Sports information and returns oI : Sports . This suppresses merchandise-oriented content and redirects gener-ation toward s4 15376 , retrieving a video about Portugal’s 2026 World Cup squad (f). Together, these three independently tool-guided beam paths produce a top-3 ranked set that covers the user’s personal preference, need for up-to-date results, and sports-intent content; the first two returned videos were subsequently clicked.
