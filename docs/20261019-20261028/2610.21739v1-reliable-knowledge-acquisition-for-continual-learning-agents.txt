## Reliable Knowledge Acquisition for Continual Learning Agents 

Yixu Huang 

University of California, Davis 

Jiaqi W. Ma 

University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign 

Abstract 

As agents powered by foundation models move into open-ended environments, continual learn-ing from experience becomes essential. For agents built on models with fixed parameters, this learning requires reusing prior task experi-ence in context to improve performance and ef-ficiency on future tasks. Existing memory and experience-learning systems typically focus on either retained facts or procedures, and lack a mechanism for establishing the reliability of claims extracted from the ongoing task stream. Indeed, we find that approximately 12–33% of entries produced by representative systems harm future task performance when reused. To address these limitations, we propose RIKA 

(Reliable Knowledge Acquisition), which com-bines hierarchical extraction of factual, concep-tual, procedural, and metacognitive knowledge with Evidence-based Knowledge Admission, which uses source evidence and subsequent ex-perience to govern which claims enter the task agent’s context, without additional validation tasks. Across six task streams from Contin-ual Learning Bench and four model backbones, RIKA achieves the highest aggregate score on every backbone. 

1 Introduction 

Foundation-model agents increasingly operate in open-ended environments, where they encounter ongoing streams of tasks whose demands cannot be fully anticipated during training (Zhang et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2024; Shu et al., 2026; Zheng et al., 2025). Their experience provides a continu-ing source of learning (e.g., lessons from failures). For agents built on black-box language models with fixed parameters, this learning takes place through context: knowledge acquired from prior trajectories is supplied to guide subsequent decisions (Shinn et al., 2023). Continual learning therefore requires organizing past experience into reusable knowledge that can inform future tasks. A central challenge in reusing experience is 

whether extracted knowledge can be trusted to guide future decisions . Existing memory sys-tems (Chhikara et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026) often lack an explicit mechanism accounting for the reliability of extracted knowl-edge. Some experience-learning systems validate extracted knowledge by replaying tasks (Li et al., 2026) or evaluating on held-out tasks (Yang et al., 2026b), both of which require additional task ex-ecution that may be unavailable in continual set-tings, where environment states cannot be revisited or reset. Our quantitative evaluation of artifacts produced by representative systems shows that ap-proximately 12–33% of evaluated atomic claims reduce subsequent task performance when reused. A complementary qualitative case study further re-veals how such failures arise. Extracted knowledge may rely on information unavailable at reuse time, mismatch the requirements of subsequent tasks, or become outdated as the environment changes (Section 3.1). Beyond reliability, continual learning also re-quires deciding what knowledge to acquire . Mem-ory systems mostly retain facts and events, while experience-learning systems distill skills, work-flows, reflections, and strategic guidance (Wang et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2026b; Ni et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026b; Shinn et al., 2023). Beyond ob-servations and procedures, agents also need to learn underlying principles and when to apply or revise a method. This motivates a structured approach to learning complementary forms of knowledge and organizing the relationships among them. To address these two challenges, we propose 

RIKA (Rel iable Knowledge Acquisition), a con-tinual learning framework that transforms agents’ task experience into structured knowledge for fu-ture decisions. To ensure reliable reuse, RIKA introduces Evidence-based Knowledge Admis-sion (EKA) , which draws on sequential hypothesis 1arXiv:2610.21739v1 [cs.CL] 15 Oct 2026 testing and truth maintenance (Wald, 1992; Doyle, 1979) to admit and revise atomic claims as evi-dence accumulates from the ongoing task stream. Claims directly established by their source trajec-tories can be admitted immediately, while those requiring broader support remain pending until suf-ficient evidence accumulates; subsequent experi-ence can also challenge earlier admissions. This process requires no additional validation tasks. For knowledge extraction, RIKA draws on a knowledge taxonomy from educational psychology (Anderson and Krathwohl, 2001) to acquire four complemen-tary types of knowledge: factual, conceptual, proce-dural, and metacognitive. Its hierarchical extractor grounds conceptual abstractions in factual knowl-edge and metacognitive abstractions in procedural knowledge, organizing both concrete experience and higher-level understanding for reuse. Our contributions are threefold: • We identify and quantify a reliability problem in experience reuse, finding that approximately 12–33% of evaluated entries from representative systems harm subsequent task performance. • We propose RIKA, combining evidence-based knowledge admission with hierarchical extrac-tion of factual, conceptual, procedural, and metacognitive knowledge. • We demonstrate RIKA’s effectiveness across dif-ferent task streams and model backbones, achiev-ing the highest aggregate score on all backbones. 

2 Related Work 

2.1 Memory for Agents 

Agent memory systems maintain information from prior interactions for later use. Much of this work is rooted in conversational personal assistants, where memory centers on past events, personal informa-tion, and user preferences (Maharana et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2024; Hu et al., 2026b). These systems develop memory storage, extraction, and organiza-tion mechanisms to retain and retrieve information across interactions (Sumers et al., 2023; Zhong et al., 2024; Packer et al., 2023; Chhikara et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026). Together, these efforts help agents preserve relevant context across interactions. 

2.2 Learning from Task Experience 

Another line of work extracts reusable knowledge from task experience. Some approaches distill ex-perience into executable skills (Wang et al., 2023; Ni et al., 2026), action workflows (Wang et al., 2024), reflections (Shinn et al., 2023), reasoning in-sights (Ouyang et al., 2026), and playbooks (Zhang et al., 2026b). While several methods validate ac-quired knowledge before reuse, they rely on replay-ing tasks or evaluating on held-out tasks (Alzubi et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026b), which requires additional task execution that is costly and often infeasible once the environment changes. In contrast, our method assesses knowl-edge using evidence from the ongoing task stream. 

2.3 Reliability of Agentic Memory 

The reliability of stored knowledge can deterio-rate as agents accumulate experience. Recent stud-ies identify errors introduced by repeated consoli-dation (Zhang et al., 2026a), stale knowledge un-der environmental change (Chao et al., 2026), and failures when agents consume conflicting memo-ries (Chen et al., 2026). Complementary empiri-cal work examines how memory management af-fects agents’ experience-following behavior (Xiong et al., 2026). Beyond characterizing these risks, TrustMem evaluates memory updates for coverage, preservation, and faithfulness, and uses preference-guided reinforcement learning to improve consol-idation (Yang et al., 2026a). Our work focuses on assessing the reliability of extracted knowledge using evidence from the ongoing task stream. 

3 Reliability of Knowledge Extraction in Existing Systems 

We first diagnose reliability failures in arti-facts produced by four representative systems: Mem0 (Chhikara et al., 2025), ACE (Zhang et al., 2026b), Trace2Skill (Ni et al., 2026), and Skil-lOpt (Yang et al., 2026b). We then introduce EKA to govern knowledge reuse and evaluate it with the SkillOpt and Trace2Skill extractors. All analyses in this section useDeepSeek-V4-Flash (DeepSeek-AI, 2026) on three task streams (DB, C OHORT , and C ODEBASE ) from Continual Learning Bench (Asawa et al., 2026) (Section 5.1).

3.2 Evidence-Based Knowledge Admission 

We introduce Evidence-based Knowledge Ad-mission (EKA) to govern reuse through evidence. EKA distinguishes claims by whether environment feedback in their source trajectories already estab-lishes them. A claim directly verified by such feed-back can be admitted immediately. For example, after an agent inspects a database schema, the claim “in schema version 1, order amounts are stored in 

orders.amount ” is confirmed by the inspection output. In contrast, a claim that generalizes beyond what was observed remains pending until corrob-orated by subsequent experience. For example, the rule “for realized-revenue queries, pending or-ders do not contribute to revenue” is inferred from the records seen in a single task, so it is admit-ted or rejected only after further evidence emerges. The ongoing task stream thus serves as a source of both knowledge and evidence for its reuse. EKA encompasses atomization, claim routing, relation judgment, evidence accumulation, admission and forgetting, and rendering for reuse (Section 4.4).

4.1 Setup: Continual Learning from Task Streams 

We consider a task agent that interacts with an environment over a stream of tasks 

T = {x1, . . . , x T }.

Solving task xt produces a trajectory τt consist-ing of the agent’s actions, environment observa-tions, and feedback. We focus on agents built on black-box models whose parameters remain fixed; continual learning therefore occurs through reusable knowledge acquired from past trajecto-ries and supplied in context for future tasks. The goal is to improve task performance and execution efficiency as experience accumulates. We define the j-th batch as 

Bj = {τ(j−1) n+1 , . . . , τ jn }.

After completing Bj , the system updates its persis-tent knowledge state from Sj−1 to Sj and produces a textual knowledge file Mj for solving the subse-quent batch. The remaining question is how this knowledge state should be constructed and updated from task experience. Throughout, a claim denotes a self-contained unit of reusable knowledge, whether newly ex-tracted or stored in the persistent state. Equivalent extracted claims are consolidated into a canonical claim, stored with its metadata, provenance, evi-dence, relations, and admission route. We use u for a newly extracted claim and v for a stored claim. Admission is reassessed after each batch. 

4.2 Knowledge Representation 

Inspired by a knowledge taxonomy from edu-cational psychology (Anderson and Krathwohl, 2001), we represent task experience using four complementary knowledge types, illustrated below with examples from the DB task. Factual knowl-edge captures task-relevant properties of entities, states, and outcomes (e.g., “ fdbk_g1.ts stores Unix timestamps in milliseconds”). Conceptual knowledge captures relations or regularities that organize multiple facts (e.g., “timestamp encod-ings differ across feedback tables: milliseconds, seconds, or ISO date strings”). Procedural knowl-edge specifies executable methods or workflows (e.g., “to filter by a top-level category, use cat_lvl = 1 AND cat_nm = ’<Category>’ ”). Metacog-nitive knowledge concerns when to apply, select, or revise a method (e.g., “when a category filter re-turns unexpected results, verify the hierarchy level before revising the query”). 44.3 Hierarchical Knowledge Extraction 

The four knowledge types differ in their level of abstraction (Anderson and Krathwohl, 2001). Fac-tual and procedural knowledge describe concrete observations and task-solving operations (Ander-son, 1982), which can be directly grounded in environment feedback and agent trajectories. In contrast, conceptual and metacognitive knowledge capture higher-level regularities and strategic judg-ment (Nelson, 1990), which typically require fur-ther abstraction over factual and procedural knowl-edge, respectively. We therefore organize knowl-edge extraction into two stages: first extracting fac-tual and procedural knowledge, and then deriving abstract conceptual and metacognitive knowledge from them, respectively. For each batch Bj , since each type requires a dis-tinct extraction objective, we instantiate four type-specific extraction subagents that operate without access to stored claims. We first extract first-order factual and procedural knowledge grounded in the environment feedback in the trajectories: 

Fj = EF (Bj ), Pj = EP (Bj ).

Second-order knowledge is then extracted condi-tioned on both the trajectories and the correspond-ing first-order knowledge: 

Cj = EC (Bj , F j ), Gj = EM (Bj , P j ),

where Cj and Gj denote conceptual and metacog-nitive knowledge, respectively. Each extractor pro-cesses the trajectories in the batch jointly. 

4.4 Evidence-Based Knowledge Admission (EKA) 

EKA evaluates claims from the ongoing task stream. Inspired by sequential hypothesis test-ing (Wald, 1992) and truth maintenance (Doyle, 1979), we keep eligible but insufficiently corrobo-rated claims pending and update their evidence as subsequent trajectories provide support or opposi-tion. EKA proceeds through stages below. 

4.4.1 Atomization 

An extractor output may bundle multiple claims within a single item. Admitting or rejecting such an item as a whole using EKA might let the unsup-ported part into context or discard the supported part. Inspired by atomic decomposition (Min et al., 2023), we use an atomizer across all four knowl-edge types, splitting claims only when their com-ponents are self-contained and independent. Each resulting atom must be grounded in the environ-ment feedback relevant to its own content, either directly or, for second-order knowledge, through its first-order claims. The atomic claims serve as the basic units for relation judgment and evidence accumulation. We denote those extracted from batch Bj by Uj = {u1, . . . , u |Uj |}. Appendix C reports technical details including prompts and out-put schemas. 

4.4.2 Claim Routing and Relation Judgment Admission routing. A routing agent examines each atomic claim u, its cited environment-feedback spans, and the explanation linking them. A claim is directly grounded if the cited feedback itself states the claim under its stated scope, and in-directly grounded if it follows from such feedback through inference or abstraction, possibly via its first-order parent claims; claims with neither are excluded. The grounding type determines the ad-mission route. Directly grounded claims follow the 

immediate admission path, since the feedback al-ready establishes them. Indirectly grounded claims follow the deferred admission path, since their full scope still requires corroboration, for example because of causal attribution or conditions beyond those observed. We denote the route by 

z(u) ∈ { IMMEDIATE , DEFERRED }.

The route depends on whether the evidence covers a claim’s scope, not on its knowledge type. A factual claim such as “schema versions use completed as the finalized label” is deferred, since an observa-tion from version 1 does not cover its cross-version scope. Conversely, a conceptual claim can be ad-mitted immediately when it only relates directly observed facts within their observed scope, such as “timestamp encodings differ across fdbk_g1 ,

fdbk_g2 , and fdbk_g3 ”. Routing decisions are recorded when extracted claims are integrated into the persistent state.

Admission. We use the admission threshold θ(v),set to 1 for immediate claims and k for deferred claims. Forgetting. To keep the state bounded, a claim is forgotten if its evidence stays below its admission threshold for m consecutive batches; reaching the threshold resets this window. The window tolerates temporary lack of support, during which the claim remains pending in the state. 

4.5 Knowledge State Rendering and Injection 

After all updates for batch Bj are complete, we render the admitted claims in the resulting state Sj ,i.e., those satisfying e(v) ≥ θ(v), into a structured textual knowledge file 

Mj = R(Sj ).

Motivated by findings that repeated memory up-dates can degrade stored knowledge (Zhang et al., 2026a; Yang et al., 2026a), we reconstruct the file from the current state rather than incrementally rewriting Mj−1.Finally, the complete rendered file Mj is sup-plied in context to the task agent while it solves the subsequent batch (Appendix D.5). The resulting trajectories provide the next source of experience, closing the continual learning loop.

Structured knowledge outperforms direct ex-perience reuse. RIKA outperforms ICL in ag-gregate on every backbone by 0.046 –0.120 , sug-gesting that structured, selectively admitted claims enable more effective reuse than directly exposing the agent to past trajectories.

7 Conclusion 

We find that approximately 12–33% of entries re-tained by existing experience-reuse systems harm subsequent task performance. To address this, we propose RIKA, which combines hierarchical ex-traction of four knowledge types with EKA, admit-ting claims as evidence accumulates from the task stream without additional validation tasks. RIKA achieves the highest aggregate score on every back-bone across six task streams, and EKA also im-proves SkillOpt and Trace2Skill. 8Limitations 

Our empirical study focuses on six text-based, tool-mediated task streams and four model backbones. Although the streams span heterogeneous domains and interaction patterns, the results may not directly transfer to more diverse settings, where experience also includes perceptual or physical feedback. In addition, we study continual learning through an external knowledge artifact while keeping the un-derlying model parameters fixed. How hierarchical extraction and evidence-based admission interact with parameter-updating approaches remains an open direction. 

Ethical Considerations 

Agent trajectories may contain personal informa-tion that could persist in the knowledge file, so deployment on real user interactions would require filtering or anonymizing trajectories first; our exper-iments use benchmarks without real personal data. Finally, our evaluation covers six text-based, tool-mediated task streams and four model backbones, and it does not show that RIKA is safe for high-stakes domains such as medicine, law, or finance, where domain-specific validation and human over-sight would be required.

Appendix A Protocol for Harmful-Entry Evaluation 

This section details the artifact-freezing schedule, entry-use conditions, and repeated paired evalua-tions used in Section 3.1. We use DeepSeek-V4-Flash throughout this diagnostic evaluation. Each comparison pairs the complete artifact with a ver-sion that removes only the target entry. 

A.1 Artifact Freezing and Evaluation Windows 

For each method and task stream, we evaluate three frozen artifacts. We freeze each artifact at the pre-scribed point in its task stream and evaluate its entries on subsequent instances. All instance in-dices below are one-based and refer to positions in the ordered stream for the corresponding run. • DB: we freeze the artifact immediately after in-stance 30 and evaluate its entries on the final 10 instances (31–40). The schema migration occurs between instances 20 and 21, before the artifact is frozen, so the evaluation window lies entirely within the post-migration stage. • COHORT : we freeze the artifact after instance 10, halfway through the 20-instance stream, and evaluate its entries on instances 11–20. • CODEBASE : we freeze the artifact after instance 9 (the Tablib stage) and evaluate its entries on instances 10–19 (the Tenacity stage). Each evaluated artifact therefore contains only ex-perience available at its freezing point, and every evaluation instance occurs strictly after that point. The artifact remains fixed throughout its evaluation window and across all five paired evaluation seeds for each entry. 

A.2 Entry-Level Reuse Conditions 

To ensure consistent entry granularity across sys-tems, we first decompose every frozen artifact from every evaluated system into atomic claims. Each evaluation entry a denotes one atomic claim, and 

A denotes the complete collection of claims for that artifact. This normalization gives all systems the same unit of comparison, irrespective of how their original artifacts group knowledge into en-tries. We evaluate each claim by removing only that claim while retaining all other claims in the artifact. For retrieval-based systems, we include an instance in I(a) only if the system’s retrieval mechanism would select a for that instance from the complete frozen artifact. The original system’s exposure pattern determines the eligible task set for each claim. Entries that are never retrieved during the evaluation window, and hence have I(a) = ∅,are excluded from both evaluation and the denom-inator of the harmful-entry rate. For systems that supply the entire artifact as a knowledge file, all en-tries are exposed at every instance, so all instances in the corresponding evaluation window are eligi-ble. For each eligible instance, we first obtain the knowledge context from the complete frozen arti-fact A. The paired entry-removed condition deletes only a from that context, keeping all other supplied entries unchanged. For retrieval-based systems, both conditions use the same retrieved context, with only the target atomic claim removed in the paired condition. For systems that supply the entire arti-fact, the paired condition supplies A−a = A \ { a}.This measures the effect of retaining a while keep-ing the rest of the supplied context fixed.

C Prompts and Worked Example 

This section specifies RIKA’s task-independent prompts, operational instructions, and output fields. Extraction prompts receive only the current trajec-tory batch and, for second-order extraction, the corresponding first-order outputs. All components are instructed to preserve evidential scope and to avoid filling missing details with model knowledge. 

C.1 Prompt Templates and Output Schemas Feedback-grounding requirement. All ex-tracted claims must be grounded, directly or indirectly, in environment feedback contained in the source trajectories. Here, environment feedback includes environment observations, tool outputs, errors, and task outcomes. Each candidate must identify the relevant feedback and its connection to the claim; actions and agent reasoning provide context but do not indepen-dently justify extraction. Indirect grounding may involve inference or abstraction through factual or procedural claims, but must remain traceable to the original feedback. Claims without such a connection are excluded before admission routing. 

Shared extraction instruction. Each extractor receives the following common instruction, fol-lowed by a type-specific objective:  

> Extract only reusable claims that have a direct or indirect grounding in environment feedback in the supplied trajectories. Environment feedback includes environment observations, tool outputs, errors, and task outcomes. Start from this feed-back and identify what it establishes or reasonably suggests. Actions, plans, and agent reasoning may provide context, but cannot independently ground a claim; do not extract generic knowledge merely because it appears in the trajectory. For every claim, cite the minimal feedback spans, mark the grounding as direct or indirect, and explain the connection. For indirect claims, state the infer-ence or abstraction and what remains unverified; trace any first-order parent claims back to their original feedback. Write self-contained claims whose scope states the task, entities, conditions, and environment version needed for reuse. Pre-serve uncertainty and exceptions. Do not infer success from an attempted action or present a hypothesis as an observed fact. Exclude every claim without this feedback connection. Return an empty list if no eligible reusable claim exists.

The factual and procedural extractors receive a serialized trajectories field. The conceptual ex-tractor additionally receives factual_items , and the metacognitive extractor additionally receives 

procedural_items . These first-order items in-clude their IDs, scopes, feedback spans, grounding explanations, and provenance fields. The four type-specific objectives are: • Factual extractor: identify concrete properties of entities, schemas, states, tool behavior, feed-back, and observed outcomes. Cite the environ-ment feedback establishing or suggesting each property, and keep statements tied to the versions and conditions actually observed. • Procedural extractor: identify executable oper-ations or workflows whose behavior is grounded in environment feedback in the trajectories. Spec-ify ordered actions, required inputs or tools, prerequisites, expected outputs, and observed failure-recovery steps. Link the relevant actions to observed tool outputs, errors, or outcomes. Ac-tion sequences alone do not qualify. Do not claim that a procedure is generally effective merely be-cause it appeared in a successful trajectory. • Conceptual extractor: given the trajectories and extracted factual claims, identify relations or reg-ularities that organize multiple facts. Each con-ceptual claim must cite the factual claim IDs on which it depends, trace them to the original feed-back, and explain the abstraction connecting that feedback to the claim. It must not merely restate one fact. • Metacognitive extractor: given the trajecto-ries and extracted procedural claims, identify evidence-grounded guidance about when to se-lect, avoid, monitor, or revise a procedure. Each metacognitive claim must cite the procedural claim IDs on which it depends, trace them to the original feedback, and explain how the ob-served success, failure, or recovery motivates the guidance. Each extracted item follows a shared field-level schema: • id , type , and claim : a local identifier, one of the four knowledge types, and a self-contained claim. • scope : the task family, entities, conditions, pre-requisites, and environment or schema version under which the claim is stated. 13 • source_trajectories and evidence_spans :immutable trajectory IDs and the minimal environment-feedback spans grounding the claim; action or reasoning context may accom-pany but cannot replace those spans. • grounding_type and 

grounding_explanation : direct or indi-rect grounding, and a short explanation linking the cited feedback to the exact claim, includ-ing any inference, abstraction, or unverified extension. • parent_ids : empty for first-order factual and procedural claims; factual IDs for conceptual claims; and procedural IDs for metacognitive claims. 

Atomization. The atomizer is conditioned on the knowledge type and receives one extracted claim at a time:  

> Decompose the extracted claim into the smallest claims that can each stand alone and be reused independently. Split conjunctions only when each part remains meaningful with its own scope and evidence. Do not split inseparable steps, remove qualifiers, broaden scope, or introduce new con-tent. For each atom, retain only the feedback spans that ground that atom, preserve the rele-vant parent lineage, and update the direct or indi-rect grounding explanation. Exclude an atom if no feedback span directly or indirectly grounds it. If the extracted claim is already atomic and feedback-grounded, return it unchanged.

grounding_type , and grounding_explanation .

After each batch, admission is recomputed with thresholds of 1 for immedi-ate claims and k for deferred claims. The pro-gram groups all currently admitted claims under the four knowledge-type headings and serializes each canonical claim once as a scoped bullet, as described in Appendix D.5. Stored claim text and its scope, prerequisites, exceptions, and environ-ment version are preserved; internal IDs, evidence counts, provenance, routing decisions, and relation labels are omitted. Empty type headings are omit-ted. The resulting document replaces the previous knowledge block at each batch boundary.

and pending . Summing all matching orders again gives an incorrect answer, whereas filtering state = settled gives the correct answer. 

Hierarchical extraction and atomization. From 

τa, the factual extractor records the schema fields, lifecycle values, and feedback on the aggregation results. τc, conditioned on the procedural record of failed reuse, schema inspection, and successful query re-vision: When a previously successful query fails af-ter a schema change, re-inspect the current schema before reusing the earlier query procedure. The conceptual and metacognitive claims retain links to their factual and procedural parents, respectively. The metacognitive claim is supported by feedback from the schema-change recovery in τc.

Routing decisions. Version-specific schema facts are routed to I MMEDIATE because inspec-tion directly establishes them. The demonstrated filtered-sum procedures are also immediate under the observed task conditions. The conceptual claim is deferred because its task-stream scope extends beyond the records and conditions observed in one trajectory. The metacognitive claim is deferred when it appears in the third batch because it gener-alizes one recovery into guidance for future schema changes. The schema-inspection output directly grounds the version-specific facts; the query-result feedback directly grounds the procedures under the observed conditions. D.1 Claim Schema and Persistent State 

The state Sj = ( Vj , E j , H j , Lj ) contains canoni-cal claims, entailment edges, other relation annota-tions, and an observation ledger. Each stored claim has: • Content and scope: canonical ID, atomic con-tent, aliases, knowledge type, task family, enti-ties, prerequisites, and environment/schema ver-sion. • Provenance and evidence: source trajectory IDs, batches, environment-feedback spans, di-rect or indirect grounding and its explanation, extraction lineage tracing parent claims to origi-nal feedback, batch-level support and opposition counts, and cumulative net evidence e(v).• Routing and activity: admission route z(v) and its source justification; creation and last-evidence batches; and below-threshold counter. Admis-sion is derived from the current evidence count and route after each batch. • Relations: incident edges and judgments with their provenance. The admitted set is recomputed from the claims in 

Vj . Claims below their admission threshold remain pending until they meet the forgetting condition. Forgetting removes the claim, its stored evidence, and its incident relations from the persistent state. If equivalent knowledge is extracted again later, it is processed as a new claim.

D.4 End-to-End Pseudocode 

Algorithm 1 combines model-based extraction, at-omization, routing, and relation judgment with model-produced batch evidence counts, cumula-tive state updates, and batch-wise admission. Unresolved rela-tions are re-judged or withheld. Extraction uses the current trajectories and corresponding first-order outputs. Each claim’s admission route is deter-mined by its cited environment feedback, while entailment edges transfer support. Forgetting and serialization follow Appendices D.3 and D.5.

All experiments on Continual Learning Bench strictly follow the benchmark protocols and settings provided in its official GitHub repository (Asawa et al., 2026), including task-stream construction, stage boundaries, within-stage permutations, en-vironment action budgets, and reward computa-tion. We apply these benchmark-defined protocols consistently across RIKA and all baselines.
