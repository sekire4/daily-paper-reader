Abstract. Dense retrievers are commonly trained with hard negatives selected from lexical or dense retrieval results. Retrieval proximity, how-ever, does not indicate whether a candidate conflicts with explicit domain structure. We introduce CA-HNM , a constraint-aware hard-negative mining framework that combines retrieval-based candidate generation with deterministic incompatibility rules derived from a structured do-main model. The framework removes known positives, constructs a local typed context around the query target, records relational, scope, target, and level mismatches, and uses the resulting candidates to fine-tune a standard dual encoder. Structured information is required only during offline negative construction. We evaluate CA-HNM on MOOCCubeX and Course-Skill Atlas with two encoders, two training losses, and ten seeds. Relative to a retrieval-rank-matched control, CA-HNM -Pure has higher mean NDCG@10 in six of eight dataset–encoder–loss configura-tions. Four hierarchical 95% bootstrap intervals are entirely above zero, mostly under triplet loss. In a separate experiment, CA-HNM -Mixed shows slightly higher mean NDCG@10 than a matched-mixed control on both collections. Structural variants show that changes in available struc-ture can also change candidate eligibility and negative-pool composition. The results indicate that the contribution of constraint-aware selection depends on the training objective and the composition of the negative pool. 

Keywords: Dense retrieval · Hard-negative mining · Structured domain models · Educational retrieval · Contrastive learning 

1 Introduction 

Dense retrieval models are typically trained by contrasting a relevant document with one or more documents treated as negatives. The choice of negatives has a arXiv:2610.35045v1 [cs.IR] 26 Oct 2026 direct effect on the training signal: random negatives are often too easy, whereas lexical or dense retrieval can provide candidates that are close to the query and therefore more informative [9, 27, 16, 29]. Existing hard-negative strategies consequently define hardness mainly through lexical score, embedding similarity, or retrieval rank. These signals are useful for locating difficult candidates, but they do not determine whether an unjudged candidate is a valid negative. Incomplete relevance judgments can leave missing positives or ambiguous cases among highly ranked documents [2]. Structured domains provide another source of information. Concepts may be linked through prerequisite, hierarchical, related-concept, scope, or level relations. A document can therefore be retrieval-hard while still conflicting with the target represented by the query. For example, a resource about advanced SQL optimization may be close to a query for “beginner SQL joins” even though its target concept and level differ. Such structure can be used during training-data construction without changing the retriever architecture. We introduce CA-HNM , a constraint-aware hard-negative mining framework. Starting from lexical and dense retrieval results, CA-HNM removes known positives, constructs a local typed context for the query target, evaluates the remaining candidates with deterministic compatibility rules, and records the rule evidence associated with each selected negative. The structured model is used only during offline negative construction; training and inference use a standard dual encoder. To evaluate the proposed selection mechanism, the empirical study addresses three research questions: RQ1 - How does constraint-aware negative selection compare with retrieval-rank-matched selection? RQ2 - What is the contribution of constraint-aware negatives within a mixed negative pool? RQ3 - How do changes in the structured information available to the classifier affect negative selection and retrieval performance? The evaluation covers MOOCCubeX and Course-Skill Atlas, BGE and E5 encoders [26, 25], triplet loss and cached multiple-negatives ranking loss (cached MNRL) [5], and ten training seeds. The contributions are threefold. First, we formulate hard-negative mining by separating retrieval hardness, positive annotation, and operational incompatibility under a structured domain model. Second, we define a mining pipeline that associates selected negatives with explicit relation, scope, target, or level evidence while leaving the inference-time retriever unchanged. Third, we provide a multi-seed evaluation aligned with these research questions through rank-matched, matched-mixed, and structural controls across multiple encoders, losses, and datasets. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews hard-negative mining, generated supervision, and structured knowledge in retrieval. Section 3 defines the problem, and Section 4 presents CA-HNM . Section 5 describes the experimental protocol. Section 6 reports the results, followed by the discussion in Section 7 and the conclusion in Section 8. 2 Related Work 

Prior work relevant to this study spans three areas: hard-negative mining for dense retrieval, synthetic retrieval supervision, and the use of structured knowledge in information retrieval. We focus on how these approaches construct or select training signals for retrieval models. Dense Passage Retrieval and Sentence-BERT use bi-encoder representations for efficient semantic retrieval [9, 18], while ColBERT and SPLADE use late in-teraction and learned sparse representations [10, 4]. BEIR highlights performance variation across domains and tasks [22]. For negative selection, ANCE mines approximate nearest neighbors [27], Rock-etQA combines large candidate pools with denoising [16], ADORE selects highly ranked dense negatives [29], and BM25 supplies lexically similar candidates [20]. Negative effectiveness depends on difficulty and sampling strategy [6, 17]. How-ever, unjudged high-ranking documents may be relevant when judgments are incomplete [2], motivating selection signals beyond retrieval proximity. A complementary line of work creates additional supervision for retriever training. Promptagator generates task-specific queries from a small number of examples [3], InPars generates synthetic query–document pairs [1], and GPL combines query generation with pseudo-labeling for domain adaptation [24]. SyNeg more directly generates semantic hard negatives using large language models [14]. These approaches modify or generate training examples from text or model predictions. They demonstrate that retriever supervision can be improved by controlling the composition of the training data, but they do not generally exploit explicit domain relations to determine whether an already retrieved candidate is compatible with the query target. Ontologies, taxonomies, and knowledge graphs have been used for semantic search, query expansion, entity-oriented retrieval, and explanation [23, 19]. Ex-plicit constraints over RDF knowledge graphs have also been used to identify recommendations compatible with user preferences [11]. Structured knowledge is also used in knowledge-intensive retrieval and retrieval-augmented generation, where retrieved evidence supports downstream reasoning or generation [15, 13]. In these settings, structured information is typically incorporated during retrieval, reranking, reasoning, or generation. Educational retrieval provides a suitable setting for studying structured signals because learning resources are often associated with explicit concept relations. Competency graphs have also been used to guide learning-resource annotation with supporting textual evi-dence [12]. MOOCCubeX provides learning concepts and prerequisite relations [28], while Course-Skill Atlas provides hierarchical and related-skill structure over higher-education curricula [8]. Taken together, prior work has studied retrieval-based hardness, generated supervision, and structured knowledge mainly as separate mechanisms. The gap addressed in this paper is the use of structured-domain incompatibility specifically during offline hard-negative selection. CA-HNM follows this direction while leaving the underlying dual-encoder architecture and inference procedure unchanged. 3 Problem Formulation 

We distinguish three properties involved in hard-negative construction: re-trieval hardness, absence of positive annotation, and incompatibility under an explicit structured domain model. 

3.1 Dense Retrieval and Retrieval Hardness 

Let Q be a set of queries and D a collection of documents. For each query 

q ∈ Q , let P (q) ⊆ D denote the set of documents annotated as relevant. Because relevance judgments may be incomplete, d / ∈ P (q) means that d is not a known positive; it does not by itself establish non-relevance. A dual encoder with parameters θ maps queries and documents into a shared vector space and computes 

sθ (q, d ) = ⟨fθ (q), g θ (d)⟩. (1) For a positive document d+ ∈ P (q) and a sampled negative set N (q), the training objective depends on the construction of N (q). Conventional hard-negative mining selects unjudged candidates that satisfy a retrieval-hardness criterion: 

Nret (q) = {d ∈ D \ P (q) : H(q, d ) ≥ λ}. (2) Here, H(q, d ) is defined so that larger values correspond to greater hardness; it may be based on BM25 score, dense similarity, or an equivalent transformation of retrieval rank. Equation 2 identifies difficult candidates but does not provide a domain-based incompatibility criterion. 

3.2 Structured Incompatibility 

We represent the structured information used by the miner as 

O = ( C, R, A, M), (3) where C is a set of concepts, R is a set of typed relations, A contains concept attributes such as labels, aliases, descriptions, or levels, and M ⊆ D × C contains document–concept associations. The structured component may correspond to an ontology, taxonomy, knowledge graph, controlled vocabulary, or typed concept graph. For each query q, let cq ∈ C denote its target concept. The structured model is not assumed to provide complete relevance judgments. Instead, we define an operational incompatibility indicator I(q, d, O). It is one when a candidate satisfies at least one implemented incompatibility rule and zero otherwise. The associated violation set is 

ϕ(q, d, O) ⊆ T , (4) where T contains relation, scope or granularity, target, and level mismatch types. The violation set records the rule evidence used during mining; it is not an independent relevance judgment. Figure 1 illustrates the distinction. 4 Constraint-Aware Hard Negative Mining 

In this section, we present CA-HNM , short for Constraint-Aware Hard Neg-ative Mining . The framework combines conventional retrieval hardness with structured-domain incompatibility during negative selection and operationalizes Equation 5. It proceeds in three main phases: candidate construction, struc-tured incompatibility classification, and negative selection for retriever training. These phases are implemented through retrieval, positive removal, target-context construction, constraint classification, negative selection, training-example ma-terialization, and dual-encoder fine-tuning. 4.3 Negative Selection, Controls, and Training 

We evaluate two uses of the constraint-aware pool. In Pure constraint-aware selection , CA-HNM -Pure groups eligible candidates by their primary violation type and selects them in round-robin order across non-empty groups, while preserving retrieval order within each group. At most four explicit negatives are retained per query, limiting the influence of any single frequent violation category. As a control, retrieval-rank-matched selection uses the same retrieved candidate pool but does not consult O. For each constraint-aware negative at retrieval position r, the control searches candidates in increasing absolute rank distance from r and selects the first eligible non-positive candidate, with ties resolved according to the original retrieval order. This provides a comparison at approx-imately matched retrieval positions without using structured incompatibility during selection. 

Mixed negative pools: The mixed strategy combines conventional and constraint-aware sources: 

Nmix (q) = Nrand (q) ∪ N BM25 (q) ∪ N dense (q) ∪ bNO (q). (11) Duplicates are removed before applying the four-negative budget. The source-specific pools are processed with the same deterministic allocation procedure for CA-HNM -Mixed and matched-mixed; the latter differs only by replacing the constraint-aware source with rank-matched candidates. This comparison therefore evaluates the constraint-aware component within the same mixed-pool construction. Training examples and fine-tuning: Selected examples are materialized as 

 q, d +, N (q), (12) where d+ ∈ P (q) is a positive document and N (q) is the negative set produced by the selected strategy. The examples fine-tune a standard dual encoder using either triplet loss or cached multiple-negatives ranking loss. Structured information influences training only through negative-set composition; it is not used as an encoder input during training or inference. 

5 Experimental Setup 

The experiments follow the three research questions introduced in Section 1: RQ1 compares CA-HNM -Pure with rank-matched selection, RQ2 evaluates 

CA-HNM -Mixed against matched-mixed, and RQ3 examines structural variants. The following subsections describe the experimental setup. 

5.1 Datasets 

We convert MOOCCubeX and Course-Skill Atlas into query–document re-trieval tasks, with concepts or skills as queries and learning resources or curriculum records as documents. Relevance is derived from source concept links. Splits are query-level, with no query or target overlap across train, develop-ment, and test partitions. Because Course-Skill Atlas has more relevant documents per query than MOOCCubeX, results are interpreted separately by collection. In Course-Skill Atlas, each DWA query is linked to institution–field–year profiles whose source top-10 DWA list contains that activity. After deduplication, up to 100 profiles define the binary judged-positive set P (q); profiles beyond this cutoff remain unjudged rather than known negatives. 

5.2 Compared Methods 

The principal experiment compares four negative strategies: 

– DPR-Random : uniformly sampled non-positive documents [9]; 

– DenseNeg : the highest-ranked non-positive documents returned by the initial dense retriever [27, 29]; 

– Rank-matched : non-positive candidates selected from retrieval positions close to those of CA-HNM -Pure, without using structured constraints; 

– CA-HNM -Pure : constraint-aware negatives selected by the procedure in Section 4. For RQ2 , CA-HNM -Mixed is compared with the conventional negative sources and with matched-mixed , which replaces its constraint-aware compo-nent with rank-matched candidates. For RQ3 , we compare CA-HNM -Pure with three structural variants. Label-only retains label/alias evidence while omitting typed relation context; shuffled-graph replaces the original relation neighbor-hood with a shuffled one; and no-structure disables relation-derived context and uses only the remaining non-structural evidence available to the classifier. Because these variants can also change candidate eligibility and negative-pool composition, they are analyzed as end-to-end sensitivity variants rather than relation-level interventions. Table 2. 5.3 Evaluation and Statistical Analysis 

We report NDCG@10, MRR@10, MAP@100, and Recall@100. Both collections use binary qrels. NDCG@10 summarizes top-ranked quality, MRR@10 emphasizes the first relevant result, MAP@100 summarizes precision over relevant documents, and Recall@100 measures coverage in the top 100 ranks [7]. For RQ1 , the paired inferential analysis focuses on the CA-HNM -Pure versus rank-matched difference in NDCG@10. Differences are paired by training seed, and hierarchical 95% bootstrap confidence intervals are obtained by resampling seeds and queries. The remaining metrics provide complementary descriptive evidence about ranking quality and retrieval coverage. For RQ2 , we report the mean NDCG@10 difference between CA-HNM -Mixed and matched-mixed in the BGE/cached-MNRL setting. RQ3 is interpreted as an end-to-end sensitivity analysis because its structural variants can change candidate eligibility as well as the available structured information. Because Course-Skill Atlas contains 32 development queries, interval width is considered together with the mean difference.

7 Discussion 

The rank-matched comparison distinguishes the effect of negative source from differences in retrieval position. The results indicate that the difference between constraint-aware and rank-matched selection depends on the training configuration, with a more consistent positive pattern under triplet loss than under cached MNRL. This pattern may reflect how the two objectives use explicit negatives. Triplet loss contrasts each query–positive pair with the selected negative, whereas cached MNRL distributes competition across explicit, cached, and in-batch examples. The present experiments do not estimate the interaction between loss function and negative selection, so this interpretation remains to be tested directly. The mixed-pool experiment separates the performance of the complete neg-ative pool from the contribution of its constraint-aware component. The mean NDCG@10 differences between CA-HNM -Mixed and matched-mixed are small on both collections under BGE with cached MNRL. These results indicate that the effect of replacing one negative source can depend on the composition of the remaining training pool. The structural variants show that retrieval performance is sensitive to changes in the information available to the constraint classifier. On Course-Skill Atlas, the shuffled-graph and no-structure variants obtain higher mean scores than the full structured version, whereas the differences are smaller on MOOCCubeX. These comparisons cannot be attributed solely to relation semantics because modifying the structure can also change candidate eligibility and negative-pool composition. A relation-level analysis would require the candidate identities, retrieval positions, and pool sizes to remain fixed while only the structural relations are modified. The rule-based classifier also records the basis of each negative-selection decision. Selected candidates are associated with a violation category and the textual or structured evidence that triggered the corresponding rule. These records provide a means to inspect the composition of the mined negative pool and the relative frequency of different rule types. They should be interpreted as operational mining annotations rather than independently validated relevance judgments. The scope of the study is limited in several respects. Both datasets are from educational domains and contain explicit structured metadata. All reported results use development partitions, and Course-Skill Atlas contains only 32 development queries. The constraint classifier relies on source labels, aliases, lexical evidence, and manually specified rules. Rank matching approximates retrieval position but does not control all candidate characteristics, and the mixed-pool and structural experiments are restricted to BGE with cached MNRL. Further evaluation should therefore include structured domains, and structural controls that preserve candidate identities and pool composition. 

8 Conclusion 

We presented CA-HNM , a hard-negative mining framework that combines retrieval-based candidate generation with structured incompatibility rules while retaining a standard dual encoder at inference time. Across two educational retrieval collections, two encoders, two losses, and ten seeds, CA-HNM -Pure has higher mean NDCG@10 than rank-matched selection in six of eight con-figurations, with four hierarchical 95% bootstrap intervals entirely above zero. The pattern is more consistent under triplet loss than under cached MNRL. In the BGE/cached-MNRL mixed experiment, CA-HNM -Mixed is close to matched-mixed in NDCG@10, while the structural variants show that chang-ing the available structure can substantially alter the resulting negative pool. Future work will extend the analysis to other structured domains, and use fixed-candidate structural perturbations to separate relation semantics from candidate-composition effects.
