# ABSTRACT 

Graph Retrieval-Augmented Generation (GraphRAG) leverages centralized knowl-edge graphs to enhance large language models with structured knowledge retrieval. However, real-world knowledge is inherently distributed across multiple organi-zations rather than being centrally accessible. Privacy constraints further prohibit sharing entire local knowledge graphs, preventing GraphRAG from effectively retrieving and utilizing distributed knowledge. To this end, we propose Feder-ated GraphRAG (FedGRAG), a unified framework for distributed graph retrieval and knowledge-enhanced generation over multiple clients with knowledge graphs. Specifically, we address two fundamental challenges in federated GraphRAG. First, the absence of a global knowledge graph makes unified retrieval impossible. To ad-dress this, we develop a Federated Knowledge Routing mechanism that efficiently identifies relevant clients through lightweight knowledge indices without exposing local graph structures. Second, local graph memory cannot be shared directly because it contains sensitive entities and knowledge information. We therefore propose a controlled-disclosure evidence construction method. It extracts retrieved evidence subgraphs and converts them into an evidence abstract, enabling evidence conversion and reconstruction on the server. Extensive experiments demonstrate that Federated GraphRAG consistently outperforms existing baselines in both re-trieval and downstream generation tasks, establishing a new paradigm for deploying GraphRAG in federated environments. 

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

Graph Retrieval-Augmented Generation (GraphRAG) (Peng et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2025; Procko & Ochoa, 2024) has recently emerged as a promising paradigm for improving Large Language Models (LLMs) (Chang et al., 2024) by incorporating graph-structured knowledge (Liang et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2025c;d; Liang et al., 2025) into the retrieval process. Representative GraphRAG systems, such as Microsoft’s GraphRAG (Han et al., 2024), HyperGraphRAG (Luo et al., 2026a), and LightRAG (Guo et al., 2024), organize knowledge into graph-structured representations to enable graph retrieval, multi-hop reasoning, and knowledge-enhanced generation. Despite their success, existing GraphRAG methods fundamentally rely on a centralized graph repos-itory. This assumes that graph-structured knowledge can be globally constructed, indexed, and retrieved from a unified location. However, in many real-world applications, graph knowledge is inherently distributed across multiple organizations and cannot be centralized due to privacy and ownership (Liu et al., 2025b;a; Han et al., 2026; 2025; Liu et al., 2026b). This motivates the develop-ment of RAG methods for distributed settings. Recent Federated Retrieval-Augmented Generation (FedRAG) methods, such as (Guerraoui et al., 2025; Addison et al., 2024; Zhao, 2024) alleviate this limitation by enabling collaborative retrieval over distributed knowledge repositories Liao et al. (2025); Fu & King (2023). Nevertheless, They are primarily designed for vector-based retrieval and cannot be directly extended to GraphRAG. Unlike vector representations, graph memories contain rich structural semantics, and effective collaborative retrieval requires not only identifying relevant

First, without graph-structured knowledge at local clients, conventional RAG relies mainly on isolated textual retrieval, making it difficult to capture explicit entity relations and multi-hop dependencies. As a result, complex queries often require repeated retrieval over fragmented evidence, increasing retrieval cost while limiting the construction of coherent reasoning paths. Based on the above analysis, we need to design a federated GraphRAG method that supports evidence retrieval augmentation on graphs and collaborative reasoning across distributed clients simultaneously. Inspired by federated graph learning (Liu et al., 2025b), which enables col-laborative optimization over graphs distributed across clients, an intuitive approach is for the server to provide a guidance signal to select the correct client for queries. However, this faces the following challenges: First, the absence of a globally accessible graph makes it difficult to identify relevant and complementary knowledge across clients, forcing queries to search dis-tributed clients individually and introducing substantial retrieval latency and computational over-head. Second, locally retrieved subgraphs contain potentially sensitive entities, relations, and struc-tural information, yet restricting their exchange may obscure the dependencies needed for cross-client reasoning. Evidence integration therefore entails a fundamental tension between preserving query-relevant semantics and relational context and limiting the disclosure of local knowledge.

# 2 RELATED WORK 

2.1 GRAPH RETRIEVAL -A UGMENTED GENERATION 

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) (Gao et al., 2023; Han et al., 2024) enhances LLMs by retrieving external knowledge to improve factual accuracy and reasoning capability. Recently, GraphRAG has emerged as a promising extension of RAG by organizing retrieved knowledge into graph-structured memories. Representative methods include Microsoft’s GraphRAG (Edge et al., 2024), which constructs entity-community graphs to facilitate hierarchical retrieval and global reason-ing. Similarly, LightRAG (Guo et al., 2025) improves retrieval efficiency through lightweight graph 2Published as a conference paper at ICLR 2027 indexing. Moreover, HippoRAG (Guti´ errez et al., 2024) models long-range semantic associations via graph-based memory organization. SubgraphRAG (Li et al., 2025) instead retrieves compact query-relevant subgraphs through lightweight triple scoring and structural-distance modeling, bal-ancing retrieval efficiency with graph reasoning capability. More recently, HippoRAG2 (Guti´ errez et al., 2025) extends graph-based memory toward non-parametric continual learning over newly acquired knowledge. HyperGraphRAG (Luo et al., 2026a) further generalizes conventional pairwise graph relations to hypergraph structures, representing higher-order n-ary relationships through hy-peredges for more expressive knowledge retrieval and generation. GFM-RAG (Luo et al., 2026b) introduces a graph foundation model trained over large-scale heterogeneous knowledge graphs, enabling transferable graph-aware retrieval on unseen datasets without task-specific fine-tuning. 2.2 FEDERATED RETRIEVAL -A UGMENTED GENERATION 

Recent studies have extended RAG to federated settings (Liu et al., 2025b; 2026a; Han et al., 2025; 2026), enabling LLM to leverage distributed knowledge. Federated RAG allows each client to maintain its own knowledge repository and performs collaborative retrieval under the coordination of a central server (Chakraborty et al., 2025). Current FedRAG approaches can be broadly categorized into two directions. Routing-based methods aim to reduce communication overhead by selecting only a subset of relevant clients for each query. Representative examples include RAGRoute (Guerraoui et al., 2025), which learns lightweight routing policies for efficient federated search, and FedE4RAG (Mao et al., 2025), which systematically investigates the effectiveness of different client selection strategies under distributed retrieval settings. Privacy-oriented methods focus on protecting sensitive knowledge during retrieval. Representative frameworks include C-FedRAG (Addison et al., 2024), and FRAG (Zhao, 2024) which improve retrieval security without exposing local knowledge bases. 

# 3 METHODOLOGY 

In this section, we present Federated GraphRAG (FedGRAG) in detail, a unified framework for retrieval and knowledge-enhanced generation on distributed knowledge graphs. The core idea is construct semantic cluster routing for different client on the server; routing queries to the clients, which identify and extract evidentiary subgraphs, and the results are then globally integrated to generate the final retrieval output. FedGRAG consists of three components: (1) federated knowledge routing (2) client evidence representation and (3) collaborative generation. The architecture of FedGRAG is shown in Fig. 2, and its pseudocode appears in Appendix A. 3.1 NOTATION 

We consider a federated GraphRAG system consisting of a central server and M distributed clients. Each client owns a private document corpus D, where the local corpora is generally non-IID. Each client utilize D constructs a knowledge graph G = ( V, E, X), where V, E, and X denote the node set, edge set, and node attribute matrix, respectively. 3.2 FEDERATED KNOWLEDGE ROUTING 

In this section, because knowledge in federated environments is inherently distributed across multiple clients, the server has no prior knowledge of which client contains the information required to answer a given query. Therefore, we propose a knowledge routing mechanism that organizes distributed client knowledge into a unified routing space. The key idea is to represent each client using a set of knowledge prototypes, where each prototype characterizes one representative semantic region covered by the local graph memory. These prototypes are then organized into a global routing space, enabling efficient query routing without exposing local graph memories.

Each client first extracts a query-relevant evidence subgraph from its local graph memory and map the subgraph into evidence representation that captures both semantic information and structural dependencies. The server then collaboratively integrates them from different clients to form a global evidence affects, which serves as the knowledge context for the subsequent generation module.

The global routing graph contains P = M ×K prototype nodes. Graph construction requires O(P 2d)

operations by computing all pairwise prototype similarities. After connecting each prototype to its top-L neighbors, the routing graph contains approximately O(P L ) edges. For each query, initial query-prototype matching requires O(P d ) operations, while T rounds of graph-based relevance propagation require approximately O(T P L ) operations. After selecting B clients, constructing the evidence interaction graph requires O(B2d) operations. Accordingly, the online computational complexity can be summarized as O(M Kd + T M KL + B2d).

B.2 DETAILS OF BASELINES 

We consider representative methods from two categories: graph-based retrieval-augmented generation and federated retrieval-augmented generation. 

Graph-based RAG Methods. 

• GraphRAG (MS) constructs an entity graph from source documents and generates hierar-chical community summaries to support query-focused knowledge aggregation. • LightRAG combines graph-based indexing with dual-level retrieval to capture both specific entities and broader relational information. • HippoRAG integrates knowledge graphs with Personalized PageRank to retrieve related evidence through associative connections across documents. • SubgraphRAG retrieves query-relevant subgraphs using a lightweight triple-scoring model enhanced with directional structural distance features. • HippoRAG2 extends HippoRAG through deeper passage integration and LLM-assisted retrieval, improving the interaction between passage-level information and graph-based associative retrieval. • HyperGraphRAG represents knowledge using hypergraphs, allowing a single relation to connect multiple entities and preserving higher-order relational information. • GFM-RAG employs a graph foundation model trained across multiple knowledge graphs to learn transferable query-conditioned graph retrieval. 

Federated RAG Methods. 

• FRAG supports collaborative approximate nearest-neighbor search over distributed vector databases, using homomorphic encryption to protect query vectors and stored representa-tions. 12 Published as a conference paper at ICLR 2027 • FedE4RAG collaboratively trains local retrieval models through federated embedding learning, incorporating knowledge distillation and homomorphic encryption for model collaboration. • C-FedRAG coordinates retrieval across distributed data providers and uses confidential computing to protect the retrieved context during processing. • FedMosaic encodes local documents into parametric adapters and selectively aggregates relevant adapters to support generation without transmitting raw documents. 

Adaptation to the Federated Setting. For graph-based baselines, indexing and retrieval are performed within individual clients using their assigned local knowledge. The federated variants use random route strategy to select clients and average strategy to combine local retrieval outputs. We preserve each method’s core indexing and retrieval mechanism while using the same corpus partitions and evaluation queries. These configurations are distinguished from centralized execution over the complete corpus. Any changes to the original training, generation, or security mechanisms are explicitly documented. B.3 DETAILS OF EVALUATION METRICS 

We evaluate FedGRAG from three perspectives: evidence retrieval, client routing, and downstream answer generation. All metrics are computed for each query and then averaged over the complete test set. Higher values indicate better performance. 

Recall@ k. Recall@ k measures the proportion of ground-truth supporting evidence covered by the top-k retrieved results. We report Recall@2 and Recall@5 to evaluate retrieval quality under different evidence budgets. A higher Recall@ k indicates that the retrieved results contain more evidence required to answer the query. 

Hit@ k. Hit@ k evaluates the effectiveness of client routing. A query is considered successfully routed when at least one of the top-k selected clients contains ground-truth supporting evidence. We report Hit@2 and Hit@5 and average the results across all test queries. 

Exact Match. Exact Match (EM) measures the percentage of generated answers that exactly match a ground-truth answer after standard text normalization. Following common question-answering evaluation protocols, the normalization procedure converts text to lowercase and removes punctuation, articles, and redundant whitespace. When multiple reference answers are available, a prediction is considered correct if it matches any valid reference. 

Token-level F1. Token-level F1 measures the token overlap between the generated answer and the ground-truth answer by combining token-level precision and recall. Compared with EM, F1 assigns partial credit to answers that overlap substantially with the reference but do not match it exactly. When multiple reference answers are provided, we report the maximum F1 score obtained against all valid references. B.4 DETAIL OF IMPLEMENTATION
