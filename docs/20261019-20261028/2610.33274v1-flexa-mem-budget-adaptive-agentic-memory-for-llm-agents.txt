Abstract 

Large language model (LLM) agents work in scenarios with very different memory needs. A question about past conversations needs the ex-act facts it names, while a multi-step task needs a trace of the steps already taken together with details that the next step cannot name. Existing memories are each designed and tested for one kind of scenario. Retrieval memories such as A-Mem miss steps that the current query does not express, summaries drop exact strings, and pairing the two fixes the split of the budget in advance. We propose FlexA-Mem, a general memory that writes each unit of history once into an immutable store and renders a fresh memory for every call. The rendered memory is a compact timeline of records plus detail that a fixed rule fits into the remaining room with-out an LLM call. The detail is either relevant units as raw text or excerpts or the verbatim pieces that best match the query. With one store, FlexA-Mem serves both kinds of scenario. On AppWorld challenge tasks at 4k, it outperforms the second-best method by 11.5%, and on 147 unused tasks with predictions fixed in advance it outperforms A-Mem with a summary by 12.2%. On LongMemEval and LoCoMo, it matches a dedicated keyword retriever that writes no memory, while memories built on text written by an LLM fall far behind at tight budgets. Ablations show that the timeline car-ries AppWorld completion, whereas recovery mainly saves steps there and decides accuracy on exact facts. Code is available at https: //github.com/WujiangXu/Flex-Amem .

1 Introduction 

Large language model (LLM) agents now work in many settings. They call the APIs of apps (Trivedi et al., 2024), resolve software issues by editing and testing code (Jimenez et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2024), and answer questions about conversations held over dozens of sessions (Wu et al., 2025a). In each setting the history soon outgrows a single call, and models use the middle of a long context less reliably (Liu et al., 2024). Therefore, each call works under a fixed token budget, and memory decides what fills it. What should fill the budget depends on the sce-nario. A question about a past conversation usually names its evidence and depends on a few exact facts, such as a date mentioned weeks before. A multi-step task depends first on an overview of what the agent has done. An AppWorld agent that sends money must log in, list the APIs and read their documentation, and if these steps leave its context it starts them again. It also needs exact details that change from step to step, such as an API argument or an earlier output. Some calls need all of a few past units, and others need a little of every past unit. Existing memories are each designed for one of these needs and tested on benchmarks of the matching kind. Retrieval memories such as A-Mem (Xu et al., 2025a) return the notes closest to the query, which suits fact recall, but an agent asked to send money has no reason to search for its earlier login. Compaction replaces old observations with placeholders or an LLM summary (Wang et al., 2025c; Lindenbauer et al., 2025), which keeps an overview of a long trajectory but drops the exact strings a fact question asks for. Summary+A-Mem combines the two with a fixed split of the budget, and MemGPT and Sculptor leave hidden details to tools (Packer et al., 2023; Li et al., 2026a) that agents rarely call (Fan et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026b). As a result, each method works well in its own scenario but loses much of its strength in the other. A memory that generalizes across scenarios is hard to build because the right balance between overview and detail is not known when the history is written (Figure 1). It depends on the scenario, on the step within a task and on the budget of the call. Nor can the query alone settle it. For this reason, a memory that commits at write time, by summarizing, by evolving notes or by fixing a split, is tuned to one scenario by construction. The decision has to be made at every call, and it must stay cheap, because an agent calls the model at every step. To this end, we propose FlexA-Mem, a general memory that writes each unit of history once and renders it anew at every call. The store keeps the raw text of every unit, and each rendering shows a timeline of short records that does not depend on the query. It fills the remaining room with recov-ered detail by a fixed rule, without an LLM call. Our contributions are summarized as follows. • We introduce a write-once, render-per-call de-sign for agent memory. It stores every unit of history immutably and decides only at read time how much of each unit a call sees. Nothing is discarded before the scenario, the query and the budget are known. • We propose fit-or-degrade, a budget-aware ren-derer that needs no LLM call. It gives each rele-vant unit the highest resolution that fits, raw text, an excerpt or its record, and turns more units into excerpts as the budget shrinks. As a drop-in renderer it raises the AppWorld completion of A-Mem’s own notes by 10.7%. • We show that one store serves both kinds of sce-nario. FlexA-Mem outperforms the second-best method by 11.5% on AppWorld challenge tasks and the strongest baseline by 12.2% on 147 un-used tasks, and on fact recall it matches a ded-icated keyword retriever. In our ablations the timeline carries agent completion, while recov-ery saves steps there and decides accuracy when the answer is an exact fact. 

2 Related Work 

Memory is essential for personalized agents (Liang et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2025b), enabling them to tailor their services to individual users’ needs and preferences. Most agent memories store past inter-actions and retrieve a few at each call (Park et al., 2023; Zhong et al., 2024; Chhikara et al., 2025; Rasmussen et al., 2025). A-Mem links its notes with an LLM and lets new notes rewrite their neigh-bors (Xu et al., 2025a), and unretrieved steps are absent from the call. A second line keeps the his-tory at several levels of detail (Packer et al., 2023; Sarthi et al., 2024; Rezazadeh et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2026a). MemGPT, RAPTOR and MemTree fix the level at write time, while MemGAS and SCM choose it with an LLM (Wang et al., 2025a) and Nemori adds raw messages of top episodes (Ma et al., 2026). The closest designs choose a form per item under a budget (Liao et al., 2026; Kang et al., 2026b). ReadAgent lets an LLM swap gists for raw pages (Lee et al., 2024), and AFM falls back from full to compressed to placeholder forms while pack-ing oldest first (Cruz, 2025). A third line lets the agent manage its context through tools (Hu et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026a; Ehrlich and Blackman, 2026; Li et al., 2026b) or learns this with reinforcement learning (Sun et al., 2025; Ye et al., 2026; Zhou et al., 2026; Yan et al., 2026), but agents seldom act on their own (Fan et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026b). FlexA-Mem builds on the two closest designs, taking degradation within a budget from AFM and in-place replacement of a gist by raw text from ReadAgent. Keeping raw text, several resolutions and in-place replacement are each known. The new design makes these choices at read time by a fixed rule, without an LLM call or an action of the agent. Unlike A-Mem it never rewrites a note, and unlike AFM it keeps a record of every unit and visits units by relevance rather than age. Our experiments then show that one store serves both kinds of scenario. Appendix D covers context compression and other memory systems in more detail.

3.1 Writing Memory 

When a unit ui leaves the recent window, FlexA-Mem writes it once as a note mi =(ri, s i, f i, e i, L i, Πi). The raw text ri is the unit ex-actly as it occurred and never changes. One LLM call reads ri and writes the record si, at most 80 words, with retrieval fields fi made of keywords, tags and a short description. A small sentence en-coder ϕ gives the embedding ei = ϕ(si∥fi), where 

∥ denotes concatenation, and the links Li connect 

ui in both directions to the three earlier notes with the highest cosine similarity to ei. Without an LLM call, FlexA-Mem also cuts ri at line breaks into pieces Πi = ( πi1, . . . , π iK i ) of at most κ tokens. Each piece stays inside one conversation turn or one block of an agent step. FlexA-Mem never rewrites a note, since a later call recovers detail from the exact raw text and a rewritten neighbor would add cost but no detail. 

3.2 The Timeline of Records 

Every call shows the timeline Tt, the records of past units in time order, which keeps a compact trace of what the agent has done. When it grows too long, FlexA-Mem replaces up to four consecutive records, oldest first, with a merged record that one LLM call writes. Let ∥Tt∥ be the length of the timeline, ρ the share of the allowance given to it, and Dt the total cost of showing every candidate of unit recovery as raw text (Section 3.3). Merging loses the detail of separate records in the context, but the raw text of every unit stays in the store and can still be recovered. On agentic tasks the oldest records are also hidden for one call when the newest unit alone takes more than a quarter of the budget (Appendix A). The room left for recovery is R0 = At − ∥ Tt∥.

3.5 Choosing a Recovery Mode 

A question about a past conversation usually names its evidence, and keyword matching over all pieces finds it while spending the room only on match-ing text. The next step of an agent re-uses a detail that its context cannot name, and whole units keep the context around each detail. For this reason we use unit recovery for agents and piece recovery for questions over a history. Within a mode FlexA-Mem adapts the detail it shows to the budget at every call, but it does not choose the mode at run time (Section 6.4). The only LLM calls at read time write merged records, and Algorithm 1 in Ap-pendix A gives the full procedure of unit recovery.

The 147 unused tasks confirm the AppWorld results (Table 3). Before the first episode we froze the code, six arms, two budgets and five predictions. 6 Analysis 

6.1 How the Baselines Fail on AppWorld 

In the failure trajectories we analyze, the main failure mode of the baselines on AppWorld is repeating their own setup, not giving wrong an-swers (Figure 5). At 4k on Test-Normal, 23.8% of the episodes of FlexA-Mem end at the step limit, against 55.4% to 85.7% for every baseline except Summary+A-Mem. The tasks that the baselines declare complete are right about as often as those of FlexA-Mem. Of their failures at 4k, 90% are loops of logging in and rereading documentation. Extra steps do not rescue them. With 150 steps instead of 60, observation masking, truncation and piece retrieval complete no more tasks, whereas FlexA-Mem rises from 64.3% to 80.4%. ReadA-gent and AFM, the two strongest baselines, keep a gist of every unit or a compressed summary of most units in view. Unlike ReadAgent, AFM packs units oldest first and drops 2.3 of the newest units per call at 4k.

6.4 When Each Recovery Mode Wins 

Each mode leads on the kind of scenario it was set for (Figure 4a). Unit recovery outperforms piece recovery in all five agentic settings, by 4.8% to 10.1%, and piece recovery leads on LongMemEval up to 16k and on LoCoMo at every budget. In the questions we test, the gap between the modes is associated with how well the question names its evidence (Figure 4b). Piece recovery is ahead by 17.6% at 4k when BM25 ranks the evidence first, and unit recovery is ahead by 26.5% when the evidence ranks below twentieth. This rank needs evidence labels. The runtime signals we tested do not reliably predict the better mode for a single query, and we offer no deployable rule for choosing it. The two modes also differ in more than the unit of recovery (Appendix F). 

7 Conclusion 

A single write-once store, rendered anew at every call as a compact timeline of records plus detail recovered by a fixed rule, lets FlexA-Mem serve both kinds of scenario. On agentic tasks the time-line keeps the trace and carries completion, coming within 2.0% of the full method on tasks unused in design. On fact recall the recovered text decides accuracy, and keyword retrieval over the kept raw text is already strong. Choosing the recovery mode for each query without labels is the next step. 8Limitations 

We designed the final FlexA-Mem after the main experiments, and only the 147 unused AppWorld tasks test it with predictions fixed in advance. Most configurations ran once, with up to 4.8% between two runs on AppWorld. The recovery mode is set per task type rather than per query. Every history here fits in the context of the model, where the full history costs fewer tokens, and all four benchmarks are in English. Appendix B gives further details.

C Baselines 

A-Mem (Xu et al., 2025a) follows the original prompts for note construction, link generation and memory evolution. Each note is indexed by its metadata and by its raw text in spans of 150 to-kens. At read time A-Mem’s search order is kept, and raw notes are taken while they fit and shown in time order. Summary+A-Mem adds a running summary written with the summarizing prompt of OpenHands and MemGym (Wang et al., 2025c; Xu et al., 2026b), extended with an instruction to keep names, numbers, identifiers, paths and error messages. The summary takes its share of the al-lowance and lends what it does not use to retrieval. On LongMemEval it is built once over the whole history before the question is seen. On agentic tasks it is updated as units leave the recent window. MemGAS (Xu et al., 2026a) keeps every unit as raw text, as turns or steps, as an LLM summary and as LLM keywords. It links units with a Gaussian mixture over their similarities, weights the gran-ularities per query, ranks units with personalized PageRank and passes them through one LLM filter. Sculptor (Li et al., 2026a) shows each unit as a fragment that the model can summarize, fold, re-store or search through the paper’s tools, with the tool schemas counted against the budget. ReadA-gent (Lee et al., 2024) writes one gist per unit and lets one LLM call per question, or per agent call on SWE-bench, choose the units whose raw text replaces their gists. MemGAS, Sculptor, ReadA-gent and AFM are our implementations from their papers. They run under the same harness, budgets, 16 agent or reader model and judges as the other meth-ods, and they reuse the prompts written for SWE-bench and LongMemEval on the other agentic and conversational benchmarks. Piece retrieval and unit retrieval make no LLM call. Unit retrieval ranks raw units by the best em-bedding similarity of their spans of 150 tokens and shows the units that fit in time order. Piece retrieval ranks the pieces of every unit by BM25 and shows the best pieces that fit, grouped by unit in time order with marks for omitted text. Observation masking (Lindenbauer et al., 2025) replaces tool outputs older than the last ten steps with a placeholder once the context would exceed ¯B, and masks more steps while the context still exceeds it. 

D Additional Related Work 

Several memories fix the unit or the tier of detail when they write. SeCom shows that turn, session and summary units each fall short and builds seg-ment units denoised by prompt compression (Pan et al., 2025), yet it commits to one granularity at write time. MemoryOS moves dialogue from a short-term queue into mid-term segments and a long-term store, each with its own budget (Kang et al., 2025), and recursive summarization keeps one rolling summary of a long dialogue (Wang et al., 2025b). GAM keeps a memo per session and the raw sessions as pages, and an LLM researcher plans, searches and reflects over them at read time (Yan et al., 2025), which raises its serving time to 12.43 seconds against 0.52 for A-Mem in that pa-per. EdgeMem keeps the original turns and builds its index without a generative LLM call (Cui et al., 2026), but units outside the retrieved set stay invis-ible. FlexA-Mem keeps the raw text of every unit, a trace of every unit in the timeline, and a rule that chooses the resolution without an LLM call. Other work argues for keeping raw text instead of rewriting it. Repeated consolidation by an LLM turns useful memories faulty, which leads its au-thors to treat raw episodes as evidence (Zhang et al., 2026a). Compactors keep on average 17% of in-jected constraints, and most do worse than no com-paction (Wang et al., 2026c). Under compression agents also call retrieval tools far more often, 63.9 times against 21.0 in one study (Liu, 2026). These findings motivate writing each unit once and recov-ering its exact text when a call needs it. Agents can also manage their context themselves. Memex(RL) and GCC let the agent read raw ex-perience back through an exact key or a context command (Wang et al., 2026b; Wu et al., 2025b), and Pichay restores an evicted tool output only after the agent issues the same call again (Mason, 2026). Other agents are trained to edit or condense their own context (Zhang et al., 2026b; Liu et al., 2026), and AgeMem learns one memory for short and long horizons with reinforcement learning and tool actions (Yu et al., 2026). Recovery then depends on an action of the agent, whereas FlexA-Mem recovers by a fixed rule before every call. Coding agents compact their history to fit the budget. SWE-agent replaces old observations with one-line placeholders (Yang et al., 2024), the Open-Hands condensers mask, truncate or summarize (Wang et al., 2025c, 2026a), and ACON optimizes the guidelines of an LLM compressor (Kang et al., 2026a). Observation masking matches LLM sum-marization at about half the cost on SWE-bench Verified (Lindenbauer et al., 2025), but the removed content cannot return, and repeated compaction erodes even the rules an agent was told to keep (Zer-houdi et al., 2026). Other methods reduce what the agent reads. LLMLingua and LLMLingua-2 drop tokens from a single prompt (Jiang et al., 2023, 2024; Pan et al., 2024), AttnCompress keeps a fixed share of each observation as verbatim blocks (Zeng et al., 2026), and AgentDiet removes 39.9% to 59.7% of input tokens by reducing trajectories (Xiao et al., 2026). Fewer tokens need not mean a lower bill, and one study finds that cutting tool-output tokens raised the billed cost of the agent (Weinberger and Hozez, 2026). We count every call and every memory construction in tokens per task. Unlike these methods, FlexA-Mem never removes the raw text of a unit from its store. Among other benchmarks, LoCoMo tests very long conversational memory (Maharana et al., 2024), and SWE-rebench collects fresh issues for decontaminated evaluation of coding agents (Badertdinov et al., 2025). MemGym offers a long-horizon memory environment (Xu et al., 2026b).

E.8 What the Timeline Needs 

A trace written by fixed rules keeps most of the benefit of the timeline at 4k, and records written by an LLM matter once there is room (Table 6). The trace is built from the agent’s actions without an LLM call and completes 61.3% of the Test-Normal tasks at 4k against 67.3% for records written by GPT-5.4 mini. This difference is not significant, and the trace still outperforms observation masking by 25.6%. At 6k the trace stays at 61.3% while the LLM records reach 75.0%, which gives a signifi-cant gap of 13.7%. Recovery also makes the memory tolerant of a weaker writer. With records from Claude Haiku 4.5 the timeline alone completes 15.5% fewer tasks than with records from GPT-5.4 mini. Recovery of raw text closes most of that gap. The memory written by Haiku then completes 62.5% of the tasks, 1.8% below the same memory written by GPT-5.4 mini, and within its run recovery adds 10.7%. 

E.9 What the Memory Recalls on SWE-bench 

FlexA-Mem keeps track of what a coding agent has seen nearly as well as the full history (Table 7). E.10 How the Memory Uses the Budget 

As the budget grows, the memory shifts toward raw text (Figure 6). Excerpts take 7.4% of the memory’s tokens at 8k and 2.4% at 32k, raw text grows from 68.2% to 73.5%, and records stay near a quarter. E.11 Excerpt Variants and Plug-ins 

Shorter excerpts help at 4k and 8k (Table 15), whereas giving raw text to every candidate first loses most of the gain at 4k. Fit-or-degrade raises the accuracy of A-Mem and of the variant with memory evolution by 13.4% and 13.9% at 4k and by 5.7% and 3.7% at 8k.

F.2 Analysis F.2.1 How the Baselines Fail on AppWorld 

In the failure trajectories we analyze, the main failure mode of the baselines on AppWorld is repeating their own setup, not giving wrong an-swers (Figure 5). At 4k on Test-Normal, 23.8% of the episodes of FlexA-Mem end at the step limit, against 55.4% to 85.7% for every baseline except Summary+A-Mem. When the five baselines of the main run declare a task complete, they are right about as often as FlexA-Mem, 77.8% to 85.0% against 84.4%. Of their failures at 4k, 90% are loops in which the agent logs in, lists the APIs and reads their documentation again. Extra steps do not rescue them. With 150 steps instead of 60, observation masking, truncation and piece re-trieval complete exactly as many tasks as before, whereas FlexA-Mem rises from 64.3% to 80.4%. On Test-Challenge at 4k, 47% of the episodes of FlexA-Mem reach the limit, against 57% for piece recovery, 61% for Summary+A-Mem and 84% to 95% for the other baselines. ReadAgent and AFM, the two strongest base-lines on Test-Normal, are also the two that keep something of every unit in view. ReadAgent keeps a gist of every unit and spends 91.0k memory to-kens per episode at 4k against 36.8k for FlexA-Mem. AFM keeps most units as short compressed summaries, but it packs units oldest first and drops 2.3 of the newest units per call at 4k. The ablations below test whether the trace of the whole history is what decides completion. 

F.2.2 The Timeline Keeps the Trace 

Removing the timeline costs the most on AppWorld (Table 5). Completion falls by 15.5% at 4k and 10.1% at 6k, and on SWE-bench Verified the agent needs 56.9 steps per issue instead of 43.8 while its resolve rate moves by only 1.1%. On the un-used tasks the timeline alone completes 76.2% and 79.6%, within 2.0% of the full method (Table 3). Even a trace written by fixed rules from the agent’s actions, with no LLM call, completes 61.3% of 23 Test-Normal at 4k against 67.3% for records writ-ten by GPT-5.4 mini ( p = 0 .15 ). At 6k it falls 13.7% behind (Appendix E.8). The trace matters where an agent must know what it has already done, and it does little for ques-tions that name their answer. Without the timeline, LongMemEval accuracy changes by at most 3.6%, and its one significant effect is a gain of 3.6% at 8k. On LoCoMo the timeline alone answers 40.3% to 67.8% of the questions from 2k to 8k, below piece recovery at every budget (Table 1). 

F.2.3 Recovery Brings Back the Detail 

Recovery decides accuracy when the answer is an exact detail. On LongMemEval, removing recov-ery lowers accuracy by 28.2% at 4k and removing excerpts lowers it by 15.7%. Four render controls, each changing one part of unit recovery on the same notes and budget, show that the choice and order of the recovered units decide accuracy there. Making every unit a candidate in a random order loses 47.5% at 4k and shuffling the relevant can-didates loses 46.8%, whereas showing the same selection after the timeline changes accuracy by at most 1.4%. Fit-or-degrade is not the best rule for this task, since giving every candidate an excerpt instead gains 6.1% and 7.0% at 4k and 8k. Excerpts also raise from 0.83 to 0.93 the balanced accuracy with which the memory tells whether a SWE-bench agent displayed a given file (Appendix E.9). On AppWorld recovery changes effort more than completion, and no render control changes the com-pletion rate of Test-Normal significantly. Unit re-covery takes 26.9 steps per episode on the unused tasks against 32.2 for the timeline alone at 4k, and 20.7 against 28.7 at 6k. Fixed-length recovery takes 3.6 and 5.0 more steps than fit-or-degrade. Excerpts add 5.0% on Test-Challenge at 4k ( p = 0 .031 ). Records from Claude Haiku 4.5 leave the timeline alone 15.5% behind, and recovery of raw text then adds 10.7% on Test-Normal (Appendix E.8). Without a timeline the recovery rule decides the completion rate. Rendering the notes that A-Mem writes with fit-or-degrade raises Test-Normal completion by 10.7% at 4k and 7.7% at 6k, and LongMemEval accuracy by 13.4% at 4k. A-Mem shows 0.87 whole notes per call at 4k and leaves much of its allowance empty, because a retrieved step is often longer than the room, whereas fit-or-degrade fills that room with about two excerpts. A-Mem with fit-or-degrade still completes 21% to 33% fewer tasks than FlexA-Mem. 

F.2.4 When Each Recovery Mode Wins 

Each recovery mode leads on the kind of scenario it was set for (Figure 4a). Unit recovery outper-forms piece recovery in all five agentic settings, by 4.8% to 10.1%, significantly at both budgets of Test-Challenge. Piece recovery leads on Long-MemEval by 8.4%, 6.6% and 3.6% from 4k to 16k, and the two tie at 32k. LoCoMo shows leads of 17.4%, 8.1% and 2.4% from 2k to 8k. The two modes also differ in where their text appears and in how much room the timeline can borrow (Limita-tions), and these gaps compare two configurations rather than isolate the unit of recovery. In the questions we test, the gap between the modes is associated with how well the question names its evidence (Figure 4b). We measure this by the rank at which BM25 with the bare question places the best evidence piece among the roughly 1,040 pieces of a history. For the 204 of the 413 held-out questions with an answer in the history whose evidence ranks first, piece recovery is ahead of unit recovery by 17.6% at 4k. Unit recovery is ahead by 26.5% for the 34 questions whose evi-dence ranks below twentieth. This rank needs the evidence labels, and the run-time signals we tested do not reliably predict the better mode for a single query. On AppWorld the detail an agent uses next lands among the top five keyword matches with its task and latest steps for 22.4% of its needs, against 22.2% under a ran-dom order. None of nine task properties correlates with the gap. The BM25 score of the best Long-MemEval piece has a Spearman correlation of at most 0.05 in magnitude with the gap at 4k and 8k.
